Can everything have a price?

Letting money decide can go wrong in two ways: the choice may not be free, and the thing sold may lose its meaning.

Justice is our own course on the big ideas of moral and political philosophy. This part builds on Part 4, where libertarians said a deal between two willing adults is just. Here we ask what that idea leaves out.

The case for letting money decide

Two claims make a market look just: people choose freely, and both sides gain.

In one block of flats, the tenants take turns cleaning the shared stairs. Dara hates her week. Pim, next door, does not mind the job, and he could use ten dollars. So Dara pays Pim to take her week. Who could object?

The first reason to leave the deal alone is freedom. Part 4 asked whether we own ourselves. Libertarians said yes, so adults may trade what is theirs, and a rule that blocks a willing deal takes away their liberty.

The second reason is welfare, the idea from Part 2: add up everyone's happiness, as Bentham did. Dara is happier. Pim is happier. No one else is hurt. So the total goes up.

Now stretch the deal. Could Dara pay someone to say sorry to a friend for her, so well that the friend never learns it was hired? Could she pay someone to sit by her father’s bed, and he is glad of the company? The same two claims say yes every time. Each deal is free, helps both sides and hurts no one else.

Yet most people feel the stairs are fine and the paid apology is not. Freedom and welfare cannot explain that gap, because they approve every willing deal that hurts no one else. People who doubt markets say two things. Some choices are less free than they look. And some things are spoiled when they are bought and sold. Each doubt gets its own lesson.

When need makes the choice

A choice made from need, or made without knowing, is not fully free.

A warehouse offers double pay for a night shift in its freezer. Two people sign up, Anna and Ben. Anna has three other job offers and some savings. Ben has no other offer, and his rent is due tomorrow. Both signed, and both said yes.

Anna's yes is a real preference. She would rather have the money than her nights. Ben's yes is closer to a no he cannot afford to say. To tell them apart, you must ask what each could have done instead. That is the first doubt about markets: a choice is only as free as the options behind it.

The same doubt appears in a real war. In 1862 the Union side in the American Civil War passed its first draft law, a law that orders citizens to serve. A man who was called up could pay someone to take his place. Men put ads in newspapers to find a stand-in. Names we still know, such as Andrew Carnegie and J. P. Morgan, hired one. People in the South complained of a “rich man’s war and poor man’s fight,” and the same complaint was heard in the North.

Most men who were called up never served themselves. Some hired a stand-in. Later the Union also let a man pay the government a fee to stay home. No one forced a stand-in to go. Was his yes free? Only if he had other good choices. Where many people have few other options, pay can push them in.

A choice can also fall short in another way: not knowing. Think of paid pregnancy, where a woman agrees to carry a baby for someone else and hand it over for money. She cannot know, before the birth, how strongly she will feel about the baby. Critics say a yes given before you can know is not fully informed.

Notice what this doubt does not say. It does not say that paid work or a paid army is wrong. It says the market must be fair underneath. If everyone had decent choices and no heavy debts, the yes of a paid soldier would count as free. How equal must a society be for that to hold? Part 8 takes this up, with Rawls.

Some things change when sold

Some things lose their meaning when they are sold, so a market can be wrong even when every choice is free.

Mia is getting married and fears the hall will look empty. She pays twenty strangers to come, smile and cheer. All twenty chose freely and were glad of the money. Nothing is unfair. Yet something went wrong. A crowd that is paid is not the same thing as a crowd that came.

Elizabeth Anderson, a modern moral philosopher, argued that we value goods in different ways, not just more or less. A ladder is for use. A person is for respect and care. Treat a person like a tool, and you degrade her.

So we cannot value a good any way we like: some ways fit it, and some do not. Paying is the right way to value a ladder or a haircut. For some goods it is the wrong way, and the price itself is the harm, even when buyer and seller both agree.

In 1985 Mary Beth Whitehead signed a contract with William Stern. With his sperm, she would conceive and carry a child, and hand it over at birth. She would give up her rights as mother, so that his wife could adopt. William would pay her. After the girl was born in 1986, Mary Beth could not give her up. The case became known as Baby M, and it went to court in New Jersey.

The trial judge enforced the contract. He found that neither side had more power over the other, and that Mary Beth was paid for a service, not for a child. The state’s highest court overturned him, and every judge agreed. It gave two reasons, one for each doubt: her yes was not fully free, because she could not know her bond before the birth and money pushed her; and a child is not for sale, even when the yes is free. It placed the girl with William, as the better home, but restored Mary Beth as her mother.

Anderson applied her idea to paid pregnancy. She argued that it treats the child as a product and the mother’s body as a factory, and pays her to hold back the love that pregnancy normally brings.

The same doubt reaches paid soldiers. Some say serving is a duty every citizen shares, not a job. Rousseau, an eighteenth century thinker, warned that a people who pay others to fight, so they can stay home, are close to collapse. A shared duty that becomes a job stops feeling like ours. Defenders answer that many paid soldiers serve out of love of their country.
