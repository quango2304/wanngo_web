A belief that shapes your life

Your abilities can be carved in stone, or they can be grown. Which one you believe shapes how you handle challenge, failure and effort, and you can change it.

Carol S. Dweck is a psychologist. Over twenty years, her research found two mindsets about ability and followed how each one leads to different choices. This summary shows how they work, and how to move from one to the other.

Two ways to see yourself

Some people think their abilities are carved in stone, and others think they can be grown. That one belief changes how you act.

Carol Dweck once gave hard puzzles to children and watched them struggle. One ten-year-old boy rubbed his hands together and cried, “I love a challenge!” She had always thought you either coped with failure or you did not. She had never thought anyone could love it.

These children knew something she did not. They believed that skills grow with effort. So they did not see themselves as failing. To them it was learning. She had believed the opposite: you are smart or you are not, and failure means you are not. The same hard puzzle meant two different things.

She calls the first belief the fixed mindset and the second the growth mindset. Growth does not mean anyone can become anything. It means nobody can know how far years of effort and training will take a person.

The beliefs show up fast. She asked people to picture a bad day: a C+ on a midterm exam, a parking ticket, and a friend who brushes you off. People with a fixed mindset said, “I'm a total failure.” People with a growth mindset said they would study harder, pay the ticket and find out what was wrong with the friend. The fixed-mindset people were not simply gloomy. When nothing has gone wrong, they feel as worthy and bright as anyone.

Learning or proving

People with a growth mindset pick what will stretch them. People with a fixed mindset pick what will keep them looking smart.

Babies never worry about mistakes. Then children start to judge themselves, and some grow afraid of not being smart. Dweck's team offered four-year-olds a choice: redo an easy jigsaw puzzle or try a harder one. Children with a fixed mindset kept to the safe puzzle. One explained that kids born smart do not get things wrong. Children with a growth mindset found the choice strange and picked one hard puzzle after another.

In a brain-wave lab, people answered hard questions and got feedback. People with a fixed mindset paid close attention at one moment only. When?

They cared about the verdict, not the lesson. The same thing happens in real life. At the University of Hong Kong, classes, books and exams are all in English, and some new students were weak at it. Asked if they would take a course to improve, students with a growth mindset said an emphatic yes. Those with a fixed mindset were not very interested. They risked their college careers to feel smart for now.

Fifth graders did the same. They all loved intriguing puzzles, until the puzzles got harder. Then the fixed-mindset children, even the best puzzle solvers, lost much of their joy and changed their minds about taking some home.

Even top students fall into this. New graduate students in her department had near perfect grades, yet some felt like frauds within a day. They could not do what the faculty did yet.

A setback or a verdict

In a fixed mindset, a failure becomes who you are. In a growth mindset, it is a problem to face and learn from.

Seventh graders imagined a poor grade on a test in a new course. What did those with a fixed mindset say they would do?

Jim Marshall played defense for the Minnesota Vikings. Against the San Francisco 49ers, he grabbed a loose football and raced for the end zone as fans cheered. But it was the wrong one: he scored for the other team, on national television. He was crushed. At halftime he decided that a mistake has to be made right. In the second half he played some of the best football of his career, and his team won. He did not let the mistake define him.

A fixed mindset leaves no such path. If failure means you lack ability, where do you go from there? People often try to repair their self-esteem instead of the failure. John McEnroe, the tennis star, blamed a fever, a backache, the heat and even a noisy cameraman for his losses. So he never trained his focus or his temper. Coach John Wooden said you are not a failure until you start to blame.
