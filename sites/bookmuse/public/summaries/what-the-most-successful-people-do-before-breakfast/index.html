What the Most Successful People Do Before Breakfast

A summary of the book by Laura Vanderkam

6 lessons · 17 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Use the quiet hours on purpose

The most successful people spend their early mornings, their weekends and their work hours on purpose, so what matters most gets done first.

Laura Vanderkam studies how people use their time. This book puts three short guides together, on mornings, weekends and work. She learned from time logs and from talking with busy people, such as executives and a children's book illustrator. Her answer is simple: choose how you spend your hours, and start with the ones before breakfast.

Mornings are the time you control

Early morning is the one part of the day that is still yours, so do what matters most first.

Steve Reinemund was the chairman and CEO of PepsiCo. Vanderkam heard about morning routines like his from James Citrin, an executive recruiter. Citrin asked about twenty leaders how they start their day. Eighteen answered.

Reinemund was up at 5:00 a.m. and ran four miles on a treadmill. Then he had quiet time to pray, read and catch up on the news. After that he ate breakfast with his teenage twins. He ran those four miles on most days for decades. He told Vanderkam, “I cherish my mornings, my personal time.” His early hours were his own, before anyone else asked for them.

Why early? Later in the day, other people's priorities take over, even those of people you love. In the early morning, you have the most control over your schedule. Many of us lose that time. We spend two or more hours rushing to get ready and get out the door. Then we spend the first quiet minutes at work on personal email and headlines.

Vanderkam compares this to a rule about money: pay yourself first, before the bills. If you save only what is left at the end of the month, nothing is left. A day works the same way. If you wait until evening to exercise, read, pray or plan your career, it probably will not happen. Her short rule is “If it has to happen, then it has to happen first.”

Willpower runs down by evening

Willpower is one supply that runs down all day, so do hard things early and turn them into habits.

Roy Baumeister, a psychologist at Florida State University, has studied self-discipline for his whole career. In one famous test, hungry students came to the lab and sat alone in a room with radishes, chocolate chip cookies and candy. Some could eat the sweets. Others were told to eat only radishes. Then every student worked on a geometry puzzle that could not be solved.

Tap an answer

How long did the students who ate only radishes keep trying, compared with the others?

Baumeister's idea is that willpower, like a muscle, gets tired from use. You have one supply for everything: resisting food, holding back anger, making choices, thinking hard and doing well at work. A day of traffic, difficult bosses and arguing children can use it all up. That is why big self-control failures tend to come late in the day. Diets break in the evening, not the morning.

After a good night's sleep the supply is full. So the morning is the time for things nobody forces you to do.

There is good news too. Self-discipline grows stronger with use, and people who have a lot of it often do not seem to use it on daily tasks, because those tasks became habits. A habit takes willpower at first and saves it later. Think of brushing your teeth. You do not argue with yourself about it each morning. Successful people turn important tasks into morning habits like that. Then they keep their energy for harder moments later in the day.

Priya has one hour for a hard report. She could write it first thing, or after a morning of small choices and tense calls. Why is first thing better?

Try this. Pick one hard task and do it at the same time each morning. It takes willpower at first, then it becomes a habit.

Fill mornings with what matters most

The best morning habits are things that matter but are not urgent: your career, your relationships and yourself.

You can make a habit of anything. But laundry gets done because it has to. Conference calls rise to the top because other people are waiting. Most people need no willpower to watch TV. The best morning habits are different. They do not have to happen, and they do not have to happen at a set hour. They need your own drive, and they pay off over the long run.

Debbie Moysychyn ran a new division at a university. Her open door meant constant interruptions, so her own projects never moved. Her teenage daughter had water polo before 7:00 a.m., which left Debbie free at 6:30 with nobody dropping by. She used that time for one top project each day. A few weeks later she was still doing her hardest work early and checking long-standing items off her to-do list. So quiet time gave her what an open door could not: hours without interruptions.

The right habit is personal. Gretchen Rubin found that heavy writing did not work for her at 6:00, so she uses that hour for email and scheduling. For exercise, studies found that people who work out in the morning are more likely to stick with it. Vanderkam gives the same reasons as before: willpower is fresh, and emergencies have not started yet.
