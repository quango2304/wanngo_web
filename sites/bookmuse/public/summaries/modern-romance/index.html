Finding love in a phone world

Today we search for a soul mate through screens and endless options, so finding love takes more care: treat people as people, not as bubbles on a screen, and give them real time.

The comedian Aziz Ansari wrote this book with the sociologist Eric Klinenberg. For more than a year they ran group interviews in cities around the world. Many people let them read the messages on their phones. The book came out in 2015 and mostly covers straight, middle-class daters.

From good enough to soul mate

A few generations ago people married someone nearby who was good enough. Now we want a soul mate, so we search longer and expect more.

Tap an answer

In the early 1960s, what share of American women said they would marry someone they did not love?

The authors began in a New York retirement home. Of thirty-six seniors they met, fourteen had married a person from their own street or neighborhood, a short walk from home. One woman married a man from the floor above. A 1932 study of five thousand marriage licenses in Philadelphia found the same pattern. One third of couples had lived within five blocks of each other.

Marriage then was a family job. The sociologist Andrew Cherlin calls it a companionate marriage: each partner had a set role, and you were a good spouse by filling it. People married young. The seniors married at about twenty if they were women and twenty-three if they were men.

Sociologists call that stage emerging adulthood. Then came the sixties and seventies. That generation wanted more than a spouse: a soul mate. By the 1980s, most Americans said romantic love was a must. Of men, 86 percent said they would not marry without it, and of women, 91 percent.

Cherlin says a soul mate marriage has the highest chance of happiness and also of disappointment. The therapist Esther Perel adds that we now ask one person for what a whole village once gave. So finding someone is more stressful than before. But the authors say you are also more likely to land a partner who truly excites you.

Your phone self

A text is now your first impression, so make it specific and make it personal.

In 2010, 10 percent of young adults asked someone out by text. By 2013 it was 32 percent. The authors read hundreds of messages on people's phones. Most first texts women got were things like "Hey" and "Wsup." Those texts feel lazy. They make her feel she is not special.

A text also forgives nothing. Face to face, you see a reaction and can fix a slip. In a text, the mistake just sits there. So you have two selves: your real self and your phone self. The other person only meets the phone one. On a screen a person can shrink to a bubble, and it is easy to forget the bubble is a person. Then people send things they would never say face to face.

At a stand-up show in Tulsa, a young man named Cody had sent twenty nervous messages to a woman who clearly liked him. He never asked her out. Then he sent one text naming a restaurant and asking her to go that week. She said yes within two hours, and they ate there the next week. One clear ask did what twenty chats had not.

The authors found that good texts share three things. They invite you firmly to one specific thing at one specific time. They refer back to the last time you met. And, if you share a sense of humor, they are a little funny.

Maya met Leo at a party, where they laughed about his karaoke. She wants to see him again. Which text should she send?

Try this. Before you send a first text, check that it asks for one specific thing at one specific time, and that it mentions something from when you met.

The waiting game

Waiting for a reply hooks the mind, so people play games with texts. Yet nearly everyone says they want a straight answer.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Women saw Facebook profiles of men who had looked at their own profile. Some were told a man rated theirs best, some average, and some that it was uncertain. Which men did they like most?

People follow unwritten texting rules. Don't reply right away. Don't text twice before you hear back. It works because uncertainty keeps a person on your mind, as the study shows. In lab tests, animals that cannot predict a reward want it more. We also want what seems less available.

But waiting hurts, because texting taught us to expect a quick answer. The anthropologist Natasha Schüll studies slot machine addiction. She says texting someone you like is like a slot machine: you expect a fast result, and a delay feels like a crisis. She once waited three days for a man's reply and could think of little else. He had lost his phone, and her number was in it.

Ansari's own text to a woman named Tanya got no reply. He replayed every word. Months later she said sorry. Her silence was about something in her own life, not his text.

If you are not interested, people use three answers. Some pretend to be busy. Some say nothing. A few are honest. At his shows, Ansari asked more than 150,000 people which they did. Most chose busy or silence. Then he asked how they wanted to be treated. Nearly everyone clapped for honesty.

Honesty is hard, and a no feels cold. Still, the authors suggest a short, kind message instead of silence.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Dan went out twice with Mia. He does not want a third date, and she texts to ask about Saturday. What should he do?

Try this. Next time you want to ignore someone who asked you out, write them one short, kind message that says no.
