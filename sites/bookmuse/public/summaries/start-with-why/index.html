Start with Why

A summary of the book by Simon Sinek

6 lessons · 17 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Why some leaders inspire

"People don't buy WHAT you do, they buy WHY you do it," says Sinek. So great leaders start with their purpose and work outward.

Simon Sinek studied cultural anthropology, the study of how people live in groups, and worked in marketing. He noticed that leaders and companies who inspire all think in the same pattern. He named it the Golden Circle and wrote this book about it in 2009. You will learn the pattern, why it works and how to find your own why.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Manipulate or inspire

You can move people with tricks or with a cause, and only a cause builds loyalty.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

General Motors had offered $500 to $7,000 cash back on its cars and trucks. Then it cut those deals. What happened to its sales?

Sinek says there are only two ways to change what people do. You can manipulate them, or you can inspire them. Manipulation is common, and it works. The book names six kinds:

  1. Price. Cut it low enough and people buy, but your profit shrinks.
  2. Promotions. A free gift or cash back tips a close choice.
  3. Fear. Buy now, or something bad may happen to you.
  4. Aspirations. A picture of the person you wish you were.
  5. Social pressure. Four out of five dentists prefer it.
  6. Novelty. A new feature, like Colgate's 41 kinds of toothpaste.

These tricks get you a sale, not loyalty. Repeat business means people buy from you many times. Loyalty means people pass up a stronger product or a lower price to stay with you. Repeat business is easy to buy. Loyalty is not. GM shows the cost. Cash back lifted its sales for a while. When it cut the deals, sales fell sharply: no cash, no customers.

The book's opening story shows the other way. Samuel Langley had a $50,000 grant, a gift of government money, famous friends and a team of experts. Wilbur and Orville Wright had a bicycle shop in Dayton, Ohio, no grant, and no college degrees on their team. Sinek reads Langley as chasing fame and money, and the Wrights as driven by a belief that flight would change the world. On December 17, 1903, the Wrights flew for twelve seconds. Langley's own test flight had ended in the Potomac River, and he soon quit. So the team with the stronger reason won, not the one with more money.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Mia's bakery has a new rival with lower prices. Her weekly customers keep coming to her. What does that show?

In your life

One store sends you a coupon every week, so you shop there. Another is run by someone you admire, so you pay a little more and wait in line. The first has your repeat business. The second has your loyalty.

Try this. Next time you choose between two phones with the same price and features, notice what tips your choice. The book asks you to pay attention to how you decide.

The Golden Circle

Every group works in three layers: why, how and what. Leaders who inspire start in the middle of the circle and work outward.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Creative's music player reached stores 22 months before Apple's iPod. Why did the iPod win?

  1. WHAT. The products or services you offer. Every company can say it.
  2. HOW. The way you do it, your values in action. Some can say it.
  3. WHY. Your purpose, cause or belief. Making money is a result, not a why. Very few can say it.

Most companies talk from the outside in. They say what they make, say how it is better, then ask for the sale. The book shows the difference with Apple.

In the second pitch, the products stop being the reason to buy. They become proof of what the company believes. Creative named its product. Apple said why people wanted it. The book says this is why Apple could sell computers, music players and phones. Dell tried pocket organizers (PDAs) and MP3 players, and both failed within a few years. Buyers saw Dell as a computer company and nothing more.

To keep the circle working, the book asks for three things:

  1. Clarity of WHY. Know why you do what you do.
  2. Discipline of HOW. State your values as actions: "do the right thing", not "integrity".
  3. Consistency of WHAT. Everything you say and do proves what you believe.

The book adds the Celery Test. Four guests at a party each give you advice: M&M's, oat milk, Oreos or celery. You could buy all four. But if your why is to do only healthy things, you buy only oat milk and celery. Your why filters the advice. It also lets others see what you believe from what you buy.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

A bike shop wants a new opening line for its website. Which one starts with WHY?

Try this. Take your last big decision and apply the Celery Test. Did what you did show what you believe?

Why the gut decides

People decide on a feeling first and use reasons afterward, so saying why speaks to the feeling.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Anthropologists watched people take clean laundry out of the dryer. What did people do first?

Soap makers asked buyers what they wanted and heard "whiter whites." But people smelled the laundry first, so smell mattered most. So what buyers said they wanted was not what moved them. Sinek says such choices come from biology, not opinion. He lays the Golden Circle over the brain. The newest part, the neocortex, handles facts, logic and language. It matches WHAT. The middle part, the limbic brain, handles feelings such as trust and loyalty. It also makes our decisions. But it has no words, so it cannot say what it feels. It matches WHY.

That is why it is hard to say why we love someone. "They are funny and smart" is true, but many people are. The real reason is a feeling, so we talk around it. The same holds, he says, when a buyer is asked why they chose a brand. The answer is a reason found afterward. The book gives one neuroscientist, Richard Restak, who says gut decisions are often faster and better. It gives little other proof for the brain map.

Sinek tells a story about a man named Brad on a first date. Brad says he is rich, has a big house and knows famous people. He is unlikely to get a second date. Next time Brad starts with why. He loves his work, and the house and the money follow from it. The facts are the same, but the order is not. So facts work better as proof of a belief than as a boast.

Tap an answer

Dana visits five flats and picks one. She says, "It just felt right," but cannot say why. What explains this?

In your life

You scroll for an hour to pick a TV. Every one looks the same on paper. You buy one and still wonder if it was the right one.
