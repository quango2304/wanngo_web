Capital in the 21st Century

A summary of the book by Thomas Piketty

8 lessons · 21 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Why old money keeps winning

When wealth earns more each year than the economy grows, money from the past pulls ahead of pay from the present, and inequality rises unless people choose to act.

Thomas Piketty, a French economist, and his colleagues gathered income and wealth records from more than twenty countries going back three centuries. They found that the 20th century became more equal mainly because of two world wars and what followed, not because of a law of nature. The old pattern is now returning. You will see why, and what he thinks we can do about it.

Wealth, counted in years of income

Count a country's wealth in years of its income. Then the size of wealth tells you how much of the income goes to its owners.

Capital means everything a person or a country can own and sell: homes, land, factories, stocks, savings and patents, minus debts. The book uses capital and wealth to mean the same thing. It leaves out a person's skills, because skills cannot be sold to someone else.

A country's income in one year splits in two. One part is pay for work. The other part comes from owning things: rent, profit, interest and dividends, which are a company's payments to its owners.

In rich countries around 2010, the average person earned about 30,000 euros a year. About 21,000 came from work and 9,000 from wealth. That same person owned about 180,000 euros of wealth, half in a home and half in stocks, bonds and savings. Divide wealth by one year of income and you get 6. This is the capital/income ratio.

Now the book's first law. Wealth earned 9,000 on 180,000, a return of 5 percent a year. So capital's share of income is the return times the ratio: 5 percent times 6 is 30 percent. It is simple arithmetic, and it holds in every society.

Two cautions. These are averages. Many people own less than one year of income, and some own ten or twenty times that. And a 5 percent return is only an average: homes and bonds often pay 3 to 4 percent, and stocks 7 to 8 percent over the long run.

Slow growth makes wealth heavy

When an economy grows slowly, the wealth already owned grows large next to what the country earns in a year.

For most of history, almost nothing grew. From the year 0 to 1700, both the population and the output per person grew by less than 0.1 percent a year. Since 1700, the world's output has grown about 1.6 percent a year: half from more people, half from more output per person.

That sounds small, but it is not. At 1 percent a year, an economy grows by about a third in 30 years, which is one generation. It triples in 100 years, and it grows twenty times in 300.

Fast growth is rare. No country at the world's technology frontier, the leaders with no one ahead to copy, has grown faster than 1.5 percent per person for long. Europe grew more than 4 percent a year per person from 1950 to 1970, but that was catching up after two wars. Piketty does not expect the leaders to go above 1 to 1.5 percent in the long run.

Slow growth matters because of a second rule. In the long run, a country's wealth, in years of income, equals its saving rate divided by its growth rate. Say a country saves 12 percent of its income each year. If it grows 2 percent a year, its wealth reaches six years of income, and it takes several decades to get there.

The reason is plain. In a slow economy, wealth from the past counts for more, because a small flow of new saving is enough to keep building the pile.

When money grows faster than the economy

If wealth earns more each year than the economy grows, old fortunes pull ahead of pay, even when their owners do no work.

The book's main finding is written r > g. The letter r is the yearly return on wealth, such as rent, profit and interest, as a percent of what the wealth is worth. The letter g is how fast the whole economy grows.

Through most of history, r was about 4 to 5 percent a year, while g was close to zero. So r was many times g, at least 10 to 20 times. That gap let a class of owners live on their wealth.

Take France in the 19th century. An owner who saved one fifth of the income from a fortune kept it growing as fast as the economy. An owner who saved more got ahead with no work at all.

The 20th century broke the rule. War losses and taxes pushed the return, after tax and losses, down to 1 to 1.5 percent from 1913 to 1950, below growth, for the first time in history. Growth at the frontier will probably stay at 1 to 1.5 percent, while wealth earns 4 to 5 percent before tax. So r > g is likely to come back.

Big fortunes may earn even more. US universities publish the results of their endowments, the funds they invest, which makes them a clear test. From 1980 to 2010, the 500 with less than 100 million dollars earned 6.2 percent a year after fees and inflation. Harvard, Yale and Princeton earned 10.2. Harvard spent nearly 100 million dollars a year to manage about 30 billion, just over 0.3 percent. The book notes that this is a good deal if experts lift the return from 5 percent to 10. The big funds were not much riskier year to year, only better managed.
