Steve Jobs

A summary of the book by Walter Isaacson

8 lessons · 23 min · quiz and takeaways

A perfectionist who joined art and machines

Steve Jobs made products people loved by joining art with technology, controlling the whole experience and keeping only what mattered, and the same intensity that drove the work often hurt the people around him.

Walter Isaacson spent two years on this biography. He had more than forty talks with Jobs and interviewed more than a hundred people who knew him. Jobs asked for no control over the book. He was adopted as a baby, grew up in Silicon Valley and left college early, and he started Apple in his parents' garage. It shows his gifts and his faults. You will take away eight ideas about how he made things and led people, and what each one cost.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Care about the parts nobody sees

Make the hidden parts as good as the parts people see, because you know they are there.

Jobs learned this from his father, Paul, a mechanic who could build almost anything. When Paul built the family fence, he gave young Steve a hammer to help. He taught his son to do the backs of cabinets and fences well, even though no one sees them. Fifty years later the fence was still standing, and Jobs showed it to the author.

Jobs used the same rule at Apple. While the first Macintosh was being built, he studied the circuit board inside it. No buyer would ever open the case. Still, he found the memory chips ugly, because the lines were too close together. An engineer asked why it mattered. Jobs answered that a great carpenter does not use bad wood on the back of a cabinet, even if it faces the wall.

He gave the box the same care, though a buyer threw it away after opening it. A team member remembered that Jobs had it redone about fifty times. When the design was final, he had the team sign their names on a sheet. The signatures were engraved inside every Mac. Forty-five people signed first, and Jobs signed last, in the center. No customer would see them, but the team knew they were there.

In your life

A friend sends you a tidy slideshow, but the notes behind it are a mess. When you open the file to change one number, nothing lines up. Work that is neat only on the surface breaks the first time someone looks closer. Code behind an app works the same way.

Try this. Pick something you make that other people use. Look at the part nobody checks. Jobs judged the inside of the Mac by the same standard as its case.

Stand where art meets technology

The best products come from people who care about both beauty and machines.

Tap an answer

Jobs left Reed College, a small school in Portland, Oregon, but he kept dropping in on classes that looked interesting. Which one later shaped the first Mac?

Calligraphy had no use for a career. Jobs took it because he found it beautiful. He learned about letter styles with and without small feet on the letters, and about how much space to leave between letters. When he designed the first Macintosh, it all came back to him. The Mac had many typefaces and spaced its letters to fit their shapes. Jobs said that without this class, the Mac would never have had these typefaces, and no other computer would either, since Windows copied the Mac.

He wanted the Mac's fonts to feel big, too. The team had named them after small towns on a train line near Philadelphia. Jobs renamed them after cities such as Chicago, New York and Geneva, because he wanted them to be world class. Mike Markkula, an early Apple investor, asked whether they had more important things to do. But the fonts, with laser printing, helped start desktop publishing, which means making printed pages on a computer. They taught ordinary people, such as parents who made a school newsletter, the joy of choosing a typeface. So a class with no job use became one of the Mac's main strengths.

He read Edwin Land, who founded the camera company Polaroid, praising people who work where the humanities and the sciences meet. The humanities are subjects such as art, music and literature. Jobs decided that was his place. Near the end of his life he said some of the best people on the Mac team were poets and musicians on the side. He also liked to point out that Michelangelo knew how to cut stone out of the ground, not only how to carve it.

Simplicity takes hard work

Real simplicity comes from digging until only the essential is left, and from moving the hard work to where it belongs.

Apple's first brochure carried a motto: "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication." Jobs met simple design early, at Atari, where games came with no manual. The only directions for one game were two steps: insert a quarter, and avoid the Klingons.

In 2001, his team built the iPod. Other music players held about sixteen songs, and nobody could work out how to use them. Jobs's main demand was one word: Simplify. He tested every screen with a rule. Any song or function had to be at most three clicks away, and each click had to make sense.

Rival players made you build playlists on the device, which made them hard to use. The iPod could not make them. You made playlists in iTunes, Apple's music program for the computer, and copied them to the player. That copying is called syncing. Jobs also ordered that the iPod would have no on-off switch. It went to sleep when unused and woke when touched. The promise was easy to say: a thousand songs in your pocket.

Jony Ive, Apple's head of design, explained that simple is more than a look. Removing screws can make a product more tangled inside. To be truly simple, he said, you must go deep, understand what the product really is, and remove what is not essential.

In your life

A TV remote has fifty buttons, and you press five. Every button was a feature someone was proud of. You pay for each one by hunting for the five you use.

Try this. Pick something you use every day, such as a phone setting or a form. Count the clicks it takes to reach what you want. Jobs's rule for the iPod was three.
