The Laws of Human Nature

A summary of the book by Robert Greene

Why people act as they do

Deep forces such as emotion, self-love, envy and fear drive us without our knowing, and once you see them in others and in yourself, you stop being caught off guard.

Robert Greene published this book in 2018, with eighteen laws. He draws on psychology, brain science, philosophy and novels. Each chapter is one “law” of human nature, told through the story of a famous person. Here are eight that matter most.

Your feelings steer your thinking

Your feelings quietly steer your thinking, and a rational mind is something you build with practice.

In 432 BC the city of Sparta told Athens to accept its terms or face war. Pericles, the aging Athenian leader, had trained himself never to decide in the grip of a strong feeling. He advised a calm plan: stay behind the city walls, never fight Sparta on land, and use the navy at sea. Athens agreed. Then a plague killed over a third of its people, and Pericles died too.

Hotter voices took over. In 415 BC Athens sent a huge fleet to Sicily, hoping for riches, and it was destroyed. In 405 BC Athens lost the war. The one man who had kept its emotions in check was gone. Once feelings ran the show, the city made choices it could not undo.

We are no different. Feelings pull our thinking toward what soothes the ego, and the book names biases that show it. Here are four:

  1. Confirmation bias. You look for proof of what you already want to believe.
  2. Appearance bias. You take a person's polished front for the real person.
  3. Blame bias. After a mistake you blame others or bad luck, so you repeat it.
  4. Superiority bias. You think you are more rational and ethical than most people.

Sudden gains or losses and rising pressure make it worse, because they stir feelings up. Greene's cure is a pause: write your answer, leave it unsent, and wait.

Two more habits help. Ask where an anger really comes from: a small trigger often hides a deeper feeling, such as envy. And see other people as part of the world, like plants, instead of wishing they were different. Then they stir you less.

Self-love can become empathy

Everyone is a little self-absorbed, and your best social skill is to turn that attention outward and feel what others feel.

The worst narcissists are missing something, the book says. What is it?

The starting fact is plain: everyone needs attention. As children we build a picture of ourselves that we like. It works like a thermostat. When people ignore us, it keeps our confidence from dropping too far.

Some people never build that picture, often because a parent ignored them or smothered them. These deep narcissists have no inner self to fall back on. They need constant attention, treat people as tools, and rage when they are insulted. Most of us sit higher on the scale.

Ernest Shackleton shows the top of the scale. In October 1915 his ship Endurance was trapped in ice and starting to sink, and he had to bring twenty-seven men home. He watched their mood even more closely than the weather. So he read each man. On the first morning he served hot tea himself. He set daily jobs and kept two men who needed extra attention in his own tent. When one rower sank into gloom, he called for hot milk for everyone, so nobody was singled out. The men on the island where they landed were all rescued.

Greene sees empathy as a skill with four parts:

  1. The right attitude. Assume you do not know a person yet, and drop snap judgments.
  2. Feel the mood first. Notice tone of voice and posture before the words.
  3. Learn their past. Ask about family and early years, because values form there.
  4. Check your skill. Gently ask people whether you guessed their feelings right.

People wear masks

People hide feelings behind a mask, but their face, voice and body leak the truth, and you can learn to read it.

Milton Erickson, later an influential psychologist, was seventeen when he woke up paralyzed by polio in August 1919. For months he could only watch and listen to his family. He noticed that a sister's “yes” came with a flat voice and a smirk, and it really meant no. Soon he had counted sixteen different kinds of “no.” He had found a second language, spoken in faces, voices and posture, that tells what words cover up. His motto became “observe, observe, observe.”

Start small. In one talk, look for one or two expressions that go against the words. Next time listen to the voice, and later to posture. Learn each person's usual mood, so a change stands out. When words and body disagree, give more weight to the negative cue, because negative feelings leak out most. Some signs last under a second. They are called microexpressions, quick flashes of tension or a forced smile.

Be careful, though. A cue can mean many things. In Shakespeare's play, Othello sees his wife look nervous and decides she is guilty. His own harsh questions made her nervous. The mistake lies in decoding the cue, not in seeing it. So look first, and ask what else could explain it.

Masks are not bad. We all act differently with a boss and with a friend, and it keeps social life smooth. The word personality comes from a Latin word for mask. Your goal is to see through other people's masks and to wear your own role well.
