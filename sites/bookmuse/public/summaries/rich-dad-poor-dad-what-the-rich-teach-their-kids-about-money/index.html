Rich Dad Poor Dad

A summary of the book by Robert Kiyosaki

7 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Two dads, two views of money

Getting rich is not about working for money. Learn how money works, buy things that put money in your pocket, and let money work for you.

Robert Kiyosaki had two fathers. His own dad was well educated but struggled with money all his life. His best friend Mike's dad never finished eighth grade and became one of the richest men in Hawaii. This book is what Mike's dad, "rich dad," taught the two boys. You will leave knowing how to tell an asset from a liability, and ten steps to begin.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

The rich don't work for money

A paycheck pays your bills, but it keeps you in the race. The rich make money work for them.

At nine, Robert and Mike asked Mike's dad to teach them how to be rich. His deal: they worked three hours every Saturday in one of his small stores, for 10 cents an hour. After a few weeks Robert wanted to quit. Rich dad answered calmly that, in under a month, Robert already sounded like his other workers. Then he stopped paying them at all.

After three weeks of unpaid work, rich dad tested the boys. He offered a raise to 25 cents an hour, then $1, then $2, then $5.

With each offer, rich dad raised the pay. What did Robert do?

Fear of having no money sends you to work. Once the paycheck comes, desire starts you thinking of things to buy, so you need more money. Rich dad called this the Rat Race: get up, go to work, pay bills, repeat. Even the rich can be caught in it, because fear grows with the money.

The way out starts with honesty. Admit to yourself how you feel about money. Then think before you react. When fear says "I need a job," ask a better question: is working harder really the best answer to this problem?

Without pay, the boys had to think. Mrs. Martin, who worked at the store, cut the front cover off old comic books and threw the rest away. The boys asked the man who supplied the comics. He said yes, if they worked for the store and never sold them. So they opened a comic-book library in Mike's basement. Children paid 10 cents to read for two hours. Mike's sister ran the desk, and the boys averaged $9.50 a week. The money came in even when they were not there.

Try this. The next time you feel afraid about money, say it to yourself plainly first. Then ask whether more work is the best answer.

Financial literacy: assets and liabilities

Know an asset from a liability, and buy assets. The book calls this the only rule.

An asset puts money in your pocket. A liability pulls money out of it. Rich dad said the difference is in the numbers, not in words. So follow the cash: where does it flow in, and where does it flow out?

The book makes a bold claim here: your home is a liability. It takes money out through the loan, taxes and repairs. Prices do not always go up. And money tied up in a house cannot buy assets. He does not say never buy a house. When he wants a bigger one, he first buys assets that pay for it. He expects most readers to disagree, so judge it yourself.

The book's picture of three people: the rich own assets, the poor have nothing but costs, and the middle class fills up on liabilities that look like assets. A pay raise does not fix this. More money can make the problem bigger, because spending grows to match it.

At $1,000 a month from assets against $2,000 of costs, Robert has half a month. At $2,000 he is wealthy, even if he is not yet rich. Then the extra income goes back into more assets.

Try this. Ask the book's question about your own life: if you stopped working today, how many days could you live?

Mind your own business

Your business is your asset column, not your job. Keep your job and keep building assets.

In 1974, Ray Kroc, who built McDonald's, gave a talk to a class of MBA students in Austin, Texas. A friend of Robert's sat in that class. Afterward Kroc joined the students for a drink and asked a question.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Kroc asked: "What business am I in?" The students laughed. What did he answer?

Rich dad taught the same idea. Your profession and your business are not the same thing. Ask a banker what her business is. She says banking. But does she own the bank? No, she works there. Most people spend their lives making someone else rich. Their paycheck is only the income column. Your business is the asset column.

Each dollar you put in the asset column becomes your employee. It works all day, and it can work for generations. Rich dad also said the rich buy luxuries last. Robert's wife Kim waited four years for her Mercedes, until their apartment buildings paid for it. Most people buy luxuries first, on credit.

The book names these real assets:

  • A business that runs without you. If you must work there, it is a job.
  • Stocks. Small shares of a company.
  • Bonds. Loans to a company or government that pay interest.
  • Real estate that earns income.
  • Notes. IOUs that pay you back.
  • Royalties. Pay each time your music, scripts or patents are used.
  • Anything else that has value, makes income and can be sold.

Taxes matter here too. Robert says an employee earns, pays taxes, then spends what is left. A business owner with a corporation, a company set up as its own legal body, earns, pays business costs, and pays tax only on what remains. He adds that a corporation can protect assets from lawsuits. Learn the rules where you live, and follow them.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

A man works as a chef in someone else's restaurant. By rich dad's idea, what is his business?

Try this. Hold on to your job, and start buying real assets instead of things that lose value once you get them home.
