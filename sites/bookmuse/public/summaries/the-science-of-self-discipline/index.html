The Science of Self-Discipline

A summary of the book by Peter Hollins

8 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

A skill, not a gift

Self-discipline is a skill you can train, and it works best when you also shape your surroundings, your people and your habits so that you need less willpower.

Peter Hollins was a skinny student who wanted to gain weight. His roommate Mike pushed him to eat at set times, five times a day, and in two months he gained eleven pounds. Later he saw that his surroundings had forced the discipline on him. The book mixes brain science with simple tools for both jobs: raising your willpower, and arranging your life so you rarely need it.

Discipline is a muscle

Self-discipline is not a trait you are stuck with. It grows when you use it.

Brain scans give the book its hope. When people chose between a big reward later and a small one now, part of the brain's decision-making hub, the prefrontal cortex, worked harder. The more you use a brain pathway, the stronger it gets. This is called neuroplasticity: the brain keeps rewiring its links. So each time you hold back, the next time gets easier. Each time you give in, it gets harder.

The book adds that willpower tires, like gas in a tank: each temptation you resist uses some. In 1996, Roy Baumeister put sixty-seven people in a room with fresh cookies and radishes. Some could eat the sweets. The others could eat only radishes. Then everyone got a hard puzzle.

It is a neat story, but later research questions it. Big repeat studies did not find the same drain. So treat the tank as a rough picture, not settled fact.

The advice holds either way: do not test yourself more than you must. Think of a bag of snacks. If you bring it home, you must resist it every time you pass the kitchen. If you skip that aisle at the store, you resist for about ten minutes.

Know what you are working for

Discipline feels like pointless suffering unless you know what it is for.

Ask what drives you, and you may guess wrong. Psychologists Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer interviewed more than 600 managers. 95 percent of them wrongly believed their staff worked hardest for money, bonuses and promotions. Then the two read more than 12,000 diary entries from employees.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

What did the diaries show was the top motivator at work?

People worked hardest when they felt they were improving. Edward Deci found the same with a hard puzzle. One group was offered cash to solve it, and the other was not. On day one the paid group worked almost twice as long. On day two he said the money was gone, and they quickly gave up. The unpaid group worked longer on day two than on day one, and longer again each session. So pay worked briefly, and interest lasted.

The book names three more things people want that keep them going:

  1. Autonomy. You want to call your own shots.
  2. Mastery. You want to get better. Kobe Bryant trained at 4:00 a.m. because he wanted to be the greatest player he could be.
  3. Purpose. You want to make a difference in the world.

For hard moments, the book gives a picture: the hard work is the vegetables, and your payoff is the dessert. You lose sight of the dessert in two ways. You forget it, or it does not matter enough to you. So keep reminders in sight, and pick a goal that you chose yourself.

Sam keeps skipping his morning run. His only reason is that his doctor said he should. What should he do?

Try this. Write down the payoff you are working for. Put a picture or an alarm of it where you will see it.

Wait out the urge

An urge is like a wave. It rises, peaks and passes, and you do not have to fight it.

Discipline is uncomfortable by nature. So the book says the skill to build is how much discomfort you can handle. Psychologist Alan Marlatt, a pioneer in treating addiction, taught urge surfing for this. You treat an urge as a wave and ride it out. Do not think, "I want a cigarette." Think, "I have an urge for one." The urge is a feeling you notice. It is not who you are.

Fighting makes it worse. Try not to think about a hippopotamus, and it is all you think about. Studies the book cites found that pushing thoughts and feelings away makes them stronger. Distracting yourself feeds an urge too. If you battle it, a typical urge peaks twenty to thirty minutes after it arrives.

The book's five steps for urge surfing:

  1. Notice. Find where in your body you feel the urge.
  2. Focus. Watch the sensations in that spot.
  3. Breathe. Notice your breath for one to two minutes.
  4. Picture a wave. See the sensations rise and fall.
  5. Remember. Note how fast it passed, for next time.

A simpler tool is the 10-Minute Rule. Whenever you want something, give it ten minutes first. If you still want it then, have it. If you want to quit something good, like exercise, wait ten more minutes instead.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Priya is trying to stop checking her phone at work. She keeps pushing the urge away, and it comes back stronger. What should she try?

Try this. The next time you want something you should not have, wait ten minutes before you decide.
