Think Like a Monk

A summary of the book by Jay Shetty

8 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

A monk's mind, without the robes

You do not need robes or a monastery to find peace and purpose: you train your mind the way monks do, by letting go, growing and giving.

Jay Shetty spent three years as a monk after college, living in an ashram, a home for monks. He says monks are the experts at training the mind for peace and purpose, and that anyone can think like one. He also teaches three kinds of meditation: breathwork, visualization and chanting. His book has three stages: let go of what holds you back, grow into your purpose, and give to others.

What you'll learn

The book's eleven chapters

The lessons teach identity, negativity, fear, intention, purpose, the mind, gratitude and service. Routine, ego and relationships are left out.

  2. Let go · “Negativity”. Complaining, comparing and criticizing spread.
  3. Let go · “Fear”. A warning to understand, not to bury.
  4. Let go · “Intention”. The why behind what you want.
  5. Grow · “Purpose”. Your calling, called dharma.
  6. Grow · “Routine”. A calm morning, the same time and place each day.
  7. Grow · “The Mind”. The voices in your head.
  8. Grow · “Ego”. A false self that wants to look better than it is.

  10. Give · “Relationships”. Trust grows in stages.
  11. Give · “Service”. The highest purpose.

Wipe the dust off the mirror

Much of what you think you value is dust from other people's voices.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Researchers asked people to sit alone for six to fifteen minutes, with no phone and nothing to read. Then they let them pick what to do: listen to music, use their phone, or something else. What did many people choose?

At the ashram, a senior monk led Shetty to an old mirror in a dusty storeroom. Shetty could not see his face. The monk wiped the glass with his sleeve, and the dust puffed into Shetty's eyes. The monk said that your identity is like that mirror. Dust hides who you are and what you value. Clearing it is not pleasant, but only then can you see yourself.

The dust comes from other people. In 1902 the sociologist Charles Horton Cooley said we are shaped by what we think others think of us. Parents, school and media add opinions, expectations and obligations. If you never look at them, you take them as your own.

To see your real values, look at what you do. Check how you spend your time, your screen time and your money. One woman complained that her family spent too much on afterschool classes. Then she saw that she spent more on her own shoes than on her kids' music lessons.

Do not block the world out. Filter it. When a thought comes, ask whether it fits the values you chose or the ones others chose for you. Shetty asks himself, “Is this dust or is it me?”

In your life

You say family comes first, yet every free evening goes to a game or an app. Your week shows what you really value.

Try this. List some values that shape your life. Beside each, note where it came from, and check the ones you truly share.

Spot, stop, swap

Negativity spreads, so notice it, pause, and swap it for something better.

Complaining is contagious. At a temple in London, one monk complained about how badly he slept and about the food. Soon Shetty was complaining about him to the other monks. He had become what he criticized.

Monks use three steps. First, spot it, because you cannot change what you do not see. Their teachers asked them to go a week without complaining, comparing or criticizing, and to keep a tally of every slip.

Second, stop: breathe, and ask where the feeling comes from. Third, swap it for something better. Envy is a clue to what you want. In the book's example, envy of a friend's busy social life can push you to host a party yourself. The book also teaches mudita, which means feeling joy when good things happen to other people.

A parable shows what suspicion costs. An evil king feared that a good king had poisoned his dinner, so he swapped the plates. The good king laughed, and the evil king grew more nervous and swapped them again. The good king calmly ate, and the evil king went hungry. What we suspect in others often says more about us.

Try this. Count the negative remarks you make in one week, and try to bring the daily count down. Aim for zero.

Find the root of your fear

Fear is a warning, not an enemy: follow it down to its root.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Scientists built a sealed glass home called Biosphere 2 to give plants ideal conditions. Trees inside grew to a certain height and then fell over. What was missing?

Shetty was a new monk when his teacher asked everyone to close their eyes and relive a deep fear. Shetty thought he had none. Then he asked himself again and again, “What am I really scared of?” First came exams. Then getting in trouble at school. Then his parents not getting along. At the bottom he found the root: he was afraid he could not make his parents happy.

Asking again, not rewording the question, corners the brain gently. The book says it should feel like an interview, not an interrogation. Shetty later saw that all his fears led back to how others saw him. Now, when he decides something, he asks if it is shaped by what others will think.

People react to fear in four ways: they panic, freeze, run away or bury it. Burying is like pulling the battery out of a smoke alarm and going back to bed. The fire is still there, and it grows. Facing it, like getting everyone out of the house, makes you stronger.

It also helps to say “I feel afraid” instead of “I am afraid.” The fear is something you feel, and it is not who you are. A slow breath helps in the moment too: in for four, hold for four, out for four. It moves the body from alarm to rest.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Raj thinks, “I am afraid, so I am a nervous person.” What helps most?

Try this. Take a deep dive into your fears. Ask yourself again and again what you are really afraid of. The deeper answers can take time to come.
