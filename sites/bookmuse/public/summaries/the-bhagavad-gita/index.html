The Bhagavad Gita

A summary of the book by Vyasa

A talk on a battlefield

You are a deathless self, so do your duty with a steady mind, let go of the reward, and make God your refuge.

The Bhagavad Gita is an old Sanskrit poem from India, set inside the great epic called the Mahabharata. The poem's last lines name the sage Vyasa as its author, and its date is not settled. It is a talk between a prince named Arjuna and Krishna, who in the poem is God, dressed as a charioteer. They speak in a war chariot between two armies that are about to fight.

Arjuna starts in despair and ends sure of what to do. On the way, Krishna answers three old questions: what am I, how should I act, and how do I stay calm?

You are more than your body

The self inside you was never born and never dies, so grief over what passes is a mistake.

The talk starts with a collapse. Arjuna, a prince and a warrior, asks Krishna, his charioteer, to drive him between the two armies. He looks along both lines and sees his own family: grandfathers, uncles, cousins and friends. His body shakes, his mouth goes dry and his bow slips from his hand. He says he will not fight, and he sinks down on his seat.

Arjuna cannot bear to kill his own family. What does Krishna give him to calm his despair?

Krishna's first answer is about what a person is. The wise, he says, grieve for neither the living nor the dead. Every living being holds a self that was never born and will never die. The poem puts it this way: "Birthless and deathless and changeless remaineth the spirit for ever."

The body is only a home for the self. Krishna says it is like putting away worn clothes and putting on new ones. Nothing can harm the self: weapons cannot reach it, flame cannot burn it and wind cannot dry it.

What hurts is what the senses bring. Heat and cold, sorrow and joy are brief and always changing. Krishna tells Arjuna to bear them, as the wise do. So grief and fear are about the body and its passing feelings. They are not about you.

The poem uses all this to tell Arjuna to fight, as a duty done without hate or hope of reward. For grief and fear, the answer is plain: what ends is the body, and the self does not.

Sam dreads growing old. His body tires a little more each year. What would Krishna say to him?

Try this. Think of one way your body has changed since you were a child. Then remember that the poem says the self stayed the same through every age.

Do the work, not the reward

Do your duty with full effort, and let go of the reward, because the result is never yours alone.

Arjuna hears a puzzle. If deep thought is nobler than action, he asks, why does Krishna push him to act?

Krishna says no one can escape action by sitting still. A person's nature makes them act at every moment, and even a thought is a kind of act. A man who sits still while his mind chases pleasures is a fraud. Work is better than idleness.

So the real question is how to act. Here is the rule he gives: "Let right deeds be thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them." The fruit is the result you hope for, like pay, praise or a win. Do the work because it is right to do it. Then let the result go.

Why let go? Because the result is never only yours. Krishna says five things go into every act: a force, the doer, the tools, the effort and God. You are one of the five. A person who thinks he is the only doer sees nothing.

Letting go does not mean quitting. The poem calls this real renunciation, which means giving up. What you give up is the grip on the result, not the work. Such a person does the work faithfully. If it fails, he is not upset. If it succeeds, he is not too proud.

One more point: do your own task. Your own work done badly is better than someone else's done well. The poem ties this to the four castes, the social ranks of its day, a view that belongs to its time. The plain point is to own your task.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Ravi studies hard for a scholarship and does not win it. He says, "All that work was wasted." What would Krishna say?

Try this. Pick one task you do for praise or pay. Do it fully this time because it is yours to do, and meet the result without being upset or too proud.

Desire grows step by step

A wish you keep dwelling on grows step by step until it wrecks your judgment, so stop it early.

Arjuna asks a hard question. Why does a person do wrong even when they do not want to, as if someone pushed them? Krishna's answer is desire. He calls it man's enemy, mighty in appetite. It covers wisdom the way smoke covers a fire and rust dulls a mirror.

The first step is small. You only keep thinking about something that tempts you. Attraction grows into desire, desire flames into fierce passion, and passion makes you reckless. Then you forget your purpose, and the mind and the person fall apart. That is why the poem says to stop at the first step.

Krishna gives two ways. First, pull your senses in, as a tortoise pulls its feet under its shell. Second, deal with tempting things without loving or hating them, so that they serve you and do not rule you. Calm follows, and with it the end of pain.

Later the book names three doors that lead to ruin: lust, anger and greed. A person who stays out of all three, it says, walks straight toward peace.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Eli wants to stop buying things online. He notices the urge starts when he browses products he likes. Where does the poem's chain say to step in?

Try this. When something tempting pulls at you, draw your attention back, as the tortoise draws in its feet.
