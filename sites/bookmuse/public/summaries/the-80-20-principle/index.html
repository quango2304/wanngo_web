The 80/20 Principle

A summary of the book by Richard Koch

7 lessons · 17 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

A few do most of the work

A few causes make most of the results. Find your few, give them more, and give the rest less, at work and in life.

Richard Koch is a management consultant and investor. He took an old idea about wealth and used it on companies, and then on time, people and happiness. You will learn how to spot the few things that matter, and what to do once you have.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

A few causes, most results

Most of what you get comes from a small part of what you put in. The two sides are rarely equal.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Two economists studied 300 movies released over 18 months. How many of them earned 80 percent of all the box office money?

In 1897 the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto studied who held the money in nineteenth-century England. A small group held most of it. He checked other times and other countries. The same lopsided pattern came back each time.

Koch calls the idea the "80/20 Principle". A small share of the causes, inputs or effort usually brings most of the results, outputs or rewards. In business, about 20 percent of products and 20 percent of customers usually bring about 80 percent of sales and profits.

The numbers are a benchmark, not a law. The split can be 70/30, 95/5 or even more lopsided. The two numbers need not add up to 100. You need two lists: the people or things, and what each one produces, such as profit.

Most of us expect the opposite. We treat every customer, hour and friend as worth about the same. Koch calls this false belief the "50/50 fallacy". When people measure, the real split is usually more lopsided than they guessed. He names the few that matter the "vital few", a phrase from the quality expert Joseph Juran. The rest are the "trivial many".

There is good news in this. If a few things work so well, you can do more of them and less of the rest.

A teacher knows a few pupils cause most of her class trouble. She now counts it in her records. What will she probably find?

In your life

Look at your phone's home screen. A few apps get most of your taps. The rest just sit there.

Try this. Think of your closet. The book says you wear about 20 percent of your clothes about 80 percent of the time. Does that fit yours?

Find your vital few

You can find the few that matter by counting, or by asking one good question and estimating.

Koch gives two ways. "80/20 Analysis" is counting. You list a group of 100 or more, measure one thing for each, rank them from the top and compare the shares.

His example is 100 friends who drink beer. Each says how many glasses they drank last week. The top 20 friends drank 70 percent of all the beer. It took the top 28 friends to reach 80 percent. The bottom 20 drank only 3 percent.

A brewer would aim at the top 20 friends. The same holds for a firm's customers, he says. Look after the few who bring most of the business, and do not give every one equal time.

"80/20 Thinking" is the quick way. You have no data, so you estimate. Ask yourself: what is the 20 percent that leads to 80 percent? Do not assume you already know. A guess can mislead you, he warns. For a big decision, count.

Counting can also be misused. A bookshop saw that 20 percent of titles made 80 percent of sales. It cut its range to bestsellers, and profits fell. Shoppers come to a bookshop for the range. Profit and what customers want matter more than sales alone.

In your life

Think about last month. Which few things gave you most of what you are proud of? Which took your hours and gave little back?

Try this. Pick one area that matters to you and ask Koch's question: what is the 20 percent that leads to 80 percent? Write the answer down.

Where the money comes from

Most firms earn nearly all their profit from a small part of their business, and lose money in another part.

Koch advises firms to split profit by product, by customer and by competitive segment. A segment is a part of the business where you face a different rival. Count profit after every cost. Most managers think they know the answer, and nearly all are wrong.

He did this for a firm that made electronic instruments. Its most profitable customer type gave 15 percent of revenue and 59 percent of profit. Its top six of 15 segments gave 26.3 percent of sales and 82.9 percent of profit. A consultancy he studied found that clients of more than three years gave 26 percent of its business and 84 percent of its profit.

The instruments firm acted on it. It spent more of its time and sales effort on its best segments. It went after more of its best customer types and raised prices for those that cost too much to serve.

Koch's rule for marketing is to delight the top 20 percent of customers and keep them for good. Focusing on the best 20 percent of customers is far easier than focusing on all of them.

One caution. Profit numbers are a snapshot. One segment lost money, but it was new, in a market growing 10 percent a year, and its share was rising. The firm kept it. The analysis should start the strategy, not end it.

In your life

A small cafe owner lists what each table, dish and customer type really brings in. A lunch crowd from one office pays most of the rent.

Try this. Start with products, then customers. Share every overhead cost (the fixed costs of running the firm) across them. Rank them by profit and look at the top fifth.
