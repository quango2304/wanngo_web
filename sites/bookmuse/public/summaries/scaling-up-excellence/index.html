Scaling Up Excellence

A summary of the book by Robert I. Sutton and Huggy Rao

7 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

How good work spreads

Spreading excellence from a few people to many is slow, hands-on work, done one person and one small change at a time.

Robert I. Sutton and Huggy Rao teach at Stanford. They spent seven years studying how teams, companies and charities spread what works without letting it fade. They call this the Problem of More. Their answer is to spread a way of thinking, not just a logo, and to make it stick person by person. The goal is more and better, not just more. Seven ideas carry the book.

A ground war, not an air war

Spreading good work is slow, hands-on work with each person and team, never one big announcement.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

In the air war of World War II, planes dropped bombs on general areas. About how many of the US bombs landed within 1,000 feet of their targets?

The authors use this as a picture of spreading good practice. A speech, a slide show or a few days of training is an air war. A ground war means helping each person and team make one small change after another. They say it is the most important thing they learned.

They also say to spread a mindset, not just a footprint. A footprint is how many people and places carry your name. A mindset is a shared sense of what is right, what to notice and what to ignore.

Stanford shows the cost of skipping the ground work. In 2003 it replaced its old system for paying bills, buying and staff records with a new one that was unfinished and unproven. It switched the new system on for over four thousand people on one day, with too little training and support.

The book blames three things together: leaders who thought the system was better and easier than it was, leaders in too much of a hurry, and leaders who did not know enough. It calls the mix a clusterfug, its polite spelling of a rude word for a rollout that goes badly wrong. The launch left four thousand able people struggling to do their work.

The book's seven mantras

  1. Spread a mindset, not just a footprint. Numbers and logos are not enough.
  2. Engage all the senses. Small sights, sounds and smells can back up a belief.
  3. “Link short-term realities to long-term dreams.” Keep asking how today's work builds the dream.
  4. Accelerate accountability. Build the feeling of “I own the place and the place owns me.”
  5. Fear the clusterfug. Illusion, impatience and incompetence ruin rollouts.
  6. Scaling requires both addition and subtraction. Growing also means removing what no longer helps.
  7. Slow down now, so you scale faster and better later. Switch to slow, careful thinking when the stakes are high.

Try this. Before a big rollout, ask each person to imagine it is a year later and the effort was a disaster. Have each write the causes in detail, including ones they would not usually say aloud. The book calls this a premortem.

Copy or adapt

Decide what must be copied exactly and what people may change, and keep the fixed list short.

The authors borrow two labels from religion. A “Catholic” approach copies the original exactly. A “Buddhist” approach keeps a shared mindset but lets each person and place change the details.

Intel's rule for its chip plants was Copy Exactly: copy every detail, unless that is impossible or the gain from a change is overwhelming. One plant got a better yield, the share of chips that work, after a machine was installed a quarter inch off center. Intel copied that exact position in some twenty more plants, and yield rose in all of them.

Home Depot shows the opposite risk: copying where the place differs. Its twelve China stores, opened in 2006, sold a do-it-yourself idea. Shoppers there who could afford the stores usually paid someone else to do the work. By 2011 its last Beijing store had shut, and the rest followed by late 2012. Yum!, the company that owns KFC, adapted its menu and did well in China.

A middle path is a short list of fixed rules. Kaiser Permanente, a large US health system, had eight regions that each did things their own way. To roll out one electronic health record system, its leaders set five non-negotiables, which they called guardrails. They included one name for the system and one common way to record data. Inside them, regions could choose the rest. The authors advise as few guardrails as you can. Today almost no Kaiser doctor would go back to the old way.

Three questions to ask before you copy or flex

  1. Do you suffer from delusions of uniqueness? Do you think proven methods do not apply to you because you are special?
  2. “Do you have a successful template to use as a prototype?” Start from one complete working model, then change only what fails.
  3. “Will bolstering Buddhism generate crucial understanding, commitment, and innovation?” Would local changes help people understand it, own it and improve it?

Try this. Write down the few things that must stay the same everywhere. Let people choose the rest.

Hot causes, cool solutions

To change what people do, stir a strong feeling and tie it to a clear action, so each pushes the other.

A hot cause is a cause that stirs strong feeling, through a story, a symbol or a reason. A cool solution is a clear, practical action. Talk alone fades: when people only discuss a cause, commitment weakens.

Tap an answer

Stanford students wanted a men's soccer team to wear bike helmets. Only one of its fourteen bike riders did. They began by showing safety numbers. What happened?

The students posted pictures of fallen riders with melons by their heads and shared a classmate's story of a bad crash. Then the players signed a pledge, posted photos of themselves in helmets and promised to throw a watermelon at any teammate caught without one. During the project every player wore a helmet, even one who had said nothing would make him. Soon seven of the nine bike riders on the women's soccer team did too.

JetBlue shows the reverse order: action first. After a 2007 ice storm left hundreds of passengers stranded on planes, an executive asked a doubting group to spend one day mapping on sticky notes every step of closing and reopening New York's airport. They put a pink note wherever something was broken, and used over a thousand. The doubters agreed to try more, and over a hundred fixes followed.

Belief and action feed each other, so start with whichever you can. Here are three of the book's seven ways to keep that circle turning.

  1. Name the problem. A clear name makes it real. A hospital safety group said “Preventable errors are killing too many people.”
  2. Do it where all can see. A stand taken in public is hard to drop.
  3. New rituals, better rituals. At one firm, executives hung their coats on a rack as a reminder to put facts before pride.

Try this. Pair a story that stirs feeling with a pledge people make in front of others, as the soccer players did.
