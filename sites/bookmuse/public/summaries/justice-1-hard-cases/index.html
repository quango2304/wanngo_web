Three ways to ask what is fair

When people argue about what is fair, they usually lean on one of three ideas: welfare, freedom or virtue.

Picture the town of Linden. Its library has one meeting room. A book club has booked it every Thursday for years. Now a group of parents asks for Thursdays, to run a homework club. Who should get the room?

One answer looks at welfare. That means how well people's lives go. The homework club may help dozens of children, and the book club has a dozen members. This way of thinking adds up the good for everyone and picks the use that helps most.

A second answer looks at freedom. That means respecting people's own choices and rights. The book club booked first and followed the rules, so the room is theirs to use. Taking it away would break a fair deal. The parents reply that a deal made years ago should not block every new need.

A third answer looks at virtue. That means good character. A good town honors loyalty, and it also cares for its young. This view asks what Linden should reward, and what kind of town it wants to be.

The three ideas can disagree. One act can pass one test and fail another. Philosophy does not end the argument at once. It makes each reason clear, so you can see where you really differ.

Thinkers have long split on this. Aristotle, an ancient Greek, said justice means giving each person their due, and that takes a view of which qualities deserve honor. Kant and Rawls, much later, said justice must not depend on any one picture of a good life. Each person is free to choose their own.

In your life

A roommate says the rent split is unfair because he has the smallest room. He may mean that the split leaves him worse off, that nobody asked him, or that it is not how good friends behave. Listen for which of the three matters most to him.

The runaway trolley

A rule can feel obvious until a hard case pulls it two ways.

Philosophers test ideas on made-up cases. The most famous is the runaway trolley. Its brakes have failed, and it rolls toward five workers on the track. They cannot get out of the way. You are driving. You can steer onto a side track, where one worker stands. Turn, and one person dies. Stay on course, and five do.

Most people say turn. Their rule is simple: save as many lives as you can.

Now change one thing. You are not the driver. You stand on a bridge above the track, and a heavy stranger stands beside you. If you push him off, he stops the trolley and the five live. He dies. The count is the same as before.

Most people say do not push. Yet the rule that said turn now says push. One of the two answers has to change.

People try to explain the gap. One try: you only want to stop the trolley, not to kill anyone. That is true in both cases. Another try: pushing is just more upsetting, and you should overcome it. Maybe. A third try: the stranger never chose to be involved. But neither did the worker on the side track. Even so, many people still feel the difference is real.

None of the tries settles it, and that is useful. The puzzle shows two principles we hold pulling against each other: save as many lives as you can, and do not take an innocent life, whatever the goal. The case strips away doubt on purpose. You know for sure who will die, so you can study the principles, not the guessing.

The two principles echo lesson 1. Counting lives is welfare thinking. “Do not take an innocent life” is about what we owe each person, close to the freedom idea of rights. And our horror at pushing speaks to virtue: what kind of person does that? A hard case makes the ideas clash, and that is how we learn how much each one weighs.

Moving between cases and rules

Moral thinking is a loop: judge a case, state your rule, test it on a harder case, then revise.

The trolley shows the loop. You judge: turn. You ask why, and state a rule: save the most lives. Then the bridge case arrives, and the rule seems wrong. That confusion is where real thinking starts.

You can revise in two ways. You may change your judgment: perhaps pushing is right after all. Or you may change the rule: perhaps it needs a limit, such as “unless it uses a person against their will.” Neither move is automatic. You weigh which one you trust more, then test again.

The same loop works at home. Say two roommates share a rule: whoever cooks never washes up. It feels fair for years. Then one of them cooks for ten guests and uses every pot. The rule now feels unfair. They keep the idea behind it, that cooking is work, and add a limit: after a big party, everyone helps.

Two traps are easy to fall into. One is changing a rule only to escape one awkward case, without asking if the new rule fits other cases. The other is never changing a rule at all. A good thinker does the work in between: the new rule has to prove itself on cases you have not seen yet.

This is an old way to think. Socrates and Aristotle worked like this. A hard case is not a trick. It is how a rule gets better.
