Love Unfu*ked

A summary of the book by Gary John Bishop

8 lessons · 17 min · quiz and takeaways

Love starts with you

A good relationship does not come from the right partner. You build it by managing yourself and keeping your word.

Gary John Bishop is a coach who grew up in Glasgow and moved to the United States in 1997. He wrote this book in 2022 for anyone who wants real love, whether you are single, dating or married. It is not about your partner’s faults. It is about how you do relationships, and how you can start again.

The book follows one path: what to handle before you get in, how to manage yourself while you are in, and how to end it well if you ever must.

All 8 key ideas in Love Unfu*ked

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Tell yourself the truth

A relationship that works just enough to get by is not a good one. You cannot fix it until you say so.

Think of someone with a slow leak in a tire. Every morning on the way to work, they stop and add air. They say it is working. It is, just enough to get them there. The air keeps seeping out all the time.

Bishop says we treat relationships the same way. We top them up each day and call them fine. The leak still drains us. It sits in the back of the mind as a small worry that never goes away. The philosopher Kierkegaard said, “People settle for a level of despair they can tolerate and call it happiness.”

Staying together proves little. Divorce is near 50 percent, but the other half are not all happy. Some couples stay to spite each other, or to avoid being judged. When Bishop says he has been married for twenty years, audiences clap. He calls that a long-service medal, the kind of prize an employee gets for years served, handed out without asking how the marriage is. He would rather ask about quality, joy and connection.

So the first step is plain. Say it: this is not working the way it could. Only then can you ask why.

Tap an answer

Dana and Kit fight, take a weekend away, and are fine until the next fight. They say, “At least we are not falling apart.” What should Dana do first?

Try this. Look closely at your relationship. Ask if it works, or only works enough to get by.

Why you chose each other

Most of us walk into love hoping the other person will fix something in us. They walk in hoping the same.

Almost everyone carries a small hole they cannot fill. It can feel like not being enough. Then you meet someone who seems to have the answer: their confidence, kindness, ambition or calm.

Bishop calls this the identity relationship. He says it is the most common thing that pulls two people together. Under looks, jobs and shared tastes, you each hoped the other would fix you.

Later it turns. The person who looked like the answer becomes the target for your old childhood hurts. Your partner has not become your mom or dad. You only look at your old upsets through them. Nobody can be your mom, your dad or your ex.

Bishop knows the trap. He wanted a relationship unlike his parents’, and his way of getting it was a mess. Copying a failed model fails, and so does running from it. The problem may not be a broken partner. It may be how you went in.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Priya was drawn to Sam’s confidence. Now she feels small next to it. What should she look at first?

Try this. Think back to the relationship that mattered most. Ask what you hoped that person would fix in you.

You would rather be right

You would rather keep your view than have love. The only side you can change is yours.

Bishop says your view does not guide you. It holds you in a cell. You will fight for it even when it is hurting your health, your money or your family.

Look at any fight in your life. Either you feel right, or they do. That belief keeps the fight in place. Being right is not the same as being accurate. A plain fact carries no feeling. Being right comes with resentment, anger, judgment or smugness.

Bishop also names a quiet version, the compatibility itch. When someone does not fit your ideal, you cut them out, and your world gets smaller. He skips tips on how to communicate, too. Shift your view, he says, and the talk shifts with it.

So the way out is not to change your partner. You work on yourself for its own sake. If you change so they will change, you have missed the point. A change in them is a bonus.

That is why the book says not to read it to fix someone else. Talk about what you found, even buy them a copy, then stop. Threatening to leave does not work either. They may jump to attention, but it is only a show to keep you happy.

One caution: if abuse or addiction is part of your relationship, the book says to handle that on its own, and stay safe.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Sam finds the chapter on being right so true that he wants his wife to read it. What should he do?

Try this. Next time you feel sure, notice the feeling that comes with it. Resentment or smugness means you are holding on to being right.
