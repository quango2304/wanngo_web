Drama Free

A summary of the book by Nedra Glover Tawwab

8 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Changing you is enough

You cannot change your family, but you can change how you take part in it, and that is enough to keep a hard relationship on your terms or to end it.

Nedra Glover Tawwab is a licensed therapist. She says that family is what people most often want to talk about in therapy, and that the way you act in your family is usually the way you act in the world. Her book shows what an unhealthy family looks like, why its patterns repeat, and what to do about it.

The book is not a replacement for a therapist. If it stirs up reactions that feel too big, she asks you to seek therapy. The book covers addiction and abuse.

What dysfunction looks like

A dysfunctional family treats abuse, chaos or neglect as normal, so it is hard to see from the inside.

Carmen’s father came home from work, got drunk and raged. Her mother drank too much and stayed in her room. When her parents argued, Carmen and her siblings blasted the TV. At her friends’ homes she saw that this was not normal. But when she tried to talk to her parents, they got defensive and blamed her. The rest of the family said, “That’s just who your parents are.” So she thought she was the problem.

Tawwab says to believe people who tell you there is a problem. Three of the main roots are these.

Addiction. A person cannot stop even when it harms their life. A parent can love you and still be unable to stop, so it is not about you. Emotional neglect. A child does not get enough care and attention, and the wounds do not show. Abuse. A child is hit, called names or threatened.

She also asks you to be honest, at least with yourself. Denial keeps you from breaking free.

Why the pattern repeats

You mostly repeat what you saw at home without knowing it, and noticing is where change starts.

A woman told Tawwab she put sugar in her spaghetti sauce because her mother did. Her mother had it from her grandmother. No one knew why. Sugar has a purpose: it cuts the acid in tomatoes. But some habits we copy have no purpose at all, and unless we ask, they keep going.

Harder habits travel the same way. Kids learn to yell, or to avoid every argument, from what they watch. A saying goes, “You can’t give what you didn’t get,” and some parents simply lack the tools, so they repeat what they know.

The book gives three reasons a pattern stays. People fear being pushed out of the family. They are content with how things are. Or they lack the tools to start.

Watching is not fate. In a story Tawwab retells, two boys grew up with an alcoholic father. One became an alcoholic and the other never drank. Each said, “I watched my father.” Same home, two paths. You do not have to change everything, and you need not be the exact opposite of your family. Noticing, then one small step, is enough.

Breaking the cycle

Change comes in five stages, and it usually starts when you get tired enough of how things are.

Kelly’s brother Jeff bullies and manipulates the family. Her two other siblings no longer have a relationship with him. Kelly stays, hoping he will change, because she feels she is the only person he has. She knows he mistreats her, but guilt holds her back. The book places her at the second stage. Her two siblings are at the last.

Knowing there is a problem is only the first step. People change when they get tired enough: tired of the same feeling, the same talk, or the same behavior. A big event can also push you, such as becoming a parent. If you feel stuck at the second stage, ask: What am I giving up to stay the same? Who gains if I change nothing?

A cyclebreaker is someone who sets out to break a family pattern. You survive when you do not repeat it, and you thrive when you build a new path. Noticing is not enough. You have to act. Expect pushback, such as “You think you’re better than us.” You do not have to take it personally.
