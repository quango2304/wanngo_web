The 5 Love Languages of Children

A summary of the book by Gary Chapman and Ross Campbell

A full tank of love

Your child needs to feel your love, not only to have it, and you can fill that need by speaking the love language she understands best.

Gary Chapman is a marriage counselor. Ross Campbell is a doctor who worked with children and teenagers. Parents asked Chapman for a children's version of his book for couples, so the two wrote this one. They name five ways to show love. In their experience, a child who feels loved is easier to guide and to teach.

Fill the love tank first

A child does well only when she feels loved, so fill her emotional tank before you train her.

Brad and Emily were puzzled by their eight-year-old son, Caleb. He had been a good learner, but now he went to his teacher's desk up to eight times a day to ask her to explain the work again. At lunch he pushed other children aside to sit near her. He also fought more with his older sister.

Emily had just started a full-time job, so Caleb spent his afternoons with his grandmother. Brad had stopped going to football games, where he used to take Caleb. At a seminar, the parents decided Caleb's love language was quality time, which means focused, undivided attention, and that he was getting less of it. Gary Chapman told them to give him large doses of it.

Within two or three weeks, the teacher asked what they had done, because the pushing and the questions had stopped. The authors say every child has an emotional tank, and love is the fuel. When it runs low, trouble starts. Even a child who is loved can feel unloved, if the love does not come in a way she understands.

Tap an answer

Can a child be spoiled by too much love from her parents?

The love that fills the tank is unconditional: love for who the child is, not for what she does. Conditional love is the opposite. It comes with prizes for good behavior and good results. The authors say it leaves a child anxious, and sure that doing her best is never enough. Unconditional love is not permissive. Training and discipline come after the tank is full, and the tank needs refilling often.

Children read love in what you do. Saying "I love you" is not enough on its own. You can act in a loving way even on a day when you don't feel loving.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Jo's father tells her every day that he loves her. Lately she is clingy and grumpy, and he is busy with a deadline. What would help most?

Try this. At the end of a hard day, do one loving thing before you feel like it. A hug counts.

Five ways to say it

Children take in love in five ways, and each child feels it most through one of them.

The authors name five love languages: "physical touch, words of affirmation, quality time, gifts and acts of service." Two children in one family often feel loved in different ways.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Julie brought her two daughters a gift from a trip. Meredith, eight, jumped for joy. Mallory, six, said thanks, then asked to hear every detail of the trip. What did Mallory want most?

  1. Physical touch: hugs, kisses, a hand on the back, wrestling, high fives. Boys of seven to nine often pull away from soft touch but still like rough play.
  2. Words of affirmation: kind words, encouragement, and praise for something true. Praise given for nothing sounds like flattery.
  3. Quality time: undivided attention, doing something together, and loving eye contact.
  4. Gifts: a gift is given freely. A toy offered for cleaning a room is payment, not a gift. Size and cost do not matter.
  5. Acts of service: do for your child what he cannot yet do, then teach him as he gets ready.

Touch needs care as children grow. A mother should not hug her teenage son in front of his friends. A hug at home after practice is welcome. Never force touch on a teen who pulls away.

Speak all five, even when one is your child's main one. In the authors' view, a mature adult can give and receive love in all five ways, and few adults can. The same ideas work between spouses. And when a child cannot take love from a parent for a while, as after a divorce, a grandparent or another adult can speak her language.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Eight-year-old Sam used to cuddle with his mother. Now he squirms away whenever she tries to hold him. How can she best give him touch?

Try this. Hug your child every day when she leaves for school and when she comes home.

Find your child's language

You find your child's main love language by watching and listening over time, not by asking.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

You want to find your ten-year-old's love language. Should you tell him what you are doing?

So look and listen. The book gives five ways: how your child shows love to you, how she shows it to others, what she asks for most, what she complains about most, and which of two choices she picks. A young child tends to give the kind of love she most wants to get. Keep the search quiet. Children are self-centered, and a child who learns the idea may use it to get what he wants.

A single complaint proves little. "Daddy, you work too much" may repeat what Mom said. A present she gives only because you suggested it is no clue. Look for a pattern: more than half of the complaints pointing to one language, for example "You're always busy."

The most direct tool is a choice between two. Ask a six-year-old, "Would you rather wrestle, or read a story together?" That is touch against quality time. Keep a record. One answer shows only the mood of the moment, so the authors want you to offer a good many choices, twenty to thirty, before you trust a pattern.

Under age five, you cannot tell. Speak all five. A love language is also not set in stone. It can change, especially in the teen years, so don't peg a child.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Seven-year-old Ana tells her grandmother and the mail carrier how nice they look, says "You're the best" to her dog, and asks every day, "Was my drawing good?" What is her most likely main language?

Try this. Offer a choice of two: "We could take the dog to the park, or I could help you study. Which would you like?" Note what she picks.
