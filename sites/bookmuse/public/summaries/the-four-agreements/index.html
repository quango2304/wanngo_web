The Four Agreements

A summary of the book by Don Miguel Ruiz

6 lessons · 16 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

A guide to inner freedom

As children we agreed to beliefs we never chose, and four new agreements can replace the ones that make us suffer.

Don Miguel Ruiz wrote this short book in 1997. He says its ideas come from the Toltec, an old tradition of southern Mexico whose people were known as “women and men of knowledge.” You will see where your beliefs came from, four promises to yourself that loosen them, and the freer life he pictures at the end.

It is a spiritual teaching, not research. It gives no studies, and many of its claims are Ruiz's own views.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Beliefs you never chose

As a child you took on the beliefs around you without choosing them. A harsh inner Judge now enforces them.

Every child grows up inside a dream: the rules, beliefs and customs of family and society. Ruiz calls it the dream of the planet. Your beliefs paint a picture of life, so each person sees the world a little differently. Parents, school and church get a child's attention and repeat the rules until they stick. Even language is an agreement. You did not pick English, your religion or your name.

In Ruiz's view, we store a belief only when we agree with it, and children agree with everything adults say. He calls the training that follows domestication, because it works like training a dog: a reward for obeying and a punishment for not obeying. The reward is attention and approval. So a child fears punishment, and also fears losing the reward.

To win the reward, we pretend to be someone we are not. Soon no one else needs to train us, because we do it ourselves. The beliefs become a Book of Law in the mind. A Judge inside us checks everything we do against it and says we are guilty. The Victim is the part that takes the blame, guilt and shame.

This is unfair. True justice, the book says, is paying once for each mistake. Animals pay once. People pay again each time they remember it. We also build an image of a perfect person to please others. We never match it, so we reject ourselves.

The book does not blame parents. They taught what they knew, and they were trained too. The first step to change is awareness: you cannot change what you cannot see.

Be impeccable with your word

Your word is powerful. Use it for truth and love, never against yourself or other people.

The word is the most powerful tool a human has. The book calls this first agreement the most important and the hardest to keep. Like a sword with two edges, it can create a beautiful life or destroy one. To be impeccable with your word is to use it with truth and love. “Impeccable” means “without sin.” By sin he means anything you say, believe or feel that goes against yourself, such as judging or blaming yourself.

The book tells of a mother who came home after a bad day at work, tired and with a terrible headache. Her young daughter was singing and jumping happily. The mother lost control and shouted that her daughter's voice was ugly. The girl believed it. She stopped singing, became shy at school and found it hard even to speak to others. She had a beautiful voice, but she never sang again.

The book calls such an opinion a spell. A word works like a seed. It grows only in a mind that is ready for it, and children believe what adults say. The mother did not know what her word could do. A word you believe becomes an agreement. Only a new agreement based on truth can break such a spell.

Gossip is the worst use of the word. It is like a computer virus that spreads from mind to mind. Say you hear before your first class that the teacher is awful. You believe it and see him through that person's eyes. You tell others in the class, and they see him that way too. You hate the class and drop out. You blame the teacher, but gossip is to blame. So treat gossip you hear as one opinion, and do not pass it on.

The same goes for what you tell yourself. We say, “I'm stupid, I never understand anything.” You can measure how impeccable your word is by how much you love yourself.

Don't take anything personally

What people say and do comes from their own beliefs and fears, not from you.

Take the book's example: a stranger on the street says, “Hey, you are so stupid.” It is not about you, because he does not know you. It is about him. You take it personally only when you agree with it. Then the words go in like poison. It turns into hurt and anger, and you feel trapped.

He calls the habit personal importance, the idea that everything is about “me.” Each person lives in a separate dream, shaped by what they learned as a child. So what they say comes from their own beliefs. If someone says you look fat, they are dealing with their own feelings. Take it, and their poison becomes yours. Leave it, and you stay immune.

This works for praise too. The author does not take it personally when people call him the best or the worst. They call him an angel when they are happy and a devil when they are mad. He knows what he is, so he does not need anyone to tell him.

Think of it as your own movie. You are the director and the main actor, and everyone else has a small part. When someone gets mad at you, they are dealing with their own fear. You are only the excuse.

This does not mean putting up with bad treatment. If someone treats you without love or respect, their walking away is a gift to you. You are never responsible for what others do, only for yourself. And with this agreement you can say “I love you” without fear, ask for what you need, and say yes or no without guilt.
