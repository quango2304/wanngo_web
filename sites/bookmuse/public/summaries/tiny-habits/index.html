Tiny Habits

A summary of the book by BJ Fogg

7 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Change by design, not by willpower

BJ Fogg says that failing to change is a design flaw, not a flaw in you: pick a tiny behavior you already want, tie it to something you already do, and celebrate it right away.

BJ Fogg started the Behavior Design Lab at Stanford University. He tested his Tiny Habits method with more than 40,000 people. Habits grow from small steps that feel good, he found, not from big promises. You will learn how behavior works, how to build a habit, and how to untangle a bad one.

What you’ll learn

Three things make a behavior

A behavior needs three things at once: motivation, ability and a prompt. BJ Fogg writes this as B = MAP.

Motivation is how much you want to do it. Ability is how easy it is for you. A prompt is the cue that tells you to do it now, like a ringing phone.

In 2010, Fogg was on a gym machine when a text asked him to give to the Red Cross after the Haiti earthquake. He gave on the spot. He wanted to help, and replying was easy. A call asking for a card number, with his wallet in the car, would have been too hard. A letter he tossed out unread would have been no prompt. Take away one of the three and he does not give.

Good habits and bad ones run on the same three parts. Katie, an executive, scrolled in bed each morning instead of working out. Her phone was her alarm, set for 4:30 a.m., so the prompt was always there. Scrolling was easy and fun. Her fix was not willpower. Each night she left the phone in the kitchen and used an old-fashioned alarm clock. Then she started working out.

To stop a habit · Remove the prompt

Removing the prompt is the best first move. In a hotel room, Fogg hid a basket of salty snacks behind a cabinet door. He left it shut for three days.

To fix a behavior that is not happening, check in this order. Never start with motivation.

  1. 1. Prompt: does something remind you at the right moment?
  2. 2. Ability: what is making it hard to do?
  3. 3. Motivation: only last, if the first two are fine.

Pick what you already want

Motivation comes and goes, so pick behaviors you already want to do, not ones you think you should.

Tap an answer

Each year, almost one hundred million people sign up for an online course. How many of them finish it?

A sudden burst of motivation is a Motivation Wave. It crests, then crashes. Sandra and Adrian bought a home and sanded, painted and scrubbed every room. Then they faced the backyard, and their energy dropped sharply. Motivation carried them through one hard job, not the next.

Fogg splits wishes from actions. An aspiration is a wish, like sleeping better. An outcome is something you can measure. A behavior is an action you can take at once, like turning off your phone. Wishes come true only through behaviors.

Pick one aspiration and list many behaviors that could help, with no limits. That page of ideas is a Swarm of Behaviors. Then make a Focus Map. Put each behavior on a card. Slide it up for how much it helps. Slide it right for how well you can get yourself to do it. The top right corner holds your Golden Behaviors: they help, you want them, and you can do them.

Fogg calls this Behavior Matching and says it is the most important step. The key word is want, not should. His first maxim, or rule: “Help people do what they already want to do.” Matching is step 3 of seven. Here are Fogg’s seven steps, in order.

  1. Step 1: Clarify the Aspiration. Say what you want.
  2. Step 2: Explore Behavior Options. List many behaviors.
  3. Step 3: Match with Specific Behaviors. Keep the Golden ones.
  4. Step 4: Start Tiny. Shrink the behavior until it is easy.
  5. Step 5: Find a Good Prompt. Tie it to something you already do.
  6. Step 6: Celebrate Successes. Feel good right away.
  7. Step 7: Troubleshoot, Iterate, & Expand. Fix what fails, then add more.

Make it tiny

Simplicity changes behavior. Make a habit so easy that you can do it on your worst day.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Fogg, a behavior scientist, could not make flossing a daily habit. When he finally did, how many teeth did he floss at first?

He asked a new question: what is making this hard to do? Five things can make a behavior hard. They form the Ability Chain, which is only as strong as its weakest link.

  1. Time: do you have enough?
  2. Money: do you have enough?
  3. Physical effort: are you able to do it?
  4. Mental effort: does it need a lot of creative or mental energy?
  5. Routine: does it fit your day, or must you change it?

His weak link was physical effort. His teeth are very close together, so thick floss was a struggle. He tried about fifteen kinds until one fit. Then he made the habit tiny: after brushing, he flossed one tooth.

Motivation changes all day, but you can make a behavior easy. In Fogg’s model, the Action Line is the curve that splits what you will do from what you won’t. An easy behavior stays above it even when motivation is low.

There are three ways to make a behavior easier: build a skill, get a tool or help, or make it tiny. Tiny comes in two kinds. A Starter Step is the first small move, like putting on walking shoes. Scaling Back is a smaller version, like walking only to the mailbox. The tiny version is the habit. Anything more is extra credit.

A big habit is like a plant with shallow roots, and a storm knocks it over. A tiny one survives a cold or a busy week, then grows deeper roots.

To stop a habit · Make it harder

Fogg and his partner keep no ice cream in the freezer. To eat some, they would have to drive to the store and buy it.
