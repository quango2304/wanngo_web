The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People

A summary of the book by Stephen R. Covey

8 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Change yourself first, then the rest follows

Lasting change works from the inside out: change how you see things, then build seven habits that take you from depending on others, to standing on your own, to working well with others.

Stephen R. Covey read about 200 years of writing on success. He saw that old books taught character, while newer ones sold quick tricks and a good image. He wrote this book in answer. It gives seven habits that grow in a set order, and you can take them one at a time.

See it from the inside out

Lasting change starts inside you, with how you see things and who you are. Tricks only treat the surface.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

In a Harvard Business School class, half the students studied a picture of a young woman for ten seconds. The other half studied an old woman. Then everyone looked at one drawing that holds both. What did they see?

Each of us carries a map of how the world works, and we seldom check it. Imagine a street map of Chicago that is really a map of Detroit. Trying harder only gets you to the wrong place faster. A better attitude only makes you happy to be lost.

Covey found the same split in 200 years of success books. The first 150 years or so taught character: integrity, humility, courage, patience. After World War I, the books turned to personality: a good image, clever techniques and a positive attitude. He calls these the Character Ethic and the Personality Ethic.

These skills are not useless, only second in line. If your character is not sound, Covey argues, people feel it, and trust never grows. Tricks are like cramming for a test: they may get you by, but you learn nothing deep.

The habits come in this order because good teamwork cannot rest on weak self-control. Private victories come before public ones.

Try this. Pick one problem that keeps coming back. Ask the book's question: could the way I see it be part of the problem?

Habit 1: Be proactive

Between what happens to you and what you do, there is a gap. You can choose your response, so you are in charge of your own life.

Viktor Frankl was a psychiatrist held in the Nazi death camps. He lost his parents, his brother and his wife. His captors controlled his surroundings and his body. But one day he saw that he could still decide how it would affect him. Between what happened and his response was his freedom to choose.

He used his memory and imagination to exercise that freedom, and it grew. He inspired the people around him, even some guards.

This gap is where being proactive lives. A proactive person takes responsibility: your behavior comes from your decisions, not your conditions. Covey splits the word: response-able, able to choose your response. Reactive people feel good only when the weather and the people around them are good. Proactive people carry their own weather.

Your words give you away. “That's just how I am.” “He makes me so mad.” “I have to.” Each one hands your power to someone else. A student told Covey he had to go on a tennis trip. If he did not, he would be kicked off the team. So Covey said he chose to go.

Reactive people spend energy on other people's weak points and on problems they cannot control, so their influence shrinks. Proactive people work on what they can change, and their influence grows.

Tap an answer

Your manager keeps changing the plan at the last minute. Which reply is proactive?

Try this. For thirty days, put your effort only into things you can change. Make small promises to yourself and keep them.

Habit 2: Begin with the end in mind

Know where you want to end up, and let that picture guide each day. Being busy is not the same as being on the right path.

The book starts with an exercise. Picture your own funeral, three years from today. Four people speak: someone from your family, a friend, someone from your work, and someone from a group you serve. What would you want each of them to say about you?

Your answer shows what success means to you. Fame or money may not even be on the list.

Many people climb the ladder of success, then find it leans against the wrong wall. Every step then takes them to the wrong place faster. You can be very busy and very efficient, and still not effective.

Everything is made twice. First in your mind, then in the real world. A builder draws a clear blueprint before touching the earth, because changes later cost far more. Your life is the same: if you do not shape the first picture, other people and chance will.

His tool is a personal mission statement, which he calls a personal constitution. It says who you want to be, what you want to do, and the values behind both. He agrees with Frankl that you detect your mission more than invent it, so it can take weeks or months to feel right. Covey reviews his own from time to time. One time, alone on a beach after a bike ride, it took him several hours, and he felt clear and free.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

A friend works very long weeks for a promotion but has never asked what it is for. What does beginning with the end in mind suggest first?

Try this. Write down what you would want each of the four funeral speakers to say about you. Then set aside time to start your personal mission statement.
