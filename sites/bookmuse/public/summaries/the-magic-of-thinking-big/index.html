The Magic of Thinking Big

A summary of the book by David J. Schwartz

8 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Think bigger, then act bigger

How far you go depends on how big you think: believe you can, drop your excuses and act, and your results grow to match.

David J. Schwartz was a professor and a sales trainer. His book opens with a sales meeting, where an executive points to his top rep. The rep earned five times the average. The executive asked the room what set him apart. The book first came out in 1959, so the money amounts are from that time.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Believe you can

When you believe you can do it, your mind goes to work on how.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

A top sales rep at one company earned almost $60,000 in a year. The other reps averaged $12,000. The company compared him with the rest. What set him apart?

This is not wishing. You cannot wish yourself into an executive suite. Belief is different: when you are sure you can do something, your mind starts to find the how.

A state highway official told him about eight bridges that would cost $5,000,000 to build. The state wanted one firm to design them for a 4 percent fee, or $200,000. The official talked to twenty-one engineering firms. The four largest bid at once. Sixteen of the seventeen small ones went over the plans and said the job was too big for them. The last one had only three engineers. They studied the plans, said "We can do it," and got the job. The size scared sixteen firms away, and belief made the difference for the one that stayed.

Your mind, he says, is a thought factory with two foremen. Mr. Triumph builds reasons why you can. Mr. Defeat builds reasons why you can't. Both obey at once, and the more work you give one, the stronger he gets.

His advice is blunt: fire Mr. Defeat and give every thought to Mr. Triumph. He gives three guides. Think success, not failure. Remind yourself that you are better than you think. And believe big, because the size of your success follows the size of your belief.

Drop your excuses

An excuse you repeat starts to feel true, and it holds you back.

The book calls the habit of excusing yourself excusitis, a failure disease. In his view, every failure has a bad case, and most average people have a mild one. The thought goes: "I'm not doing well. What reason can I give so I look fine?" Each repeat sinks the excuse deeper. At first you half know it is a lie. Soon it feels like the real cause.

Successful people had good excuses too: Roosevelt's lifeless legs, Truman's lack of college, Kennedy's youth, and heart attacks for Johnson and Eisenhower. They did not hide behind them.

The book names four common forms of excusitis, and a cure for each.

  1. Health. Do not talk or worry about it, and be glad for the health you have.
  2. Intelligence. How you use your mind matters more than how much mind you have.
  3. Age. Count the productive years you still have, and start now.
  4. Luck. Nothing happens without a cause, so look for the cause behind each win and loss.

Intelligence excusitis is the quiet one. Chuck graduated with honors and learned every reason a small business can fail. He never started one and now audits freight shipments.

Take the age excuse. Cecil was forty and wanted to go out on his own in sales, but he thought he was too old. Schwartz asked him when a working life begins and when it ends. Cecil said about twenty, and about seventy if a person stays in good shape and likes the work. That is fifty years. Cecil had used twenty of them.

Cecil stopped thinking "I'm already old" and began to see the many years ahead.

Act before you feel ready

Fear shrinks when you act, and it grows while you wait.

A paratrooper instructor told Schwartz that the jump is not the hard part. The waiting is. A trainee who thinks too long about it gets more scared with each trip, until he cannot jump at all. The rule: action cures fear, and waiting feeds it.

Many people wait for perfect conditions. J. M. had a wife, a child and a modest income. His rent was as much as the monthly payments on a new home. The problem was a $1,200 down payment. He told his wife they would buy a house, before he knew how. Then he asked a builder to lend him the $1,200 and take it back at $100 a month, with interest. The builder did not like the idea at first, but J. M. kept asking until he agreed. A $25 cut in spending and ten extra weekend hours of work from his boss paid for it. Deciding first set his mind to find the way.

C. D. had an idea to import low-cost gifts and toys. Ten years later he still works at the Customs Office. Each time he was about to start, something stopped him: no money, a recession, a new baby.

The cure has two steps. First, isolate your fear: pin down exactly what you are afraid of. Second, take some action, because there is some action for any kind of fear. Schwartz adds a rule: to think confidently, act confidently. His five ways:

  1. Be a front seater. Sit near the front, not in the back row.
  2. Make eye contact. Look the other person in the eye.
  3. Walk 25 percent faster. Shoulders back, head up.
  4. Speak up. Say something at every meeting.
  5. Smile big. Smile until your teeth show.
