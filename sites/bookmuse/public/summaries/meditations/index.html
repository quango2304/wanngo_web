A summary of the book by Marcus Aurelius

A busy emperor's notes to himself

You cannot control what happens, only what you think and do, so judge calmly, treat people kindly and do your work now.

Marcus Aurelius was the Roman emperor from 161 to 180 AD, and he followed Stoicism, an old philosophy of self-control and a calm mind. He wrote these notes for himself and never meant them to be read by others. Two of the twelve parts say they were written while he was away fighting in Germany. The book has no plot. A few ideas return again and again, and they are the seven below.

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Your opinion is what hurts

Things outside you cannot hurt your mind. Your opinion about them can.

Marcus comes back to this idea more than any other. If something outside you upsets you, he says, the thing is not what upsets you. Your opinion about it is. And you can drop that opinion right now. He puts it in two short lines: drop the thought that you have been harmed, and the harm is gone.

That sounds too easy, so he offers a way to do it. Hold on to what you first saw or heard, and add nothing. Say someone tells you that a man speaks badly of you. That is all you were told. You were not told that you are hurt. Or say you see that your child is sick. You see the sickness. You do not see that the child is in danger. That part is your own addition.

Your body can still feel things. You do not have to pretend pain is not there. He says that feeling is natural. Your mind only needs to refuse to put "this is bad" on top of it.

In your life

A friend takes hours to answer your message. You feel ignored. All you know is that no answer has come yet. The feeling of being ignored is the part you added.

Try this. Next time something upsets you, say only what you actually saw or heard. Add nothing to it.

See what things really are

Fancy things look less grand when you say plainly what they are.

Marcus had a habit for things that dazzle him. He looked at them and gave them a plain name. When a rich meal is in front of him, he tells himself it is the dead body of a fish, a bird or a pig. A famous wine is a little grape juice. A purple robe is wool from a sheep, colored with the blood of a shellfish.

Showy things twist your thinking: the fancy look makes you forget what the thing is. They fool you most when you feel sure you are busy with something worth your effort. That is the moment to look closer.

Four questions help with anything that grabs you. What is it in itself? What is it made of? What does it do in the world? How long will it last?

This is not a way to make life grey. Marcus also enjoyed small beautiful things, like bread that cracks open in the oven, or a ripe fig that splits.

In your life

A shop shows a new phone in soft light, with calm music. Step back and look at it as Marcus would: a thing made of metal and glass that will be old soon.

Try this. Pick one thing you want. Ask the four questions about it, one by one.

Keep a safe place inside

Nothing outside can force your mind. You can go back to it at any time.

People look for places to get away, Marcus says: a house in the country, the seaside, the mountains. You often want that too. But it is a common wish. At any time you can go back into your own mind. He says nowhere is quieter, because calm is nothing more than a well ordered mind.

He pictures that mind in strong images. A mind free from strong feelings is a fortress, and a person has nothing safer to run to. A clear spring keeps giving clean water even when someone curses it, and it washes away any dirt thrown in. And a rock on the coast stays firm while the waves keep breaking on it, and it calms the water around it.

It helps that Marcus believes nothing will come that you cannot bear. What happens to you is the kind of thing people can bear. And a block in your path can be put to use. The mind turns every hindrance (anything in your way) into help, he says, so that what stands in the road helps you along it.

What do you keep inside? Short, simple thoughts that you can return to. He keeps a few for any bad moment: things outside do not touch your soul (your mind), only your own opinion upsets you, and everything you see will soon change.

In your life

A long meeting ends badly. You cannot change what was said. You can decide what you will do next, and no one can take that choice from you.

Try this. Pick two short thoughts to repeat when you are upset. Marcus's are: outside things do not touch my soul, and everything I see will soon change.
