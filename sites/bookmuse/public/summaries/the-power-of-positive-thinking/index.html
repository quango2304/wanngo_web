The Power of Positive Thinking

A summary of the book by Norman Vincent Peale

7 lessons · 17 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

A mind you can refill

What you keep in your mind shapes how you feel and act, so push out fear and doubt, fill your mind with faith, and expect the best.

Norman Vincent Peale was a New York minister. He wrote this book in the early 1950s, and it sold more than two million hardcover copies. He says problems beat you only if you let them, and he gives simple habits to prevent it.

He built it on what he saw in his church: hundreds of people who changed their lives by changing their thinking. He calls it applied Christianity, so its tools are prayer and Bible lines. The lessons show them the way he does.

Attitude beats facts

How you think about a fact can beat you before you do anything about it.

A man of fifty-two came to Peale in despair. He said everything he had built was gone. He believed there was no hope, and he was too old to start again.

Peale asked him to write down what he still had. The man said there was no use, but he answered question after question. By the end, the page held seven things.

The man grinned. He had not thought of it that way. He said he might start again if he could just get some confidence. He did start again, but only after his viewpoint changed.

The psychiatrist Karl Menninger put it this way: “Attitudes are more important than facts.” A fact can be hard and still not be the whole story. Keep your eyes on what is against you, and those things grow into a power far bigger than they really have. So list what is for you.

Peale's own answer to doubt is faith: God is with you. Four of the book's ten rules for self-confidence:

  1. Picture yourself succeeding. Hold the picture and never let it fade.
  2. Say a faith line often. Peale's pick, ten times a day: “I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me.”
  3. Do not be awestruck by other people or copy them. Most are as scared as you are.
  4. Make a true estimate of your ability, then raise it 10 per cent. Stay modest, but respect yourself.

Empty the mind, then refill it

A mind cannot stay empty, so after you drain out fear and worry, fill it with faith.

Worry is a habit, Peale says. You did not have it at birth. You learned it, and a habit can be changed. He tells how one businessman did it with a one-day calendar.

This man always wondered if he had said or done the right thing. He went over each decision again and again. Peale calls him an expert at replaying the day. Each evening, before he left his office, he tore that day's page off the calendar by his door. He rolled it into a ball and dropped it in the wastebasket. Then he prayed. He thanked God for the day, said sorry for his mistakes and gave the day back.

Then he went out to enjoy the evening. His old mistakes slowly lost their hold on him.

The method has two parts. First, empty your mind. He suggests doing it before sleep, because the last minutes before you sleep are when the mind takes in ideas most easily. Picture your worries running out like water when you pull the stopper from a basin.

Second, refill it at once. An empty mind will not stay empty, and the old fears come back in. Fill it with thoughts of faith, hope and courage, and say them out loud. His example: “God is now filling my mind with courage, with peace, with calm assurance.” Do this half a dozen times a day.

To break a habit · The worry-breaking formula

Peale's ten points start with one idea: worry is a habit, and you beat it with the opposite habit, faith. Say that you believe, three times, before you get up. Never join a worry conversation, because a group that talks gloomily can infect everyone in it.

Slow down for easy power

Hurry and inner strain waste your power, and a calm pace gives you more of it.

A doctor told Peale about a man who worked from morning to night and never seemed worn out. The doctor said it is not hard work that drains a person. It is emotional upheaval.

Peale learned the cost of hurry himself. On a lecture trip he was rushed from two bookstore signings to a lunch, a meeting and a reception with several hundred people. Then a call told him to hurry down to dinner. He ran out of his room, out of breath, and stopped. He asked himself what all the rushing was for.

He went back, put his feet up and read Psalm 121 slowly aloud. He prayed for fifteen minutes. When he reached the dining room, the others were just finishing the first course. All he had missed was the soup. He felt in control of himself again.

Captain Eddie Rickenbacker, a very busy man, sat calmly in a rocking chair while a television filming ran late. Peale asked how. Easy power means calm, loose energy. It has three steps: go limp, drain the mind, and think spiritually, which means to turn your mind to God and pray.

In a meeting that turned bitter, one man took off his coat and lay down on a couch. He said he was starting to get mad, and it is hard to get mad lying down. Everyone laughed, and the tension broke. He had another trick for his temper: he kept his fingers open instead of making a fist, and he lowered his voice. It is hard to argue in a whisper, he said.
