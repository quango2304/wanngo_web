Say it, then show it

A boundary is a need you say out loud and then back up with action, and the short discomfort of doing it is worth the peace you get.

Nedra Glover Tawwab is a therapist who has worked on relationships for fourteen years. She found that many problems people bring, from burnout to family fights, come down to boundaries nobody set. This book shows what boundaries are, why we avoid them, and how to set them and keep them.

A boundary keeps you safe

A boundary is a need you protect, so you can feel safe and calm with other people.

Few people walk into therapy saying they have a boundary problem. They say they feel overwhelmed, or that they have no time for themselves. Tawwab has heard this for fourteen years.

Kim came to her two weeks after her honeymoon. She wanted to be the best friend, daughter, sister, coworker and now wife. For Kim, being the best meant always saying yes. Her week held a friend's move and a coworker's project on top of her own work. She asked how to do more without feeling so tired.

What does Kim's therapist tell her first?

Tawwab defines a boundary as an expectation or need that keeps you safe and at ease with other people. Without one, you start to notice signs:

  • You feel overwhelmed.
  • You resent people for asking for your help.
  • You avoid calls from people who might ask for something.
  • You feel burned out.
  • You have no time for yourself.

Look at the last sign. Many of her clients cannot find five minutes to meditate, yet they spend hours volunteering at their children's school. Tawwab says self-care begins with boundaries: you say no to one thing so you can say yes to your own well-being.

Porous, rigid, healthy

Boundaries can be too loose, too tight or healthy. Most trouble comes from the two extremes.

Tawwab sorts boundaries into three kinds. Porous boundaries are weak or poorly said out loud, like a fence full of holes. People with them overshare, find it hard to say no, and accept mistreatment. They end up drained and anxious. Kim was porous.

Rigid boundaries are walls. They come from a fear of being hurt, or from being taken advantage of before. The person allows no exceptions, even when one would be healthy.

Healthy boundaries sit between. You know your values, say no without apologizing, and hear no without taking it personally. You open up more to people who have earned your trust.

Amber shows the rigid side. She had lived in three homes by age ten. At thirty-two she was successful and fully independent, and alone. She ended things before anyone could get close, and she had stopped dating three years earlier. Her walls kept out the hurt. They also kept out the closeness.

The six types of boundaries

Boundaries come in six kinds: physical, sexual, intellectual, emotional, material and time.

Alex told people her life story within ten minutes of meeting them, and she expected the same back. When they did not, she thought something was wrong with them. Her friends began to pull away, and she did not know she was crossing their lines. At home, her father had told her everything, even about her mother's affair, and then told her how to think. He brushed off her feelings, so she stopped trusting them and asked her friends if they were right. To Alex, closeness meant telling all. She had never learned that how much you share is a boundary too.

Time is the one people struggle with most. If you have no time for something you want to do, your time boundary is weak.

Boundaries also change. If a hug from someone you used to hug starts to feel wrong, you may say so. Your needs move as your life does.
