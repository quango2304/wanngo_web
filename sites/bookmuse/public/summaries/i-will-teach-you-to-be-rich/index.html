I Will Teach You to Be Rich

A summary of the book by Ramit Sethi

6 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

A boring system for a rich life

You get rich by getting a few big things right once, then letting automatic, low-cost investing do the rest.

Ramit Sethi lost half of a $2,000 scholarship the first time he invested it, so he set out to learn about money. In 2004 he began a blog, and the book turns his advice into a six-week program. He says you do not need to be an expert, and you do not need to give up what you enjoy.

The advice is Sethi's, for readers in the United States. Limits and rules change, so check the current ones.

What you'll learn

Start now, not perfect

Getting started beats being an expert, so do the job in six small weeks and settle for 85 percent right.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Sally invests $200 a month from age 35 to 45, then stops and leaves it alone. Dan waits until 45, then invests $200 a month until 65. Whose account is worth more at 65?

Sethi says the most important thing you can do to get rich is to start early. His second big idea is the 85 Percent Solution: act and get it 85 percent right, instead of waiting to get it perfect. For example, when you choose how to pay off several cards, spend no more than five minutes deciding. Pick one way and start.

Many people never start, and they have reasons. He ran a "Save $1,000 in 30 Days Challenge." A few people said they did not earn enough to try. He asked: if you cannot save $1,000, what about $500, or $200? A small start beats a perfect plan.

The book turns this into a program you can finish in six weeks. Each week has a few small jobs.

Tom wants to start investing. He keeps reading about funds, hoping to find the very best one. What fits the 85 Percent Solution?

Try this. Start small on purpose. Set up automatic payments into an investing account, even if it is just $50 a month, as Sethi says.

Play offense with cards and banks

Credit and fees can cost you far more than small treats, so pay on time and ask for fees to be dropped.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Sethi went to buy a car in his twenties. The dealer said he had the best credit of anyone his age he had seen. Yet the dealer would not give him the low-interest loan. Why?

Credit means borrowing money and paying it back. Your credit score is one number, from 300 to 850, that tells lenders how risky you are. Higher is better, like an SAT score. The biggest part of it is paying on time: 35 percent. On a $200,000 home loan over 30 years, a top score means you pay $355,420 in all. A low one means $425,585. Sethi calls the few things that move this much money "Big Wins."

A credit card lets you buy now and pay later. If you pay the whole bill each month, it costs nothing. If you do not, the card charges interest, usually about 14 percent a year (called the APR), plus a late fee of around $35.

Five steps out of card debt

  1. Know your debt. Find out exactly what you owe.
  2. Pick what to pay first. Take five minutes at most.
  3. Ask for a lower APR. Call the card company.
  4. Find the money. Decide where each payment comes from.
  5. Start within a week. Pay more than the minimum.

Banks hide fees too. An overdraft fee is what a bank charges when you spend more than your account holds. Over breakfast, a man told Sethi about his girlfriend, a schoolteacher. She guessed she had paid $100 or $200 in these fees over the last year. It was $1,300. Avoiding those fees was a Big Win for her.

Sethi suggests you call and ask for the fee to be dropped. If the person on the phone says no, do not say "okay." Say what you want again, ask what else they can do, and say how long you have been a customer. He says about 85 percent of people get the fee back this way. The bank already paid to win you, and it does not want to lose you over a small fee. Sethi calls this playing offense: you act, instead of just accepting what companies charge.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Ben owes $3,000 on a card and has $100 spare each month. A friend tells him to move the debt to a new card with a low starting rate. What fits Sethi's advice?

Try this. Call your bank and ask it to confirm that your account has no fees and no minimum balance.

Fill the best accounts first

Put your money into accounts in a set order: the free match from your job first, then debt, then a Roth IRA.

Saving is not enough. Sethi's example: $1,000 left in a savings account for 30 years becomes $2,427, but prices rise, so it buys no more than before. Invested, it could be worth over $10,000. He uses a ladder of six rungs to show which account comes first. Finish one rung before you start the next.

A 401(k) is a retirement account through your job. An amount comes out of each paycheck before you see it. Many employers add money of their own, called a match. A Roth IRA is a retirement account you open yourself. You pay tax on what you put in, but not on its growth. Both are for the long run, and taking money out early can cost extra.

Start at 35 and earn 8 percent a year, and Sethi's example grows to just over $367,000 at 65 without a match. With the match it is more than $730,000. He says there is no better deal.

One reader told Sethi about a friend. She had put money into a Roth IRA for almost ten years, and her $3,000 had barely grown. She never chose what to buy, so it sat there like plain savings. It could have been worth more than $12,000. A Roth IRA is only an account. You must also pick investments for the money.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Sam's job matches his 401(k) up to 5 percent of pay. He owes $3,000 on a card at 18 percent, and he wants to open a Roth IRA. Where do his first extra dollars go?

Try this. Ask your HR office whether your company matches 401(k) contributions, and by how much.
