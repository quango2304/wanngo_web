The Pomodoro Technique

A summary of the book by Francesco Cirillo

6 lessons · 16 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Time as a tool, not an enemy

Work in 25-minute boxes with real breaks, and time stops being an enemy and becomes a tool you can measure and improve.

Francesco Cirillo had the idea as a university student in the late 1980s, with a kitchen timer shaped like a tomato, and built the method by 1992. Pomodoro is the Italian word for tomato. He taught it to individuals from 1998 and to teams a year later. The book builds the method one step at a time for one person, then shows how a team can use it.

Work in 25-minute boxes

Set a timer for 25 minutes and work on one task until it rings. Then rest. That is one Pomodoro.

Cirillo found the idea as a student. After his first-year exams he felt he was wasting his time. He watched his classmates and himself, and saw that too many interruptions and too little focus were the cause. So he asked himself if he could really study for even ten minutes. He wanted something to check him, and he found it in a kitchen timer shaped like a tomato.

One cloudy afternoon in September 1987, he started his first Pomodoro to finish one chapter of a book for an exam. He set it for only two minutes. When it rang, he felt a calm he could not explain. His mind had been drifting from one worry to the next, and now he felt he could do it. He wanted to set another. That calm helped him pass the exam. So a tiny timed step was enough to quiet his fear.

Later he found that 25 minutes works best. In his view, ten minutes is too short for a real result, while attention peaks in 20 to 45 minutes. You need only a kitchen timer and paper: a list of today's tasks and a master list of everything you have to do. He says a real timer beats an app, because winding it up is a promise to start. The method has only a few rules.

  1. Work 25 minutes on one task. When the timer rings, put an X next to the task.
  2. Rest 3 to 5 minutes. Stand up, drink water or stretch. Skip anything that takes hard thinking.
  3. After four Pomodoros, rest 15 to 30 minutes.
  4. Once it starts, it has to ring. It can't be split into halves. If something stops it, it is void: start a new one.
  5. When it rings, stop. No “just a few more minutes,” even if you could finish.
  6. If you finish early, review your work until it rings.

As you improve, use the first 3 to 5 minutes of a Pomodoro to repeat what you have learned so far, and the last 3 to 5 to review it. It works best aloud or with a partner.

Protect the box

Once a Pomodoro starts, nothing pulls you away. Every distraction waits until it rings.

Interruptions come from two places. Internal ones come from your own mind: the urge to get a snack, make a call, look something up or check e-mail. They often hide a fear of not finishing the task well. They can be more frequent than outside ones, such as an e-mail ping.

Take Mark, who is writing an article. In his second Pomodoro he suddenly wants to call a friend to ask when his favorite rock group plays next. He asks himself if it is urgent. It is not. So he makes a small mark, an apostrophe, next to the task. He adds the call to his master list with a deadline of the end of the week, and he keeps working. Ten minutes later he craves a pizza. Another apostrophe, a note on today's list, and back to work. In his third Pomodoro, eight more urges come, and he handles them all the same way.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

At the end of the day, Mark reads the urges he marked as urgent. How many were really urgent?

Some of his items moved to the master list, some were done in a longer break, and some were deleted. A thought can feel urgent and still wait 25 minutes.

The second kind comes from people. Protect the Pomodoro with three steps: inform, negotiate and call back. Say you are busy. Agree on a time to get back to them, from 25 minutes to tomorrow, by how urgent it is. Then really call. True emergencies are rare. Once people see you keep your word, they start to protect your Pomodoros too. Mark each interruption with a dash.

If you give in, the Pomodoro is void, even when it is about to ring. Mark it with an apostrophe or a dash, take a break and start a new one. The next Pomodoro will go better.

Lena is writing a report in a Pomodoro. A teammate walks over and asks for help with a spreadsheet. What should she do?

Try this. In your next Pomodoro, mark an apostrophe for each thought that pulls you away and a dash for each person. Write each one down, decide when to do it, and keep working until it rings.

Count, then estimate

Count the Pomodoros each task takes. That number shows your real effort and helps you plan better.

The book's first goal is simple: find out how much effort a task takes. You do it by counting finished Pomodoros, not hours. You do not even need to note start times. Cirillo calls the count of Pomodoros you actually finish the key to understanding the technique.

Mark wrote, polished and cut down an article called “How to Learn Music.” It took 10 Pomodoros. That seemed like too many. He wanted the same result in nine or fewer next time. So he asked himself what he could cut, which steps were really useful and how to arrange them better. One number gave him a question to improve with.

Next comes guessing. At the start of each day, write how many Pomodoros each task will take, and fill only the Pomodoros you have that day. The book's example is a day with eight. If a task runs past its guess, you guess again for the work left. That is a second guess. Two rules keep tasks the right size.

  1. “If it takes more than five to seven Pomodoros, break it down.” Small tasks are easier to guess.
  2. “If it takes less than one Pomodoro, add it up.” Join small tasks until they fill one.

Lucy's summary took four Pomodoros. She had guessed three, so she made a second guess of two, and needed only one of them. Over time, aim for fewer second guesses, then a smaller gap in the first. Do not pad every guess. A good sign is that guesses too low and guesses too high happen about equally often. Always guessing high does not make you better.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Omar's list says “plan the whole move to a new apartment.” He thinks it will take about twelve Pomodoros. What should he do?

Try this. At the start of tomorrow, write a guess in Pomodoros next to each task. At the end of the day, write the real count beside it and look at the gap.
