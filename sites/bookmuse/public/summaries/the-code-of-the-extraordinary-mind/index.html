The Code of the Extraordinary Mind

A summary of the book by Vishen Lakhiani

8 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Choose your own rules

Many of the rules you live by were handed to you, not chosen. Question them, pick your own beliefs and habits, and be happy now while a bold vision pulls you forward.

Vishen Lakhiani grew up in Malaysia and trained as a computer engineer. More than twelve of his start-up ideas failed before one took off. He says the ten laws come from what he learned from people he admires, such as Richard Branson and Arianna Huffington.

What you’ll learn

Rules you never chose

Much of what feels like how life works is a set of rules people invented and handed down. You can test each one.

Lakhiani says you live in two worlds. One is the physical world: rocks are hard and fire is hot. The other is the world of ideas and customs that people invented, such as marriage, money and religion. These differ between cultures, so they are not true for everyone. He calls this second world the culturescape.

Most of its rules reach you without a choice: through childhood lessons, people in charge, the wish to belong, “everyone does it” and our own doubts. Lakhiani coined a word for rules that have outlived their reason. A Brule is a rule you follow only because it was handed to you. The word is short for a rude name for a nonsense rule. He does not question the rules of physics and law.

At nine, he wanted a burger, but his Hindu family did not eat beef. His mother said, “Why don’t you go find out?” He read an encyclopedia and decided the old rule came from loving cows as pets. His family kept a dog, not a cow, so he argued the rule did not apply to them. His mother agreed, and he ate his first beef burger. One rule checked made him question the rest.

The Brule Test: five questions

  1. Does it trust people? A rule that assumes humans are bad is suspect.
  2. Does it break the Golden Rule? It says to treat others as you want to be treated. A rule that lifts some and lowers others is suspect.
  3. Did it come from my culture or religion? If it bothers you, you need not follow it.
  4. Did I choose it, or did it just spread to me? Ideas can pass from mind to mind unchecked.
  5. Does it serve my happiness? Put your happiness first.

Beliefs you can swap

Your beliefs shape how you feel and what you do. You can swap a belief for a better one.

Lakhiani calls this consciousness engineering. Your beliefs about the world are your models of reality, like a computer’s hardware. Your habits are your systems for living, like its software. He says bad beliefs create bad habits, so a swapped belief can change your life.

Hotel maids clean about fifteen rooms a day, yet none said they exercised regularly. Researchers told some of them that this work counts as real exercise. A month later, what had changed?

Your mind also makes up a meaning for every event. A date does not call back, and you decide you said something wrong. Maybe the person lost a phone. Lakhiani calls this the meaning-making machine, and many of its beliefs form in childhood.

Two questions to test a belief

  1. Is it true for everyone, or only in my culture? Needing food is true for all people. How to raise kids or worship is not.
  2. Is the meaning I gave this event really true? Ask yourself if you are sure.

Habits you can upgrade

Treat your habits like apps. Look for better ones, refresh them often, and set a floor you never drop below.

A repeated, improved way of doing something is what Lakhiani calls a system for living. How you eat, work, parent and exercise are all systems. Most of us never update ours. We fall into the do-do trap: we stay so busy that we never stop to ask how we do things, or why.

Three steps to upgrade a system

  1. Discover. Learn a better way from a book, class or friend. Try it for a month or two, then judge it.
  2. Refresh rate. This is how often you try something new. He tries a new exercise system every year.
  3. Set points. Pick a floor you will not drop below, and measure it.

Do not confuse a set point with a goal. A goal pulls you toward something new. A set point protects what you already have. His is that he can do fifty push-ups at any time, no excuses. He tests it once a week. If he cannot, he eats low-carb for a week and goes to the gym three times. He is usually back to fifty in a week.

After a slip, he aims for fifty and a little more, so fifty-one. When he reaches it, that becomes the new set point. Setting the bar much higher is just punishing. A small jump turns a slip from a failure into a challenge.
