Don’t Overthink It

A summary of the book by Anne Bogel

7 lessons · 17 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Spend your thinking where it counts

Overthinking eats the hours of your life. You can win them back with small shifts: aim for good enough, decide once, and say yes to what you love.

Overthinking is going round and round in thoughts that help no one. Anne Bogel is a writer and podcaster who has done it for most of her life. She wrote this book from her own experience, not from a lab. She says the habits she built over the years changed her days, and she shares them so you can try them.

Try it and see

There is seldom one right answer, only many good ones. Pick a good one and treat it as an experiment.

Anne and her husband Will took a long drive to the beach every summer. It takes about eleven hours, and they always did it in one day. They wondered if a hotel stop halfway would be better.

How long did they go back and forth on this one question?

Then Anne saw it for what it was: overthinking. This time she took four steps.

The whole family preferred the two-day drive. If they hadn't, it would still have been a success, because the goal was a result, not the best trip.

Perfectionism makes this hard. Anne once thought "right" meant "perfect". A perfect answer rarely exists, so you freeze. She also warns against saying "I'm just a chronic overthinker". That belief makes change impossible. She says her own calm is a skill she learned bit by bit. Past a point, more information only overwhelms you too.

Marta has wondered for a long time whether to shop at the Saturday market instead of the store on weekdays. She keeps weighing it. What should she do?

Try this. Pick one choice you keep turning over and take one small step. Anne's friend, stuck on a school choice, booked a school tour before the day was over and felt much better.

Let your values decide

When you know what matters to you, many choices stop being hard.

Anne's friend Ally flew thirty hours to spend four days in Thailand. Ally already travels for work and does not like to leave town more than she must. Anne asked if the choice was hard.

Tap an answer

What did Ally say?

Years ago Ally left an abusive marriage with help from friends. She then decided to help women who have been abused. Now, when a chance to do that comes, she says yes, even if it costs a lot.

A value is something you care about, such as community or showing up for people. Anne asked people she admired how they decide, and many said "values". One friend always picks the option closer to home, because she wants to know her neighbors. Anne and Will share that value, and it led them to a school a few blocks away. Their value of showing up lets them say yes to weddings and family reunions without long debate.

A value must show in your life. Anne's test is to ask, "What does my behavior indicate I value?" A calendar helps. Will added a purple arts calendar, so the family can see at a glance if they go to the arts events they care about. One such event was a library reopening they nearly skipped. They went, because they value showing up.

Anne used this on her own dilemma. She hated flying and had put off a trip to Scotland for months. When she asked what she valued, the answer was clear: new experiences with Will, time with friends there, and no debt, because points paid for the flight. A mentor had told her that a choice driven by fear is not a good choice. They went.

Tap an answer

Priya says she values being there for her daughter. A work event falls on the night of her daughter's recital, and she can't decide. What should she do?

Try this. When you feel stuck, ask yourself: "Do I hold a value that can inform this decision?" If you are unsure what you value, look at where your time, money, energy and attention go.

Decide once

Every small choice uses up mental energy, so decide once and let a rule decide after that.

Anne's early morning barre class (a workout of small, slow moves) ended with a cooldown of stretching. The teacher said anyone could leave five minutes early. Each time, Anne lay there wondering whether to go. Then she told herself that she had chosen to come, so she should simply stay. The pressure vanished.

Small choices add up. Anne says the more you decide in a day, the worse your later choices get. This is decision fatigue: being so worn out by choosing that you can't choose well. Her answer is a rule you set once. She calls it a default setting, not handcuffs.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Anne was lost among endless bedding choices online. A friend named just one store. How long did she then take to choose?

The same idea works for food. Anne eats nearly the same breakfast and lunch on most days. A signature dish for guests means you never wonder what to serve. If you love choosing clothes or cooking, keep that joy. Use rules where a choice wears you out.

Nia keeps asking herself when to phone her mother, and the question nags her. What should she do?

Try this. Pair a new habit with one you already do. Anne's example: when she brushes her teeth, she takes two extra minutes to stretch.
