A long life with something to do

A long, happy life starts with a reason to get up each morning, kept alive by staying busy, close to friends, light on food, gentle in movement and calm in pace.

Héctor García, born in Spain and living in Japan, and Francesc Miralles, an author from Barcelona, went to Ogimi, a village on the island of Okinawa with the highest life expectancy in the world. In one week they held a hundred interviews with the oldest people there. All of them seemed to share an ikigai, a word for the reason you get up in the morning.

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

A reason to get up

Your ikigai is your reason to get up in the morning, and people who keep one stay active for as long as they can.

Ikigai is a Japanese word. It roughly means the happiness of always being busy. People on Okinawa, an island where far more people live past 100 than almost anywhere, say it is the reason you get up in the morning. In the Japanese view, everyone has one. Some people have found theirs, and others are still looking.

Blue Zones are places where people live the longest. The book names five, from Okinawa to a Greek island and a peninsula in Costa Rica. Some studies suggest that a clear ikigai and a close community matter as much as the famous Japanese diet, or more.

Many Japanese people never really retire. They go on with what they love as long as their health allows. Japanese has no word for retiring in the sense of leaving work for good, as English does. The reporter Dan Buettner says that purpose matters so much there that our idea of retirement does not exist.

Take Hayao Miyazaki, who directs the animated films of Studio Ghibli. In 2013 he announced that he would retire. The day after his retirement, he went to the studio and sat down to draw. A year later he said he would make no more feature films, but would draw until the day he died. So leaving his job did not mean leaving what gave his days meaning.

Meaning is found, not made

You do not invent the meaning of your life. You find it, and feeling empty is a sign that you have not found it yet.

Viktor Frankl ran a clinic in Vienna and started logotherapy, a kind of therapy that helps people find reasons to live. The authors share one of its ideas: we do not make up the meaning of our life, we discover it. Your reason for being can change many times over the years.

Frankl did not treat an empty feeling as an illness. He saw it as a push to change. A person feels empty, frustrated or anxious. Frankl helps them see that this is a wish for a meaningful life. Then they look for a purpose and decide freely whether to accept it.

A diplomat from the United States came to Frankl. He hated his job. For years, his analyst had read this as a problem with his father and urged him to make peace with him. Frankl showed him in a few sessions that he simply wanted a different career. Five years later the man worked in another field and was happy. So his frustration was not an illness. It was a sign that he needed a new purpose.

Frankl also described Sunday neurosis, a heavy feeling that comes when the duties of the workweek stop. The person sees how empty he feels inside. The way out is a reason to get out of bed, an ikigai.

Frankl lived this idea. He arrived at the Auschwitz camp with a manuscript of his theories, and it was taken from him. He felt he had to write it all again, and he jotted down key words on any scrap of paper he found. That goal gave his life meaning there. Psychiatrists noted that the prisoners likeliest to survive were those with things they wanted to do after getting out.

Dev has a good job and good friends. Every Sunday evening, when the week's duties are over, a heavy, empty feeling comes. How would Frankl read it?

Try this. Picture yourself looking back on your life from the end. Frankl used this with a mother in deep despair, and it helped her see that her life already had meaning.

Lose yourself in the doing

Flow is being so absorbed in a task that time disappears, and you reach it with a stretch, a clear aim and one task at a time.

You start cooking and hours pass. You read all afternoon and notice the sunset. The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi called this state flow, being so involved in something that nothing else seems to matter. He found it is the same at every age and in every culture, in New York and in Okinawa. Asking when you lose track of time can help you find your ikigai.

Choose a task that stretches you a little. If the task is too easy, you get bored. If it is too hard, you give up and feel frustrated. Someone who reads a physics book for experts will quit within minutes. Someone who already knows everything in a book is bored at once.

Know what you are aiming for. Ask a clear question before you start, such as how many words you will write today. Then let the goal fade into the background. The book pictures a writer with three months to finish a novel. Each morning she thinks, I have to write that novel, and each evening she has written nothing. All it would take is the first word. So a clear aim helps, but staring at it blocks you.

Do one thing at a time. This may be the most important step. When we multitask, we switch quickly between tasks, and our energy goes into the switching. The Stanford researcher Clifford Nass asked students to count red arrows among blue ones. The heavy multitaskers did worse, because their brains were trained to notice every signal.

Ana plays the same easy piece on the piano every day and feels bored. She wants to find flow. What fits?

Try this. Use the Pomodoro Technique. Set a kitchen timer for 25 minutes and work on a single task until it rings. Then rest for 5 minutes.
