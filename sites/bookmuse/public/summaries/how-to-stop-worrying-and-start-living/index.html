How to Stop Worrying And Start Living

A summary of the book by Dale Carnegie

6 lessons · 17 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Shut the doors on yesterday and tomorrow

Worry wears down your body and clouds your mind. You can break the habit by living one day at a time, facing the worst with clear facts, and choosing calm action.

At the New York Public Library, Dale Carnegie found 22 books on worry beside 189 on worms. So he wrote his own. He read what thinkers said about worry and interviewed scores of well-known people. He also ran a five-year laboratory in his adult classes, where students tried his rules and reported what happened.

The book has ten parts and many small rules. Six ideas carry most of it. Each lesson below takes about two minutes.

Live in day-tight compartments

Worry wears you down, so shut the iron doors on yesterday and tomorrow and put all your strength into today.

Worry is not harmless. Dr. Alexis Carrel, a Nobel prizewinner, said: “Business men who do not know how to fight worry die young.” A Mayo Clinic study of 15,000 stomach patients found that four in five had no physical cause. Carnegie says fear, worry and hate were largely to blame.

In the spring of 1871, young medical student William Osler was worried about his final exams and how to earn a living. Then he read twenty-one words by Thomas Carlyle urging him to focus on the task lying clearly at hand instead of straining at what waits dimly far away. The book says those words made him the best-known doctor of his day.

Forty-two years later, Osler spoke to students at Yale. He asked them to picture an ocean liner whose captain presses a switch to shut iron watertight bulkheads. Shut out the dead yesterdays and unborn tomorrows the same way, he said, and live in day-tight compartments.

Army clerk Ted Bengermino learned the same lesson in April 1945. He kept records of soldiers killed, missing or in hospital. He lost thirty-four pounds from worry. An Army doctor told him to think of his life as an hourglass. The grains of sand pass slowly through a narrow neck, and no one can push more than one through at a time. Tasks are the same. Bengermino kept saying “One grain of sand at a time,” and it carried him through the war and later through his office job.

To break a habit · Seal off yesterday and tomorrow

Living one day at a time does not forbid careful planning. It means planning with no anxiety about tomorrow, and no brooding over yesterday.

The magic formula for crises

Part One, Rule 2: name the worst that can happen, accept it mentally, and calmly improve on it.

1
Ask the worst. Name the worst realistic outcome if you cannot fix the mess.
2
Accept it mentally. Make peace with that worst case in your head if you have to.
3
Improve on the worst. Spend your calm energy making that outcome better.

Young engineer Willis H. Carrier installed a new gas-cleaning system at a glass plant in Crystal City, Missouri. It failed its guarantee, putting his job and twenty thousand dollars of company money at risk. Sick with worry and unable to sleep, he applied those three steps: he accepted losing his job and charging the loss to research, calmed down, ran tests, spent five thousand dollars on extra gear, and earned a fifteen-thousand-dollar profit.

The same formula rescued a New York oil dealer during wartime rationing. His drivers cheated deliveries, and a crook posing as a government inspector demanded five thousand dollars to keep quiet. After three days without food or sleep, he asked what the worst was. It was losing his business, and he could live with that. His mind cleared, and he went to his lawyer. The lawyer sent him to the District Attorney, who said the “inspector” was a crook wanted by the police.

In your life

Dreading an ugly call with a landlord or client? Write down the worst outcome, accept it for a moment so your pulse drops, and list the first practical step you can take.

Get the facts, then decide

Half of all worry comes from trying to decide before you have the facts. Write the facts down, decide, and act.

1
Get the facts. Collect them fairly. Pretend you are gathering them for someone else, or arguing the other side as a lawyer.
2
Analyze the facts. Write them down, both sides, including every fact you do not like.
3
Decide and act. Choose a path, start at once, and drop your anxiety about the result.
4
Answer four work questions. Write the problem, its cause, all possible fixes, and the best one.

Columbia dean Herbert E. Hawkes helped two hundred thousand students with worry and said confusion is its chief cause. In 1942 in Shanghai, insurance manager Galen Litchfield left a block of securities worth $750,000 off the list he gave a Japanese admiral. When the admiral found out, Litchfield feared the Bridge House, a torture chamber where friends had died after ten days of questioning and torture. That Sunday he typed what he was worried about and four things he could do about it, with the likely result of each. He chose to go to work Monday as usual and felt immensely relieved. The admiral said nothing, and six weeks later he went back to Tokyo.

Cutting visits after the second call freed the half of Bettger's day he had been wasting and nearly doubled what each call paid him. Publisher Leon Shimkin made his staff write answers to four questions before any meeting. Three-quarters of problems then solved themselves.
