Why more information is not more truth

Information does not mainly show us the truth. It connects people into networks, so every network needs ways to fix its own mistakes, and AI is the first member of those networks that is not human.

Yuval Noah Harari is a historian. He asks why humans are so good at gaining power and so bad at using it wisely. His answer is not human nature. It is the way our information networks work. You will see how stories, paperwork, science, democracy and dictatorship each handle the truth, and what changes when AI joins them.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Information connects

Information does not mainly show us the truth. It connects people into networks.

Harari starts with a puzzle. We named our species the wise human. Yet we face ecological collapse and keep building technology that may escape our control. If we know so much, why are we so self-destructive?

His answer is about information. Many people hold what he calls the naive view. It says more information leads to more truth, and truth leads to power and wisdom. Mistakes are fixed with even more information.

Harari agrees the naive view is sometimes right. But a phone today holds more than the famous library of Alexandria did, and we still damage the planet and build ever more powerful weapons. More information did not bring wisdom.

Think about astrology. It is not accurate, but it connects. Roman emperors asked astrologers for advice, and in 2021 the world astrology market was worth $12.8 billion. Music works the same way. A tune is not true or false, yet it makes people march or sing together.

So Harari says information does not mainly inform us. It puts things in formation, the way a marching song lines up soldiers. A horoscope does the same for lovers. Information sometimes shows reality and sometimes not. It always connects.

Truth takes special effort, and most information makes no such effort. When information grows in amount and speed, cheap content can swamp the rare, truthful kind. Harari also rejects the opposite view, that information is only a weapon and power is all that is real.

Stories link strangers

People can cooperate in huge numbers because strangers can share the same story.

A group of chimpanzees usually has 20 to 60 members. People can build close bonds with only a few hundred others. Yet the Catholic Church has about 1.4 billion members, and global trade links about 8 billion people. How?

About seventy thousand years ago, Sapiens (our own species) bands began to tell and believe made-up stories. Strangers no longer had to know each other. They only had to know the same story. A story is like one socket with endless outlets, and anyone can plug in. In a fight, five hundred Sapiens would easily beat fifty Neanderthals, who lived in small bands that rarely worked together.

Even a famous leader works this way. People follow a carefully made story about the leader, not the person. Stalin once told his son Vasily that he was not Stalin, and neither was Stalin himself. Stalin was the figure in the newspapers and portraits. People follow that made story, not the man.

Stories can create a third kind of reality. A stone exists whether we know about it or not. Pain exists in one mind. But money, laws, gods and nations exist between many minds, in the stories people tell each other. Harari calls these intersubjective, which just means shared between minds. If people stop telling the story, they disappear.

A rich traveler who crashes on a desert island with a suitcase of banknotes can buy nothing. Cut off from the network, the money stops working.

In your life

Think of a brand you like. What you feel about it comes from a story told again and again, not only from the product.

Truth or order?

Every network has to find the truth and keep order, and the two often pull apart.

Power needs both. To build an atom bomb you need to know physics. You also need many people to work together. The Manhattan Project directly employed about 130,000.

Order is easier to build with fiction. Fiction can be simple, but the truth is often complicated. Fiction can flatter, but the truth can hurt. A politician who tells only proud stories about her nation may win more votes than one who talks about its dark days. So networks often protect the stories that keep order. Nazi Germany trained leading experts in chemistry and rocket science, but it did not let anyone question its racist myths.

Paperwork has the same problem. Stories are easy to remember, but tax records are dull lists, so people invented written documents. A clay tablet from ancient Ur lists the sheep and goats received in one month. Its total says 896, yet the entries add up to 898. Documents bring order, but they are not always true.

They also create a new problem: finding the right one. The answer was bureaucracy, which means rule by writing desk. Each document goes into a drawer, and the world must fit the drawers. If your case fits none, you must change to fit the form. Each drawer also has its own narrow goal, so a department can harm what lies outside it.

In your life

You fill in an online form, and none of the choices fits your situation. You pick the closest one, and from then on the system treats you as that choice.

Still, bureaucracy is not the enemy. In 1854 cholera killed hundreds of people in London. John Snow listed each patient, home and water source. His list pointed to the Broad Street pump, officials disabled it, and the outbreak ended. It was dull data work, and it saved lives.

So Harari sees no march of progress. He sees a tightrope walk between truth and order. Faster technology only makes the balance more urgent.
