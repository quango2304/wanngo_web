Freedom before happiness

Libertarians say you own yourself, so what you earn is yours and only a thin state is fair. This part tests how far that idea can go.

This is Part 4 of 10 in Justice, our own course on moral and political philosophy. Parts 2 and 3 judged laws by happiness. This part starts from freedom as a right instead.

A state that does little

A libertarian says each person has a right to use what they own as they choose, so the state should do very little.

Harrow Bay has an old sea wall. Grown adults like to walk along the top. Last summer two of them fell and broke bones. Now the council wants to ban the walk and fine anyone caught. Is that a good law?

A plain utilitarian, like Bentham in Part 2, judges laws by whether they make people happiest overall. That thinker would count the gains and losses. Fewer broken bones may outweigh a lost pastime, so the ban could pass. A libertarian answers in a different way. Their test is not happiness. It is a right. Each person has a right to freedom: to use their own things as they like, while letting others do the same. So the state should do little: it may enforce contracts, stop theft and keep the peace.

So the sea wall ban is the first refusal. The walkers are adults and the risk is theirs. Protecting people from themselves is called paternalism. A ban on a card game, because most of the town finds it a waste of time, is the second refusal. A charge on rich households to pay for a new pool is the third. Moving money from some people to others is called redistribution.

A libertarian does not say that helping others is bad. They say help must be a free gift, never a rule backed by fines. A contract is a promise that the law will enforce, and that is why a thin state still has that job.

This view does not sit neatly on one side of politics. Someone who wants low taxes may still want laws about morals. Someone who wants a big welfare system may still want adults free in private matters. Economists such as Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman argued for it in the 1960s. The philosopher Robert Nozick gave it a full philosophical defense in 1974.

In your life

Next time you hear "there ought to be a law", ask whose risk it is: the person's own, or someone else's.

Fair means fairly gained

For Nozick, a spread of wealth is just if it began fairly and every step after was a free choice.

Is it unfair that some people are far richer than others? Many say yes. Nozick says the numbers alone cannot tell you. You must ask how the money got there.

Nozick tested the idea with a famous case. Wilt Chamberlain was a basketball star whose pay in the early 1970s reached $200,000 a season, a lot of money then. Nozick says: first set up any split of money you think is fair. Then imagine fans paying the star directly, a little at each game. Try it.

Now ask who has a complaint. The fans paid because they wanted to watch. Someone who stayed home paid nothing and lost nothing. The star played for the pay he was offered. Every step was a free choice. Nozick says that if each step was fair, the end is fair too, however uneven. Keeping the first split has a cost. The state must undo free choices again and again, and undoing them means taking what the star earned.

So Nozick replaces patterns with two questions. Were the things you started with fairly yours? And did you gain by free exchange, or by a gift freely given? If both answers are yes, what you hold is rightfully yours, however unequal the result.

The first question has teeth. Suppose a neighbor's fortune began with land his grandfather took from another family by force. That start was not fair. Nozick says a case can be made for putting the wrong right, by reparations, taxation or other means. But the aim is to repair a past wrong. It is not to make people equal. He admits it is hard to know how clean any start was.

Taxing work, owning people

If you own yourself, you own your work, so taking its pay treats you as partly owned by someone else.

Nozick goes further. He says taxing what you earn from work is as bad as forcing you to work for the state. The wrong is not only about money. It is about who decides what your time is for.

Take Mira, a baker who sells loaves for coins. Say the town takes a quarter of her pay. Now say it ordered her instead to bake for the town for a quarter of her hours, unpaid. For Nozick, those are two ways to take the same share of her work. In both, other people decide that part of her effort serves their goals, and not hers.

The idea is called self-ownership: each person belongs to themselves. So no one may order me to work, or I would be in their hands. If I own my work, I must own what it earns, or someone else would own my work, and so a part of me. A tax does not reach all of a person. Nozick says it reaches a share: the share that pays for things beyond the thin state.

The idea also gives one reason for Part 1's runaway trolley puzzle. Many people would not push a stranger off a bridge to stop the trolley, even to save five lives. His life is his own, and not ours to spend.

Libertarians have an answer to most of these objections. Some say, "You can work less and pay less tax." The libertarian asks why the state should push you into that choice at all. Others say, "The poor need the money more." Need is a reason to ask, not to take. A friend may need your help moving house more than you need your day off. That is a reason to offer, and it gives her no right to order you. A third says, "You voted for the law." A vote does not settle it: if a majority could take your pay, it could take your speech too.

One reply is harder. Your talents and your luck are not your doing, so you cannot claim all the credit for your pay. The libertarian turns it round. If your talents are not yours, then you do not own yourself. If you do not, who does? Whether that settles it is open, and a later part comes back to it.
