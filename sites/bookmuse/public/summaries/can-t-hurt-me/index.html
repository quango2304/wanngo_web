Can't Hurt Me

A summary of the book by David Goggins

7 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

The mind quits before the body

Most people quit long before their limit, and you can move that limit by telling yourself the truth, doing hard things on purpose and learning from every failure.

David Goggins grew up poor, with an abusive father and with racism at school. Years later he weighed almost 300 pounds and sprayed pest poison in restaurants at night. Then he became a Navy SEAL, one of the Navy's special forces, and ran some of the hardest races in the world. His book ends ten chapters with a challenge for you.

He says motivation does not last: "Motivation changes exactly nobody." What lasts is a toughened mind. His title is a promise: keep training your mind and you reach a point where "not even pain can hurt you." He also guesses that most people live at about 40 percent of what they could do.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Tell yourself the truth

You cannot fix what you will not name, so tell yourself the plain truth.

In high school, Goggins cheated for years. He copied homework and tests. Then two warnings came in one school year. He scored 20 out of 99 on the ASVAB, the military's entry test, when the Air Force minimum was 36. And a letter said he had missed over a quarter of the year, had a D average, and would not graduate unless he improved.

That night he shaved at the bathroom mirror and talked to the person in the glass. He used no soft words. He said he read poorly, had never tried hard at anything but basketball, and that nobody was coming to save him. He says the only way to change is to be real with yourself.

It became a nightly ritual. He shaved, got loud, and stuck goals on the mirror with sticky notes. He calls it the Accountability Mirror. The first notes were small chores: make your bed, pull up your pants, wash all the dishes.

He copied his school books by hand, page after page, then did it again. In six months his reading went from a fourth grade level to a high school senior's, and on his third try he passed the ASVAB. So honest words at a mirror, plus hard work, did what years of cheating never did.

Goggins asks for the same honesty about why you are stuck. In his view, racism and a hard past can be real, and still the thing that stops you is giving up. Name the real reasons, and the odds become fuel. He calls the talks you have with yourself the most important ones of your life.

Walk toward discomfort

Comfort keeps you small, so do something hard on purpose, and do it again.

After the Air Force, Goggins weighed nearly 300 pounds. He took a night job killing pests in restaurants because it paid 35 percent more and came with a free truck. He calls that the path of least resistance: the easy road handed to you. Then he saw a TV show on SEAL training and could not stop thinking about it.

A recruiter weighed him at 297 pounds. A chart on the wall showed the Navy's limit for his height: 191. He had under three months to drop 106 pounds.

He went out to run and lasted 400 yards. He sat down, then went home and cried over a milkshake. Then he watched the scene in the film Rocky where the fighter gets up again in round 14. He went back out and ran a full mile. He saw that not every limit is real, and that he had a habit of giving up too soon.

After that, every day was hard on purpose. From 4:30 in the morning he rode a stationary bike for two hours while he studied for the test, swam for two hours, did a gym circuit, and rode again. He calls this walking the path of most resistance.

One day his goal was twelve pull-ups, and he quit after eleven. At home he could not stop thinking about it. He drove back and did his whole pull-up workout again. He says there is suffering either way: the hurt in the moment, or the doubt afterward. He chose the hurt. In the end he lost over 100 pounds in less than three months.

Maya runs every day and loves it. A friend says strength work would help her, but she finds it dull. What fits this lesson?

Try this. List the things you dislike but know are good for you. Pick one, do it today, and do it again tomorrow. Small counts: make your bed, wash the dishes, get up early for a short run. When it gets easy, raise it, from two miles to five.

A calloused mind

A hard past can toughen your mind the way weights toughen your hands, if you choose to see it that way.

In SEAL training, Goggins faced an underwater knot drill with a hurt right knee. He had to tread water with one leg, then dive and tie knots on the pool floor. The instructor splashed water in his face and bet he could not tie the last three on a single breath.

Down there, his lungs burned and stars gathered at the edge of his eyes. Quitting looked sane. Then he saw the instructor smiling at his pain, and a new thought came. He had always seen his life, the abuse and the bullying, as something done to him. Now he saw it as a training ground.

He smiled back. The pain eased. He tied the last knot slowly and came up. The instructor was gasping, and Goggins felt relaxed. So the past that once made him feel like a victim carried him through.

He explains it with hands. In his first days in a gym, his palms tore on the steel bars. After thousands of reps, they grew a thick callus. A mind works the same way, and he calls the result a calloused mind. But you choose where the callus forms. See yourself as a victim, and it turns into resentment. Stay open, and it turns into strength.

A calloused mind cannot heal a broken bone, though. That same day he learned his kneecap was fractured, and he was sent home to heal. Later he saw that a calloused mind also means facing his past. For years he hid from it. He chose to accept that his father was part of him, and to use it as fuel.

Back to the title: if you accept what happened, endure it, and keep toughening your mind, you reach a point where "not even pain can hurt you."
