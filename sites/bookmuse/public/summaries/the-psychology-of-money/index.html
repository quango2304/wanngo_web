The Psychology of Money

A summary of the book by Morgan Housel

8 lessons · 20 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Money is a people problem

How you behave with money matters much more than how much you know about it.

Morgan Housel, a writer on money, opens with two men. Ronald Read fixed cars and swept floors, and he died with more than $8 million. Richard Fuscone had a Harvard degree and a top job at Merrill Lynch, a large Wall Street firm, and he went bankrupt. Read was patient. Fuscone was greedy. The book is twenty short chapters, each one a different idea about how people really act with money.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways with all twenty chapters in one line each.

No one is crazy

Everyone learned about money from their own life, so a choice that looks crazy to you can make sense to someone else.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Two groups grew up in America. For the first, born in 1950, the stock market went nowhere in their teens and 20s. For the second, born in 1970, it grew almost ten times. Later in life, which group put more of its money into stocks?

What you lived through feels more real than what you read. Your own life is a tiny part of all that has happened, yet it shapes most of how you think money works. So two smart people can disagree about risk, saving or recessions, the times when the economy shrinks, and neither is crazy.

Inflation, the rise in prices over time, works the same way. If you were born in America in the 1960s, prices more than tripled in your teens and 20s. Someone born in 1990 may never have thought about it, because it has stayed so low. So the same economy teaches each group a different lesson.

Look at lottery tickets. The lowest-income households spend about $412 a year on them, four times what the richest households spend. Yet 40% of Americans cannot find $400 in an emergency. That looks crazy. But picture their view: a ticket is the only time they can hold a real dream of the life others take for granted. Buying tickets is still a bad idea. It just makes sense from where they stand.

Saving for retirement is also new. The 401(k), a company retirement plan, did not exist until 1978, and the Roth IRA, a personal one, came in 1998. So we are not crazy. We are beginners.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Your uncle lost most of his savings in a crash when he was young. Now he refuses to buy any stocks, and you think that is silly. What is the best way to see it?

In your life

You and a friend argue about whether to buy stocks. Before you decide who is right, ask what each of you saw happen to money growing up.

Try this. When someone's money choice looks crazy, say to yourself: "That looks crazy, but I can see why they do it." Then ask what they have lived through.

Luck and risk are twins

Luck and risk are the same force pushing in opposite ways, so success is never as great, and failure never as awful, as it looks.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

In 1968 there were about 303 million teenagers in the world. How many of them went to a school that owned a computer like the one Bill Gates used?

Bill Gates went to Lakeside School near Seattle. A teacher there got the mothers' club to use about $3,000 from its sale of used goods to rent a computer in 1968. Gates was 13. Of 303 million teenagers in the world, about 100,000 lived near Seattle and about 300 went to Lakeside. Gates is very smart and works hard. He also said that without Lakeside there would have been no Microsoft.

Now take his best friend Kent Evans. Gates called him the best student in the class. They planned their futures together. Evans died in a climbing accident before he finished high school. The odds of that were also about one in a million. So one friend got a rare lucky break and the other a rare terrible one, and neither chose it.

We rarely admit this. When someone fails, we blame their choices, but we blame risk for our own failures. The line between bold and reckless is thin, and often clear only afterward. The same act looks bold when it works and reckless when it does not.

There are two ways to cope. Be careful whom you praise and whom you look down on. And study broad patterns that many people share, not the lives of a few extreme winners, because extreme results had the most luck or risk in them.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Two founders in the same business worked equally hard. One company became huge and the other closed. What is the best conclusion?

Try this. Before you copy a famous winner or blame someone for failing, ask how much was luck or risk. The author wrote to his son that not all success is hard work and not all poverty is laziness.

Know when you have enough

The hardest money skill is making your goal stop moving, so you never risk what you have for what you do not need.

Rajat Gupta lost both parents as a teenager in Kolkata. By his mid 40s he ran McKinsey, a top consulting firm, and by 2008 he was reportedly worth $100 million. Yet he wanted to be a billionaire. In 2008 he sat on the board of Goldman Sachs, a large bank, and learned that Warren Buffett planned to put $5 billion into the bank. Sixteen seconds after learning it, he called the manager of a hedge fund, a private fund for rich investors, who bought 175,000 shares before the news came out. Gupta and the fund manager both went to prison. So reaching for more cost a man who had more than enough.

Bernie Madoff had a real, very profitable business before his fraud. A former staff member said it earned $25 million to $50 million a year. Gupta and Madoff each already had wealth, status and freedom, and threw it away for more. Buffett said it makes no sense to risk what matters to you for something that does not matter. He said it of a hedge fund that lost everything in 1998.

Comparison is the trap. A rookie on $500,000 a year is rich, but next to Trout he feels broke.

Enough is not too little. Some things are never worth the risk: your reputation, your freedom, your family and friends, being loved and your happiness.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

A manager already earns more than she needs. A contact offers a secret tip that could add a million dollars but could cost her job if it goes wrong. What does the book say?

Try this. When your pay rises, keep your lifestyle where it is and save the raise. The author and his wife have done this since their 20s, and they say it stopped their goal from moving.
