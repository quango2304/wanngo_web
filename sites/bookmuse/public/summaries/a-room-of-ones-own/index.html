A Room of One's Own

A summary of the book by Virginia Woolf

What a writer needs besides talent

A woman needs money of her own and a private room that locks if she is to write fiction, because a free mind needs both, and for centuries women had neither.

Virginia Woolf wrote this essay from two talks she gave in October 1928 to student societies at Newnham and Girton colleges. She hands the talk to a made-up speaker, whom she asks us to call Mary Beton, and follows her thoughts over two days. A plain dinner, an angry professor and an imagined sister of Shakespeare show how money and freedom shape a mind.

Money and a lock on the door

The made-up speaker once earned her living with odd jobs. She reported a donkey show for a newspaper, addressed envelopes and read to old ladies.

The worst part was not the work. It was the fear and bitterness it bred. She had to flatter people to keep it, and she felt her one small gift was dying. Then an aunt died after a fall from her horse in Bombay and left her five hundred pounds a year for ever. The news came about the time women won the vote. Of the two, the money seemed to her far more important.

Nobody could take that money away. So she no longer had to flatter anyone, and she no longer needed to hate anyone. Her fear and bitterness turned slowly into pity, and in a year or two even the pity went. In its place came the "freedom to think of things in themselves": to ask of a building or a book only whether she liked it.

At the end, Woolf says in her own voice what a woman needs to write fiction or poetry: an income of five hundred pounds a year and a private room that locks. Her reason: freedom of mind depends on material things, and good writing depends on freedom of mind. She quotes a professor of literature who counted the twelve great English poets of the last hundred years or so. Nine went to university, and of the other three only Keats, who died young, was not fairly well off.

Where the money went

Good thinking needs comfort, and for centuries the money went to men's colleges while women could not earn, own or leave any of it.

Woolf's speaker walks in a made-up university town called Oxbridge. A college guard, a beadle, waves her off the grass, because only Fellows and Scholars, the college's teachers and top students, may walk there. A man at the library door turns her away too. Women may enter only with a Fellow or a letter of introduction.

Lunch at one of the colleges is a feast. That evening she has dinner at Fernham, a made-up college for women, and it is plain gravy soup, beef, prunes and custard, then biscuits and cheese, with water. She says a poor dinner is bad for thinking, loving and sleeping.

Why the gap? She pictures the old colleges built over centuries with gold from kings and nobles, and later from merchants who left money in their wills. The women's college began about 1860. Her friend there, who teaches science, tells how it took meetings, letters and a long struggle to raise thirty thousand pounds, and how the comforts would have to wait.

At first the speaker blames the women before them for leaving no money. Then she sees it is no use. It was impossible for them to earn money, and the law would not let a wife keep what she earned. The speaker says it was only in the last forty-eight years that such a woman had a penny of her own.

Why the professors were angry

The speaker thinks men's angry opinions of women show their need to feel superior more than anything about women, so they cannot settle what women are.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

The speaker spends a morning in the British Museum library reading what men have written about women. What does she bring home in her notebook?

The library has shelves of books about women by professors, clergymen and novelists. Women, she notes, do not write such books about men. And the opinions clash. Napoleon thought women could not be educated, and Dr Johnson thought the opposite.

In her notes she finds a drawing she made without thinking: an angry professor writing a book on the inferiority of women. He is imagined, and she calls him Professor von X. She drew him in anger, and she finds anger under those books too. She asks why men with so much power are angry.

Her answer is about confidence. Life is hard, and everyone needs to believe in themselves. A quick way to get that is to think others are beneath you. So a man who must rule may need half the human race to be beneath him. When he stresses women's inferiority, she thinks, he is guarding his own superiority.

She puts it as a picture. For ages women have been looking-glasses that show a man at "twice its natural size". That is why a woman's honest criticism stings more. If she tells the truth, the figure in the mirror shrinks.
