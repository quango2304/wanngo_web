Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order

A summary of the book by Ray Dalio

8 lessons · 22 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

The same story, again and again

Empires, their money and their markets rise and fall in one repeating Big Cycle, so the smart move is to learn its pattern and protect yourself from the worst case.

Ray Dalio runs an investment firm and has studied the world's economies for about 50 years. He wrote this book in 2021, after studying how the Dutch, British, American and Chinese empires rose and fell. He found one pattern, driven by three cycles: debt, order at home and order between nations. When all three turn bad, a country declines. Every "now" in this summary means 2021.

Rise, top and decline

Every great power follows the same path: it rises, it reaches a top, then it declines, and the same causes drive each step.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Dalio studied the great empires of the last 500 years. How long did a typical one last, from rise to fall?

Dalio scores each empire on 18 measures. The key eight are: education, competitiveness, innovation and technology, economic output, share of world trade, military strength, financial center strength, and reserve currency status. A reserve currency is money that the whole world accepts for trade and saving.

The eight rise and fall together, but in order. Education comes first. A strong money market and a world currency come last, and they fade last. People keep using the old currency out of habit, like a common language.

The Dutch show the whole path. They were a small nation of 1 to 2 million people. At their peak, a quarter of the world's major inventions were Dutch. In 1602 they opened the first stock exchange and listed the first public company, the Dutch East India Company.

Then the Dutch grew rich and costly. British shipbuilders hired Dutch designers and built better ships with cheaper workers. The Dutch lost a war to Britain in 1780 to 1784, and their currency, the guilder, lost its place as the world's money. A small country rose on learning, invention and open markets. It fell when it grew costly, indebted and overstretched.

The same top shows up again and again. People grow rich, and rivals copy their methods. Their children never knew hardship, so they borrow and bet that good times will last.

How a country stays strong

  1. Stay productive.Keep learning, building and inventing.
  2. Earn more than you spend.Do not live on debt.
  3. Make the system work for most people.A fair system keeps peace at home.
  4. Build win-win ties with rivals.Cooperation beats a fight to the end.

The debt cycle

Debt lifts spending now and squeezes it later, so money and credit move in cycles that break roughly once in a lifetime.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

On August 15, 1971, President Nixon said the US would stop turning paper dollars into gold. Ray Dalio, then 22, worked on the New York Stock Exchange floor. What did stocks do the next morning?

That Sunday night Dalio watched Nixon on TV and thought that money as he knew it had ended. The US had spent heavily on weapons and social programs and paid by borrowing. It had promised gold for every dollar note, but it held far less gold than the notes it had issued. Investors noticed and turned in their notes, so Nixon cut the link. What shocked Dalio was that he had never seen a currency lose value. It had happened many times before.

Debt gives buying power now and takes it back later. When many people borrow, there is a boom. When they must pay back, spending falls. A short debt cycle lasts about eight years. A long one lasts about 50 to 75 years and holds six to 10 short ones. Because a long one breaks only once in a lifetime, most people are caught off guard.

  1. Stage 1. Little debt and hard money. Gold or silver coins settle a sale on the spot, with no trust needed.
  2. Stage 2. Paper claims on hard money. Banks keep the gold and issue notes that can be turned in for it.
  3. Stage 3. More debt. Everyone likes it, so claims grow past the gold in the bank.
  4. Stage 4. Crisis, defaults and devaluations. People rush to turn claims in, and the central bank breaks the link and prints money.
  5. Stage 5. Fiat money, then debasement. Fiat money is paper with no gold behind it, and printing without limit lowers its value.
  6. Stage 6. The flight back into hard money. People run from the currency into gold, silver and stocks, and governments try to block them.

Four ways to shrink debt

  1. Austerity.Spend less.
  2. Defaults and restructurings.Cut the debts, which cuts someone else's savings.
  3. Transfers.Tax those with more to help those with less.
  4. Printing money.Print it and let it lose value.

Leaders reach for them in roughly this order. Austerity hurts too much to last. Printing is the easiest and the most common, because few can see who loses. The losers are people who hold cash and bonds. With every calm year, people forget the last blowup, even as the risks grow.

Money loses value

Every currency is devalued or dies, so cash is less safe than it feels.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

About 750 currencies have existed since 1700. How many are still in use today?

Most people watch their stocks and homes, not their currency. Dalio says that is a mistake. When debts are too big, a government prints money, because more money makes debts easier to repay. Savers who hold cash lose. Since 1850, cash earned a gain after inflation in about half of the countries and a loss in the other half. German savers were wiped out twice.

A reserve currency lets a country borrow far more than it otherwise could. That is why it plants the seeds of its own end: the country borrows too much, prints to pay, and the money loses value. Three world currencies show the same pattern.

  1. The Dutch guilder, 1780 to 1783.After a war with Britain, the Bank of Amsterdam printed paper to lend to the Dutch East India Company. Customers rushed for gold and silver. Accounts backed by coins fell from 17 million guilders to 300,000.
  2. The British pound, 1947 to 1967.Deep in debt after World War II, Britain suspended the pound's convertibility in 1947, devalued 30 percent against the dollar in 1949, and devalued again in 1967.
  3. The US dollar, 1971.The US ended the link to gold. The price of gold had been fixed at $35 since 1944, and it reached $850 in 1980.

Dalio's rule is "when in doubt, get out." Late in a crisis, doors close and governments bring in capital controls, which limit how money can leave. When a country with big debts starts doing that, run from its currency.
