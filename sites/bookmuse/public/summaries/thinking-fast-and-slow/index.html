Thinking, Fast and Slow

A summary of the book by Daniel Kahneman

A fast mind and a lazy checker

Your mind has a fast storyteller that is always sure, and a slow checker that mostly signs off. Learn the storyteller's tricks by name, and slow down where they cost you.

Daniel Kahneman, a psychologist who won the Nobel Prize in economics, spent years with his friend Amos Tversky catching their own first answers in little puzzles. Trained statisticians fell for the same traps.

All 8 key ideas in Thinking, Fast and Slow

Two characters in your head

Most of your thinking is fast and automatic. The careful part of you is lazy, and mostly signs off.

Ten cents jumps to mind, and it is wrong. The ball costs 5 cents and the bat $1.05. More than half the students at Harvard, MIT and Princeton answered 10 cents.

Kahneman calls the two characters System 1 and System 2. System 1 is fast, effortless and always on. It finishes “bread and…” and hears anger in a voice. System 2 is slow and takes effort. It works out 17 × 24 and parks in a tight space.

Effort costs something, so the mind takes the easiest route. Most of the time System 2 just agrees with System 1. The students who said 10 cents were not slow. They did not check, and checking was all that was missing.

System 1 is not the villain. It gets most things right. But it cannot be switched off, and it never tells you when it is guessing.

Which of these needs System 2?

The book's main advice. Learn to spot a minefield: a situation where System 1 is likely to be wrong. Then slow down and ask System 2 for help.

What you see is all there is

Your mind builds the best story it can from what it has, and the story feels true even when it has almost nothing.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Mindik is intelligent and strong. Will she be a good leader?

Notice what you did not do: you did not ask what you would need to know before judging a leader. System 1 started from the first word and built the best story it could. Kahneman calls this WYSIATI: the mind treats what you see as all there is, and what you cannot see does not count. Knowing little does not slow it down.

The story also feels better when it is easy to take in. Clear print, a message you have seen before, even a good mood all make things feel familiar, and familiar feels true. People who had seen the phrase “the body temperature of a chicken” more readily accepted that it is 144°, a number that was made up.

One more habit works the same way. When a question is hard, the mind quietly answers an easier one and never tells you. Kahneman calls this substitution.

The investor felt he had judged a stock. He had judged a car. Feelings make the easiest swap: if you like a technology, you tend to see its benefits as large and its risks as small.

Try this. When a hard question gets a quick, sure answer, stop and ask yourself which question you just answered.

Anchors pull your guess

A number you have just seen pulls your guess toward it, even when it has nothing to do with the question.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

A wheel of fortune stops at 65. Now guess: what share of United Nations members are African countries?

This is anchoring. Ask whether Gandhi was older than 114 when he died, and your guess at his age comes out much higher than if you were asked about 35.

Two more shortcuts bend your numbers in the same quiet way:

  • Easy examples. What comes to mind easily feels common. In one survey, 80% of people said accidents kill more people than strokes. Strokes kill nearly twice as many. Vivid news had made accidents feel more common than they are.
  • Good stories. A good fit feels likely. Linda is 31, single, outspoken and very bright. Asked if she is more likely a bank teller, or a bank teller and a feminist activist, 85 to 90% of undergraduates chose the second. A detail can only shrink a group.

Both push aside the base rate: how common each answer is to begin with. In the United States there are more than 20 times as many male farmers as male librarians. Start from the base rate, then move only as far as the evidence earns.

Try this. Before you judge a person or a case, ask how common this kind of thing is. Start from that number, then adjust.
