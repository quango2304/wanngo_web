The signals under your words

People judge you on warmth and competence, and they read both from tiny signals called cues. You can learn to read them and to send them on purpose.

Vanessa Van Edwards teaches people skills and leads a research team called Science of People. Her book covers more than forty cues from body, voice, words and looks. This summary gives you the ones that matter most.

All 8 key ideas in Cues

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Warmth plus competence

People judge you on two things, warmth and competence. Charisma is a balance of both.

Jamie Siminoff pitched his video doorbell on the TV show Shark Tank in 2013. His sales were strong, yet every investor, called a Shark, said no. He asked his own name like a question, swallowed hard when pushed, and paused oddly in the middle of a sentence. His idea was fine. His cues were not. Five years later he came back as a Shark, smiling and shaking hands, and he seemed a different person.

Van Edwards says people ask two questions about you, in this order. Can I trust you? That is warmth. Can I rely on you? That is competence. Most of us lean one way. Warm people are trusted but may not be taken seriously. Competent people are taken seriously but can seem cold. A balance of both is the Charisma Zone. Low on both is the Danger Zone, where people overlook, dismiss or pity you. Doctors show why both matter: those whom patients rated poorly on warmth were more likely to be sued.

The best communicators use the scale like a dial. In one early interview Jeff Bezos laughed and gestured freely, which dials up warmth. In a later one he sat expansively and spoke in a lower voice, which dials up competence, and he still chuckled. The book's number one advice: send clear cues based on your goals. For a pitch, a negotiation or an interview, dial up competence. For trust and teamwork, dial up warmth.

Priya is kind and helpful, and her team likes her. But people talk over her in meetings. Next week she presents a plan to the managers. What should she do?

Try this. Which words sound more like you: trustworthy, kind, a team player, or impressive, smart, an expert? That shows which way you lean.

Cues go both ways

Every cue you read, feel and send feeds one loop, and moods spread along it.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

In a study, small groups of students decided how to share raises. In each group, a hired actor played one mood, such as cheerful or irritable. Afterward, how many members could say why their meeting went the way it did?

The book calls this emotional contagion. On the show Queer Eye, host Karamo Brown said he was catching the anxiety of a woman who wrung her hands and bit her nails. When you see an expression, your own face copies it a little, and that makes you feel it.

So a cue can come in, change you, and go out again. Siminoff failed at reading and at sending. He missed a sign of doubt from the Shark Mark Cuban, who pulled the corners of his mouth down in a mouth shrug. And he sent weak cues of his own.

You cannot switch your cues off. Poker bluffers go very still, and the stillness gives them away. At work, a blank face and flat voice send you toward the Danger Zone. Aim to match your cues to your goals. Naming a cue helps too: when people labeled an angry face, the fear center of their brain calmed.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Sam is nervous before a big meeting. What should he do?

Try this. Record yourself in a meeting or on a video call, as naturally as you can, and watch it. The book calls this the only true way to see the cues you send.

Lean in, open up, face them

Five body cues show that you are present: lean in, stay open, face the person, use space and gaze with purpose.

Kofi Essel, a children's doctor, has a few seconds to win over a child and a parent. He greets the child first with a high five and drops to the child's eye level. The parents relax. Then he turns his body and eyes to them. When he types notes, he stops and turns all the way around, so no screen sits between them.

A podium or a microphone held to your chest is a block too, so step away from the podium and use a remote for slides. Open posture even works in a photo. A website owner swapped his header picture of crossed arms for one with open arms. Across 237,797 visitors in ninety days, open arms won, with 5.4 percent more email sign-ups.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Rita talks with a new client at a table. She holds a folder against her chest and sits back. What should she do?

Try this. Today, turn your toes, torso and head toward everyone who talks to you.
