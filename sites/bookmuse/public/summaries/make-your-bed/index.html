Ten lessons from SEAL training

Small habits, good teammates, courage and never quitting can change your life, and one person at a time, they can change the world.

William H. McRaven is a retired Navy admiral who served as a Navy SEAL, a member of the Navy's special forces. In 2014 he spoke to graduating students at the University of Texas. His speech was built on ten lessons from SEAL training, and people liked it so much that he wrote this short book. Each chapter adds a story about someone who inspired him.

He did the math for the graduates. If each of them changed the lives of ten people, and each of those people changed ten more, then in five generations, or 125 years, they would have changed 800 million lives.

The ten lessons

  • Start your day with a task completed. Begin with a small win, like making your bed.
  • You can't go it alone. Find someone to help you paddle.
  • Only the size of your heart matters. Judge people by their heart, not their size.
  • Life's not fair. Drive on! Don't complain about an unfair day. Keep going.
  • Failure can make you stronger. Don't fear failing. Learn from it.
  • You must dare greatly. Take a risk, even a scary one.
  • Stand up to the bullies. Don't back down from them.
  • Rise to the occasion. Be at your best when things are darkest.
  • Give people hope. Lift up others when times are hard.
  • Never, ever quit! Don't quit, even when it looks easier. Trainees quit by ringing a bell.

Start with one small task

Start your day with a task completed, such as making your bed, and the next task gets easier to begin.

Every morning in SEAL training, the instructors checked each trainee's bed. The corners had to be square and the covers pulled tight. The pillow sat in the center, and the extra blanket was folded at the foot. The last test was a quarter, flipped onto the mattress. On a tight bed it bounced high enough for the instructor to catch.

A perfect bed earned no praise. The instructor only nodded. It was expected, and it showed discipline and attention to detail. It also gave McRaven a small sense of pride and pushed him to do another task. Little things matter, he says: someone who can't get them right will not get big things right either. At night, a made bed waited for him, even after a bad day.

McRaven kept the habit for life. After a serious injury, he lay in a hospital bed at home. The first thing he did once he could lift himself up was pull the sheets tight and straighten the pillow. It was his way of showing he was moving forward.

Find people to paddle with

You can't go it alone: find someone to help you paddle, because no one gets through hard times alone.

In the first phase of SEAL training, every trainee had a boat crew. The crew was seven trainees, three on each side of a ten-foot rubber boat and a coxswain, who steers and sets the stroke count. They carried the boat everywhere. They ran with it on their heads, and they paddled it for miles through heavy surf. Every paddle had to follow the count, or the boat turned against the wave. When one trainee was sick or hurt, the others paddled harder and even gave him their food. Later he returned the favor.

McRaven needed his own crew most after a parachute accident. During a routine jump, another jumper's parachute opened right in front of him and hit him at 120 miles an hour. His parachute tangled around his legs. When it opened, it tore his pelvis apart by almost five inches.

He thought his career was over, and he felt only self-pity. His wife reminded him who he was and refused to let him feel sorry for himself. Friends came by and called. His boss, an admiral, found a way around a rule that called for a medical review of whether he could stay a SEAL. McRaven believes that help likely saved his career.

Let setbacks make you stronger

Some bad days are unfair and some are your own failure, and both can make you stronger if you keep moving.

  • Life's not fair. Drive on! Don't complain. Stand tall and keep going.
  • Failure can make you stronger. Let each failure teach you, then try again.

In SEAL training, an instructor could send a trainee into the surf to roll in the sand until he was covered. Then he wore the wet, sandy uniform all day. Trainees called this being a sugar cookie. It could come for no reason, even after a perfect effort. Some trainees could not accept that, and they did not make it through training.

An instructor named Moki Martin told McRaven why: life isn't fair. Later, Moki crashed his bike head-on and was paralyzed from the waist down. McRaven never heard him ask "Why me?" Moki became a painter, had a daughter and founded a yearly triathlon. An unfair blow did not decide who he became.

Tap an answer

A trainee who missed a standard got two extra hours of exercise at the end of the day. It was called a Circus. After many of them, what happened to those who kept going?

McRaven and his swim partner came in last on a swim, so they landed on the Circus list. Tired the next day, they could miss again and face another Circus. Many trainees quit in that spiral. These two kept going, and their swims got better.

On the final five-mile ocean swim, after about four hours in cold water, they were the first pair to finish. The second pair was not even in sight.

Try this. When something unfair lands on you, don't complain. Stand tall and drive on. When you fail, ask what it can teach you.
