Talking when it matters most

Most problems last because we dodge or botch the talks that matter most, and the way out is to make it safe for everyone to say what they think, starting with your own motives and your own stories.

The authors looked for moments when what people do matters most. Many are risky talks, and a few people handle them far better. These are the skills those people use, in the book’s order: what to do first, how to start talking and how to finish.

Dialogue and the Fool's Choice

Skilled people refuse to pick honesty or friendship, and they get everyone's meaning into the open.

A crucial conversation is a talk where opinions differ, the stakes are high and emotions run strong. Of eight vice presidents at one company, only Kevin was named by colleagues as very influential, so the authors watched him. In a meeting about a new office site, the CEO pushed his own pick. When people disagreed, he got defensive: first a raised eyebrow, then a finger, then a louder voice. The questions stopped. Then Kevin said, “Can I check something out with you?” He told the CEO he seemed to be breaking his own rules for deciding. The CEO admitted he had been forcing his opinion, and the team chose a far better site.

Everyone saw the problem. They all thought they had two bad options: speak and make an enemy of the most powerful person, or stay silent and let a bad decision pass. The book calls this the Fool’s Choice: believing you must pick honesty or friendship. Kevin asked a different question. How can I be completely honest and completely respectful at once?

What he created is called dialogue, the free flow of meaning between people. Picture a pool. Everyone arrives with their own thoughts and feelings. The more of them go into the shared pool, the better the choices. When people hold back, smart people do foolish things. A surgical team once removed part of a woman’s foot when she came in for her tonsils. At least seven people questioned, in their heads, why the surgeon was operating on her foot. None spoke.

Silence also costs time, and the book says this wait does the most damage. A team’s health shows in the lag time between seeing a problem and talking about it. Meanwhile we act it out: we snap, pull away or get sarcastic.

Start with Heart

When a talk gets tense, your motive slips before your words do, so stop and ask what you really want.

Greta, a CEO, had spent six months pushing her leaders to cut costs. In a tense meeting, a manager asked why a second office was being built while everyone was told to save paper and skip travel. Greta’s jaw tightened and she raised a finger at him. The room went silent. Then her finger dropped. She thanked him and admitted she had not watched this project as tightly as she asked others to. The group agreed to cut the office’s cost in half or cancel it, and support for cost cutting grew fast.

Later she explained. At first she wanted revenge. So she paused and asked, “What do I really want?” The first answer was to humiliate him. She asked again. Now the answer was 200 managers leaving supportive of cost cutting. The man at the back stopped looking like an enemy. He looked like her best ally in the room.

In a hard talk, your motive goes wrong first, and you rarely see it. So work from the outside in: look at what you are doing and ask what you are acting like you want. Asking hard questions also calms the urge to fight or run. Then ask these three.

  1. What do I really want?For you, for them and for the relationship.
  2. What do I not want?The bad result that makes silence or attack look sensible.
  3. How can I get the first without the second?Join both in one question: how can we be candid and still strengthen the relationship?

Master My Stories

Other people’s actions do not make you feel anything. The story you tell about them does, and you can retell it.

Maria, a copywriter, was meant to present a proposal with her colleague Louis. He made almost all their points, so she had nothing left to say. She told herself he was shrinking her role because she was the only woman on the team. She felt angry and hurt, said nothing and made the odd sarcastic jab.

A step sits between what you see and what you feel: a story. You see something, tell yourself why it happened, feel, and then act. Ten people can watch the same event and feel ten different ways. You tell the story, so you can change it.

First split fact from story. A fact is something you could see or hear. Louis gave “95 percent of the presentation and answered all but one question.” That is a fact. “He doesn’t trust me” is a story.

When we know we did something wrong, we often tell a clever story to feel better. There are three.

Victim Story“It’s not my fault.” You leave out your part.
Villain Story“It’s all your fault.” You make a decent person a villain.
Helpless Story“There’s nothing else I can do.” You see no choice ahead.

Clever stories leave things out. Tell the rest by asking what you are pretending not to notice about your own role, and why a reasonable, rational and decent person would do this. Maria saw she had never asked why she was left out, and had said nothing when he took her slides. She asked Louis to talk. He apologized and said he talks more when nervous. They agreed to take a half each.
