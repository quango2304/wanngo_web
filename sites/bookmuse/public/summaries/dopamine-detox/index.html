Dopamine Detox

A summary of the book by Thibaut Meurisse

A calmer brain for hard work

Constant stimulation makes your important work feel dull. Lower the noise, and hard things get easier to start.

Thibaut Meurisse is a personal development author. He wrote this short guide for people who keep putting off key tasks and feel restless. His idea is simple: cut back what stimulates you, and your most important work feels easier.

Craving, not pleasure

Dopamine, a chemical messenger in your brain, does not give you pleasure. It makes you want things, and each hit makes you want a bigger one.

Dopamine makes you expect a reward, like good food, and it gives you the wish to act. Meurisse says this drive helped humans survive.

But it is not a pleasure chemical. When you pay close attention, you often feel empty as soon as you get the reward. Then you look for the next one.

Think of dopamine as the “molecule of more.” The more you trigger it, the more you want your next “shot.” Your tolerance grows: it takes more to give you the same feeling. This is also how many addictions work.

Today many things are built to trigger it. Social media companies earn more advertising money the longer you stay, and notifications are one of their best tools. Each time you see one, you expect a reward, so dopamine rises. The reward is usually brief.

Algorithms, the programs that pick the next video for you, help them. Meurisse watched one basketball video, got a recommendation for another and watched that too. Then he watched another, and so on. The book names four places where you expect a reward:

EmailInvestment or website statsYouTubeSocial media

Why hard work feels dull

When you are overstimulated, hard work feels dull because it needs a calm state, and you are in a fast one.

What is the most overlooked reason we procrastinate?

Think of a typical morning. You grab your phone, check email, then visit social media for “a few minutes.” By then, emails, notifications and likes have released dopamine. You are overstimulated, which means you have had too much stimulation. Now you sit down to work, and you suddenly want another coffee.

The author is a writer, so writing is his most important task. Once he checks social media, YouTube or his sales figures, writing becomes one of the hardest and least appealing things he can imagine. The answer is to lower your stimulation. That is what a detox does. It also fixes the most overlooked reason we put things off: overstimulation.

Four tricks that keep you stuck

Once you are overstimulated, your mind plays four tricks to keep you there:

  • Trick #1: returning to work is easy. It says you can start whenever you choose. In most cases you can't get back to work for hours.
  • Trick #2: you can do it later. It says there is plenty of time. Put off enough and delay becomes a habit.
  • Trick #3: excitement is not fulfillment. It sells you on the fun. Ask what the time really gave you.
  • Trick #4: you're missing out. Checking email or news every thirty minutes feels like control. It is the fear of missing out.

The author says he can't count how many times he told himself he would start writing later. It never happens.

What a detox is

A detox cuts your stimulation so hard tasks feel easier to start. It comes in three sizes, and it is quiet, not empty.

The book defines a dopamine detox as reducing stimulation, so you are in the right state of mind for major tasks. The name is not scientific, because you are not draining dopamine. When you are overstimulated, your tolerance has grown, so you need more outside stimulation to release the same amount of dopamine.
