Calling in “The One”

A summary of the book by Katherine Woodward Thomas

7 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Getting ready for a great love

Searching harder for the right person will not change your love life. Becoming someone a great love can grow with will.

Katherine Woodward Thomas was a licensed therapist in marriage and family work. She was still unmarried at forty, and she says she kept choosing men who could not commit. Then she began to look at her own part. The book is the course she built from that: forty-nine daily practices over seven weeks. She says it led her to her husband Mark. They later divorced, and she says she used the course again in her sixties to meet a new partner. She cannot promise you will meet “The One” in seven weeks. She promises you will be open and ready for love.

The course can be intense. The book says that anyone who has been through severe abuse may need extra support, and should get professional help if needed. It also says that serious depression is not cured just by choosing to feel better.

The lessons below follow the book’s main ideas, not its days. It asks you to try three ideas first, and to drop them later if they do not help.

  1. Life is still being made. Your beliefs, choices and words shape what happens to you.
  2. Set intentions, not expectations. Choose a clear aim, then live to match it.
  3. Change how you treat yourself. The outside of your life then changes too.

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

It happens through you

A painful pattern in love does not only happen to you. It also happens through what you do, and you can change that.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

A Stanford psychiatrist studied 1,500 people to see what set happy couples apart from unhappy ones. What was it?

Aida kept falling for men she called “takers.” They seemed charming at first. After she was all in, they gave less and expected more. When she tried to talk, she felt ashamed for needing too much. She blamed men. The author asked how Aida took part, and Aida felt accused.

Then she looked at what she did. She put all her attention on pleasing him. She hid her feelings and stayed quiet, so she would not seem needy. When he asked how she felt, she changed the subject. So he never got a chance to show he cared. She began to share her needs. Some people adjusted. A few friends left, who liked her better as a doormat. She later married a kind man who listens.

Her pattern was not only bad luck or bad men. It was also something she did without seeing it, so she could change it.

This is not blame. The author says you are not to blame, and abuse in childhood was never your fault. Some causes are outside your control. How you show up now is yours.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Dev says, “Everyone I date goes cold after a few weeks.” When he feels hurt, he goes quiet and never says why. What should he do first?

Try this. Write your pattern in one plain sentence, such as “I get involved with unavailable people.” Then make one new choice today, like telling someone how you feel.

Let go to make room

Love needs room. Before something new comes in, something old has to go.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

In the book’s view, what does it mean to forgive someone who hurt you?

Soon after the author set her goal, three ties had to end. She stopped hoping a former boyfriend would change. She ended the romance with a man who had no wish for a family. And she let go of a man who kept hinting he might leave his live-in girlfriend someday. She says all three had to go before Mark could come in.

At her workshops, people described in detail the partner they wanted. When she asked what they would give up, the room went silent. Wanting love is easy. Making room for it costs something. Another example the book gives is a wall built around the heart.

Forgiving is part of making room. An ex-boyfriend had ended up in charge of a project they built together, and she was bitter. When she let the bitterness go, she saw her own part. To forgive, the book says, is to cancel the debt you believe someone owes you. It is not agreeing with what they did, or letting them back in. She had given him half the ownership because she doubted her own idea.

Do not forgive too soon. If someone is still hurting you, anger can tell you to set a boundary first. Forgive once you are safe. If someone abuses you in a dangerous way, get out at once.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Rae keeps her ex’s old letters and hopes each week that he will change his mind. She wants a partner. What should she do?

Try this. Take one action today that lets something go. End a friendship that clearly is not working, burn an old love letter, or clear out clothes you have not worn for a year to leave room for your future partner’s things.

The story under the pattern

Under your pattern sits a story you made up as a child. It feels true, but it is only a story.

Children try to explain what hurts. A child cannot see that the adults were limited, so the child blames herself. The book calls the result your False Love Identity: three beliefs about yourself, about others and about life.

Maria’s father beat her mother. When Maria was four, they left and moved in with her mother’s parents. Her grandparents often made remarks about her Mexican blood. At five, she did not know the word racism. She only decided she was a “bad seed” and less than other kids.

As an adult, she was drawn to two kinds of men: those she thought were beneath her, and those who looked down on her. Her story was a five-year-old’s answer, yet it shaped her love life.

Why does a made-up story feel true? Over the years you gather proof for it, without seeing that you do. How you act pulls other people into playing along. To start, write the sentence down and ask what an old hurt made you think about yourself.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Every time a date goes quiet, Mia thinks, “I’m too much.” What should she do first?

Try this. Write: “What did I make my biggest childhood disappointment mean about me?” Then ask how old the part of you is that still believes it.
