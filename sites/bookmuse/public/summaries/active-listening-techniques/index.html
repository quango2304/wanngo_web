A summary of the book by Nixaly Leonardo

Listening that makes people open up

People listen to you when they feel heard, so start by listening with your whole self.

Nixaly Leonardo is a licensed clinical social worker and psychotherapist. She teaches active listening to her clients, and says it changes how they see themselves and how others treat them. Her book explains the basics first. Then it gives 30 tools for real situations at home and at work, such as heated arguments and hard talks with a boss.

Listening is more than waiting

Active listening means answering with your body, your face and your words, so the speaker feels heard.

Most people listen passively. They let the other person talk and wait for their turn. Active listening asks for more. It blends three kinds of listening. Passive listening means letting the speaker finish. Reflective listening means saying back what you heard. Critical listening means weighing the message and reading the speaker’s feelings and body language.

Alan works in IT for a firm that supplies Wi-Fi to big conferences. A client, Eddie, told him the problem and everything he had read online about fixing it. Alan thought he knew the answer and tried to explain, but Eddie kept talking over him. So Alan guessed that Eddie wanted to be heard and praised for his research. He gave Eddie uninterrupted time, with eye contact, nods, “mhmm” and “I see.” Then he said, “Wow, looks like you did your research!” and explained his plan. Alan finished his task, and Eddie felt heard.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

In a 2010 study, interviewers repeated back, in their own words, what people told them. What changed for the people who were interviewed?

The book lists seven basics. Saying it back works best with nonverbal cues, and none of the basics is effective alone, so you use them together.

  1. Paraphrasing: say what you heard in your own words.
  2. Nonverbal language: nods, “mhmm” and a face that shows interest.
  3. Emotional labeling: name the feeling, like “You seem really upset right now.”
  4. Silence: leave room for the speaker to think and feel.
  5. Redirecting: steer back to the topic, or to a calmer one.
  6. Mirroring: match their posture and tone a little. Too much looks like mimicry.
  7. Validating: say they have the right to feel what they feel.
QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

A friend tells Lena, with a shaking voice, that she was passed over for a promotion. Lena wants her to feel heard. What should she do?

Try this. In your next talk, do what Alan did. Give the speaker uninterrupted time, show interest with your face and a “mhmm,” and only then answer.

Quiet your own noise

You cannot hear someone while your own noise is loud. Notice it and set it aside.

The book sorts what blocks listening into four kinds of noise. Physical noise is outside sound, like a beeping phone. Physiological noise comes from the body, like illness or feeling tired and hungry. Semantic noise is trouble with the words, like jargon. Psychological noise is in your head: stress, strong feelings and fixed opinions about people.

Hannah is a writer with a deadline in a few hours. Her friend Mary calls, crying. Her boyfriend just broke up with her. Hannah keeps typing while Mary talks. Mary hears the keys and asks, “Have you been working this whole time?” She says no one cares about her and hangs up. She ignores Hannah’s calls for several days. Hannah’s deadline and her screen were noise, and Mary felt it.

The book says five minutes of full attention is probably worth more than an hour of partial attention. Hannah could have listened for a few minutes, then said she had a deadline and would be happy to talk more later.

Another tool: picture your own worries on a shelf. They are still in view, but not heavy, and they wait while you listen. They cannot stay there forever. Mindfulness helps too. It means focusing on the present moment without judging it. For a face-to-face talk, cut the physical noise: find a private space and silence your phone.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

A coworker stops by Nora’s desk to talk through a worry. Nora’s phone keeps buzzing with messages. What should she do?

Try this. Before you listen, picture one worry on the shelf. It stays in view, but it is not heavy, and it can wait.

Check your assumptions

A guess about what someone meant is not a fact. Check the evidence before you react.

Paul and Cheryl were making dinner. Paul, who is proud of his cooking, offered Cheryl a tip on chopping onions evenly. Cheryl wanted to cut them her own way and brushed him off with a sarcastic grin. At dinner Paul got angry and made an unkind remark. Cheryl had been put down by men before, so she heard a judgment in his tip. Paul had been bullied, so her brush-off made him feel powerless. Each one read far more into the moment than was there. The book calls these distortions: beliefs shaped by past hurt that bend how you read someone.

The worksheet comes from cognitive behavioral therapy, a kind of talk therapy. You write down what happened, how you felt and the thought that popped up on its own. Then you test the strongest thought like a lawyer weighing evidence. Most often you find the assumption was wrong.

You can also act in the moment. Do not accuse. Give the benefit of the doubt, then ask for what you need. If a friend keeps checking his phone, ask: “Is there something important you need to attend to?” If not, ask: “Would it be okay if we put our phones away for now?”

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Carlos sends his sister a long, friendly text. She answers with one letter: “k.” He thinks: “She’s mad at me.” What should he do first?

Try this. Before you talk with someone who often sets you off, take a moment to think about their good intentions.
