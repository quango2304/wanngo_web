Grit

A summary of the book by Angela Duckworth

What carries you farther than talent

What you achieve over a lifetime depends more on grit than on talent. Grit is passion and perseverance for a goal that matters to you, and it can be grown.

Angela Duckworth was a math teacher before she became a psychologist. She saw that her most talented students were not always her best. Then she studied who sticks with hard things, from West Point cadets to spelling bee finalists, and asked what sets them apart.

Effort counts twice

Talent matters, but effort counts twice: it builds your skill, and it makes your skill produce something.

At West Point, new cadets face a hard seven-week program called Beast, and some quit early on. The school rates each cadet's talent with one admissions score. Duckworth also gave cadets a ten-statement quiz about finishing what you start and keeping your interests. Which best told who would stay?

Angela Duckworth taught math in New York before she became a psychologist. Some of her most talented seventh graders earned weak grades, while some who struggled at first did better, because they came every day, asked questions and tried again. She had been distracted by talent.

Most people say effort matters more than talent. Yet professional musicians heard what seemed to be two pianists with the same record, one called a "natural" and one a "striver". It was one pianist playing parts of the same piece. The musicians judged the natural more likely to succeed. Duckworth calls this the naturalness bias, a hidden liking for people we think were born gifted.

Her theory has two steps. Talent is how fast your skill grows when you put in effort. Skill times effort gives achievement, which is what you do with your skill. The potter Warren MacKenzie shows both. A good potter, he says, turns out forty or fifty pots a day, and only a few are worth selling. As his skill grew, more of each day's pots were good, so the total of good pots grew too.

A person with twice the talent but half the effort can reach the same skill and still produce far less over the years. Even more than effort on one day, what counts is coming back the next day.

Passion is a compass

Grit has two parts: passion, which means holding one top goal for years, and perseverance, which means getting up again.

Duckworth's Grit Scale has ten statements, half about perseverance and half about passion. "I finish whatever I begin" tests perseverance. "My interests change from year to year" tests passion. Here passion is not a strong feeling. It is staying loyal to the same aim over time. In her words, "Enthusiasm is common. Endurance is rare."

She pictures goals as a ladder. Small tasks sit at the bottom. Keep asking why, and the answers climb. When the answer is only "just because", you have reached your top goal. It gives direction to every goal below it. A lack of grit shows in three ways:

  1. A dream with no steps. You picture being a doctor but have no goals below it.
  2. Many goals with no top. Mid-level goals that serve no single aim.
  3. Rival ladders. Two or three top goals that never meet.

Bob Mankoff wanted to earn a living by being funny. He tried stand-up and cartoons, then dropped stand-up and kept cartoons. The New Yorker rejected him about two thousand times from 1974 to 1977. Trying again was not working, so he studied every cartoon the magazine had printed since 1925. He saw that each cartoonist had a style of their own. He built his dot style, and a cartoon was accepted. He is the magazine's cartoon editor.

To go with the ladder, Duckworth uses ink and pencil: write the top goal in ink and the goals below it in pencil, and erase them when they are blocked.

Try this. Write down twenty-five career goals and circle the five that matter most. Avoid the other twenty, because they pull time from what matters. Then ask whether your five serve one common purpose. Warren Buffett reportedly gave his pilot the first steps. The last question is Duckworth's.

Grit can grow

Grit is partly inherited, but it grows with experience, in four parts: interest, practice, purpose and hope.

Is grit in your genes? Partly. A British study of over two thousand sets of teenage twins found that genes explain 37 percent of the differences in perseverance and 20 percent in passion. Experience explains the rest. Height works the same way. Genes matter, yet the average British man grew from five feet five to five feet ten in about 150 years, as food and medicine got better.

Grit also grows with age. In Duckworth's data, adults in their late sixties or older were the grittiest, and people in their twenties the least. Part of the reason may be the maturity principle: as life goes on, most of us grow more dependable, confident, caring and calm, because life asks more of us. A headmaster's daughter was late for school nearly every day. At a summer job in a clothing store, the manager said the first time she was late she was fired. She set two alarms at once. As Duckworth puts it, "Lectures don't have half the effect of consequences."

People rarely quit because they are lazy or unreliable. They quit for a reason. Just before they give up, one of four thoughts often crosses their mind:

  1. "I'm bored."
  2. "The effort isn't worth it."
  3. "This isn't important to me."
  4. "I can't do this, so I might as well give up."

Four strengths answer them. The first three tend to grow in this order, and hope is needed at every stage. They are not "you have it or you don't" gifts. You can learn each one.
