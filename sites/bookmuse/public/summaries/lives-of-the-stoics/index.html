A summary of the book by Ryan Holiday and Stephen Hanselman

A philosophy you can watch

The Stoics were judged by what they did, not what they said. Their lives and deaths show how to live well: with four virtues, a calm focus on what you control, and daily practice.

Ryan Holiday and Stephen Hanselman tell the lives of 26 people, from Zeno in Athens to the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius. One was a slave and one was an emperor. Stoicism is an old Greek philosophy built on four virtues: courage, temperance (self-control), justice and wisdom. The authors say they care more about the lessons than exact history, so some old stories may be legend. Some of these people lived up to their ideas. Some did not. Both teach.

Deeds before words

A Stoic is known by what they do. The best way to learn Stoicism is to watch how people live.

Cato the Younger wrote almost nothing. He had no time to write, between office and war. Yet the book says he became famous for what he did and who he was. As a young treasury official, he was first to arrive and last to leave. Politicians turned down requests by saying that Cato would never agree. Sent to oversee Rome's takeover of Cyprus, he sold its treasures with no cheating at all. He kept one thing back from the sale: a statue of Zeno, founder of Stoicism.

Cleanthes shows the same thing from the other side. He arrived in Athens poor and carried water at night so he could study by day. Years later, some citizens thought he looked too healthy to be working so hard, so they took him to court to explain how he earned a living. A gardener and a woman whose grain he ground spoke for him. He was freed and given a hundred drachmas, far more than he had when he arrived. He still turned down gifts from a king. For him, work and philosophy were two sides of one coin.

Epictetus, a later Stoic, put the rule in one line: "Don't explain your philosophy, embody it."

In your life

At work, you trust the person whose habits you have watched for years, not the one who talks best about trust.

Try this. Do one small thing for a value you care about, and tell no one. Epictetus said to train for yourself, not for the world to see.

One good, four virtues

Virtue is the one thing that is truly good. The Stoics named four parts of it: courage, temperance, justice and wisdom.

Zeno was a merchant who traded a rare purple dye. His ship sank and he lost everything. In the best-known story, he said the shipwreck was a good voyage, because it sent him to Athens and to philosophy. He lived simply from then on, on bread, honey and a little wine. Losing his money had shown him that money mattered very little. He was the first to put the four virtues into words. A later Stoic, Arius Didymus, made each one plain:

What about money and health? Zeno called them "preferred indifferents": nicer to have, but not good in themselves. His student Aristo went further: only virtue mattered, and a wise person needs no practical rules, only trained instincts. Fellow Stoics set a trap. One twin left money with Aristo. The other twin came back, pretended to be the first, and asked for it. Aristo handed it to the wrong brother. A plain rule, like checking who someone is, would have served him better than his gut. So the Stoics kept a middle path: aim for virtue, treat wealth and health as tools, and use simple rules where they help.

What is up to you

Sort every problem into what is up to you and what is not. Then spend your effort only on the first.

Epictetus was born a slave. One story says his master twisted his leg. Epictetus calmly warned him it would break. When it snapped, he said, "Didn't I warn you?" The book admits it cannot be sure how he came to limp. What is sure is his main lesson. Our chief task, he said, is to separate what depends on us from what does not. Up to us are our attitudes, wants and opinions about what happens. Not up to us are outside things, like what we own. Even a slave keeps the power to choose how to answer.

One night a thief stole the iron lamp from his home. Epictetus felt a flash of anger, then paused. He told himself he would find a clay lamp tomorrow, because you can only lose something you own. The thief took a lamp. He did not take his calm.

Another Stoic, Antipater of Tarsus, used this picture of an archer. We train, aim and release, and that is our part. Many things outside our control decide where the arrow lands. So our worth is not in the bull's-eye. It is in the training and the aim.
