I’m Glad My Mom Died

A summary of the book by Jennette McCurdy

8 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

A girl who lived for her mother

A girl raised to live for her mother’s approval loses track of what she wants, and she begins to heal only when she tells the truth about her mother and learns what she wants herself.

Jennette McCurdy was a child actor, best known for the Nickelodeon show iCarly. Her mother had breast cancer when Jennette was two, and the fear of it filled their home. Her mother also ran her career, her food and her days. Jennette tells it as she remembers it, and she says some names were changed.

The book covers an eating disorder, abuse and grief, and parts of it are painful to read. The title is blunt. By the end she believes that, if her mother were alive, she would still be trapped in the life her mother built. What you take from it is how a controlling love works, and what getting free looked like for her.

Living for her mood

A child who lives to keep a parent happy learns to read every mood and loses track of what she wants herself.

On her sixth birthday, Jennette had spent two weeks deciding what to wish for. A birthday wish was the most power she had, so she spent it on one thing: that her mother would stay alive another year. Her mother had stage four breast cancer when Jennette was two, and the house felt to her like a held breath.

Nobody else in the family could read her mother’s moods. Jennette had spent her whole life studying them, so she could keep her mother happy at any moment.

At an ice cream shop, Jennette chose Cookies ’N Cream. Her mother said Nutty Coconut had been her favorite for eight months. Her mother’s eyes filled with tears, and she said Jennette was changing and growing up. So Jennette took it back and ordered Nutty Coconut. She was secretly sick of coconut, so she made happy sounds with every bite. Even her favorite flavor belonged to her mother’s mood.

Years later, after her mother had died, Jennette sat before her twenty-second birthday candles and did not know what to wish for. The wish she had made all her life was over. She had tried so hard to know her mother that she never came to know herself.

Over time she learned a small habit. At a party, she passed the cheeseburgers and chose a cookie, because it was what she felt like eating. She wrote that she now paid attention to what she felt.

A dream that is not yours

A parent’s dream can become a child’s job, and a child who says no may end up comforting the parent.

Jennette’s mother had wanted to be an actress, but her own parents would not let her. When Jennette was six, her mother asked if she wanted to be “Mommy’s little actress.” Jennette remembers that there was only one right answer.

At the talent agency, a man said it mattered that Jennette wanted to act. Her mother answered that she wanted it more than anything. Jennette thought that it was her mother who wanted it more than anything, not her. The day had been stressful and not fun. But she also wanted what her mother wanted.

By eleven, her best skill was crying on cue, which means making real tears the moment a camera needs them. To do it, she pictured her brothers or her grandfather dying. Her mother helped her pick the sad scene.

At an audition for a police show, the tears would not come, and she failed. On the drive home, she told her mother she did not want to act anymore.

Then Jennette suggested her mother’s favorite Phil Collins album, and her mother asked why she was not singing along. Jennette sang, with her best fake smile. Her one no had turned into her mother’s crisis, so she took it back.

She did have one thing of her own. Writing a screenplay felt real, because the words were hers. When she showed it to her mother, her mother hoped she did not like writing more than acting. Jennette said she did not. It was a lie.

Control that feels like closeness

When control is called teamwork, a child can feel close to the parent while she is being harmed.

At eleven, Jennette felt a sore lump on her chest and feared cancer, like her mother. Her mother laughed and said she was just growing. Jennette was horrified. Growing up meant her mother might love her less, because her mother often wept and said she wanted her to stay small and young. Jennette asked how to stop it. Her mother said there was a secret: calorie restriction, which means eating much less.

Her mother gave her diet books, and they worked on it together every night, like a secret only they shared. Jennette wrote that she was thrilled, and that it brought them even closer.

Soon two other people spoke up. They used the word “anorexia,” an illness in which a person eats far too little.

Her mother was not outside the problem. She had planned it with Jennette and supported it. The adult who could have stopped it was the one running it. Closeness and control came from the same person, so Jennette had no way to tell them apart.

Her mother also washed her in the shower herself and gave her private exams to look for lumps, saying they could be cancer. They went on until Jennette was seventeen. During them she felt outside her own body and thought about Disneyland.
