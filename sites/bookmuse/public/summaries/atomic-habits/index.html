Tiny habits, big change

Small habits, repeated every day, build the person you want to become. Make good habits obvious, attractive, easy and satisfying, and flip those four laws to break bad ones.

In high school, a baseball bat hit James Clear in the face. It broke his nose and his skull. Two years later he started college and built small habits. He went to bed early, kept his room tidy and lifted weights. Six years after the injury, his college named him its top male athlete. So small habits, repeated daily, can rebuild a life.

The book is a manual for building good habits and breaking bad ones.

To break a habit

Flip each of the four laws: make it invisible, unattractive, difficult and unsatisfying. Lessons 4 to 7 each have a box for it.

What you'll learn

Tiny gains add up

Small habits look like nothing on any one day, but they add up. Build a daily system, not just a goal.

Before 2003, British road cyclists had won just one Olympic gold medal since 1908. Then Dave Brailsford took over British Cycling. His plan was to improve everything by 1 percent. How many of the available cycling gold medals did Britain win at the 2008 Olympics?

His team hunted for a tiny gain in everything. They tested massage gels. They taught riders how to wash their hands, to avoid colds. Together, the small gains took British cycling from almost no golds to most of them.

The math shows why. Get 1 percent better every day for a year, and you end up 37 times better. Get 1 percent worse every day, and you fall close to zero. Clear calls habits the compound interest of self-improvement: growth that builds on itself, the way interest earns interest.

But results come late. At first you see no change, and many people quit. Clear calls this the Valley of Disappointment. Your work is not wasted. It is stored, until you cross what Clear calls the Plateau of Latent Potential, the point where the hidden gains finally show.

So focus on systems, not goals. A goal is the result you want. A system is the daily process that leads to it. Every Olympian wants gold, so goals do not separate winners from losers. As Clear says, “You fall to the level of your systems.”

Try this. Ask three questions about your week. Am I spending less than I earn? Am I going to the gym? Am I learning something new?

Habits shape who you are

Lasting change starts with who you believe you are. Every action is a vote for that person.

There are three levels of change. Outcomes are what you get, like losing weight. Processes are what you do, like a new gym routine. Identity is what you believe about yourself. Most people start with outcomes. Clear says to start with identity.

Picture two people who are offered a cigarette. One says, “No thanks. I'm trying to quit.” That person still sees themselves as a smoker. The other says, “No thanks. I'm not a smoker.” That is a change of identity. So “I'm not a smoker” changes who you are, not just what you do today.

Brian Clark had chewed his nails since he was young. He stopped for a while, then got his first manicure. The manicurist said his nails were healthy and attractive. He felt proud of them, and he has never chewed them since. So pride in who he had become did what effort alone had not.

Your identity grows from evidence. Each page you write is a vote for being a writer. You do not need every vote, only most of them.

It takes two steps. First, choose who you want to become. Second, prove it with small wins.

The habit loop

Every habit runs through four steps: cue, craving, response and reward. Each of the four laws changes one step.

In 1898, Edward Thorndike put cats in puzzle boxes, boxes that open only when an animal works out the trick. To reach the food, a cat had to press a lever. In its first three trials, one cat took 1.5 minutes to escape. In its last three, it took 6.3 seconds.

The cat had built a habit: with practice, escaping became almost automatic. A habit is a behavior you repeat until it is automatic. It is a shortcut your brain learned, so you do not have to think each time.

Here is the loop with a phone. A buzz is the cue. You want to read the message, which is the craving. You pick up the phone and read it, which is the response. You feel satisfied, which is the reward. Soon a buzz makes you reach for the phone.

If any step is missing, the habit fails. With no cue, it never starts. With no craving, you have no reason to act. If it is too hard, you will not do it. If the reward does not satisfy you, you will not repeat it. Each law changes one step: the first, the cue; the second, the craving; the third, the response; the fourth, the reward.
