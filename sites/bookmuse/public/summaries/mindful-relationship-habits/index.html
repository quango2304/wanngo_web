Love needs tending

Love fades mostly from lack of attention, and a couple can bring it back by choosing small, mindful habits, one at a time.

Barrie Davenport and S.J. Scott know from their own past marriages how a couple slides into disconnection. They say most relationships come apart slowly, from lack of attention and effort, not from one big blow. Their answer is to be mindful, which simply means to pay attention on purpose, and to build small habits that show it. The book has 25 habits, but seven ideas carry them.

One small habit at a time

Pick one tiny habit, tie it to something you already do, and give it a month.

Think back to when you first fell in love. You may have tidied your home, got in shape or quit a bad habit, just to impress your partner. Love made you try. The authors ask a simple question: can you win some of that back by choosing loving habits now, on purpose? They think so. You may not feel the same thrill, but you can warm up the closeness you have. Even a small change often shows results right away.

The book gives an eight-step plan. Four of its steps are the core.

  1. One habit at a time. Your willpower is shared by every part of your life, so one habit gets all of it.
  2. Give it a month. People say 21 days, others say 66. It varies, so the book says to commit for 30 days.
  3. Tie it to something you already do. For example, when you get home from work, you share a quiet meal, free from distractions, and talk about your day.
  4. Make it tiny. A foot rub during TV, phones off at dinner, making your partner's favorite hot drink in the morning. It should be so easy that you cannot miss a day.

Then plan for the bad days. The book calls this if-then planning. One example: when anger or resentment shows up, you will take a short break of five minutes.

Not sure where to start? The book says to pick the area that causes the most conflict.

A busy couple never fights, but they say little that matters. What usually happens to them?

On a typical day, many couples rush out, text once or twice, come home to chores, eat in front of the TV and fall asleep having said little that matters. No one fights. Still, the two of you are not really together. Psychologist John Gottman found that couples who grow distant do split, after about 16 years on average.

The book's first habit is a regular relationship meeting. Hold it weekly or twice a month, for up to an hour, and put it in both calendars. It is only about the two of you, not the kids, your to-do lists or your next vacation. In a hard time, meet every day until things are back on track.

Shared rituals work the same way. A ritual is anything you do together in the same way, such as a long hug when you both get home. Rituals keep a relationship from running on autopilot. Dates help too. In one study, couples with a monthly date night were 14% less likely to break up.

To break a habit · Put the phone away

Agree where devices stay out: at meals, in bed, and for the first 30 minutes after you meet again after work. Begin with one. When you get into bed, turn both phones to silent and put them away, then cuddle and talk.

Be kind first

Treat your partner at least as well as you would treat your boss or your best friend.

When Barrie's children were teenagers, they sometimes spoke to her unkindly. She reminded them they would never talk to another adult that way. They said they felt safe being angry with her because they knew, “Mom will always love me.” The authors say couples do the same. We let off steam on the person who will always be there. So the person we love most often gets our worst manners.

This is psychologist John Gottman's finding. Most couples do not make up for each hurt, so the bad feelings pile up and wear away trust. He also named four habits that do the most harm.

  1. Criticism: says your partner's character is the problem, not one behavior.
  2. Contempt: looks down on your partner, with sneering, sarcasm, eye-rolling or name-calling.
  3. Defensiveness: pushes the blame back or plays the victim.
  4. Stonewalling: shuts down, or answers in very short words.

Kindness also means accepting your partner. Your partner has had the same flaws all along. In the early days you overlooked them. What changed is how you see them. You can still ask for change, if you ask with respect.

Kindness lands best in the form your partner feels. To cherish someone is to hold them dear, like a treasure. So ask what being cherished means to them. The book also borrows Gary Chapman's five love languages: words of affirmation, quality time, gifts, acts of service and physical touch. Asking directly is the clearest way to learn your partner's.
