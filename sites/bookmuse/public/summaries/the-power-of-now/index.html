The Power of Now

A summary of the book by Eckhart Tolle

7 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Stop living in your head

You are not your mind. The endless thinking in your head, and the old pain it feeds on, cause most of your suffering, and you end it by watching them and giving this moment your full attention.

Eckhart Tolle's book first came out in 1997. He says that at 29, after years of anxiety, his mind went quiet one night and he lived in deep peace for months. The book is his guide to that change. Its claims are spiritual, and many are his own, so the lessons say “in Tolle's view” where that matters.

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

You are not your thoughts

You are not the voice in your head. You are the one who can hear it.

Tolle tells how he lived in almost constant anxiety until he was 29. One night a thought kept repeating: “I cannot live with myself any longer.” Then he saw how odd it was. If he could not live with himself, there must be two of him: the “I” and the self it could not stand. He says his mind stopped. At dawn he woke to a bird's song, and everything looked fresh. For the next five months he lived in deep peace. So the voice that suffers is one thing, and the one who sees it is another.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Close your eyes and tell yourself: “I wonder what my next thought will be.” Then wait, as a cat waits at a mouse hole. What happens?

Almost everyone has a voice in the head that never stops. It comments, judges, complains and compares. It replays the past and rehearses the future. When it imagines things going wrong, that is worry. Most people take this voice for themselves.

So listen to the voice, and do not judge it. Judging is the same voice coming back in. You will soon notice two things: the voice, and you, hearing it. That watching attention is what Tolle calls presence. He says this is the first moment of freedom. The thought loses its power, and a gap of quiet opens. At first it lasts a few seconds.

Tolle calls the false self built from this thinking the ego. It is the self you think you are, made of your thoughts about your past and your story.

The book's main advice. The most important step is to stop taking your mind for yourself. Each gap you make in the stream of thought makes your awareness stronger.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Your inner voice says: “You always get this wrong.” You reply, “No, I get plenty right,” and the voice answers back. What just happened?

Try this. Pick a routine task. Walking up the stairs is one. Give every step your full attention, even your breathing. The sign you are doing it right, he says, is the peace you feel.

Old pain wakes up

Old pain does not just fade. In Tolle's view, it gathers in you, wakes up and feeds on more pain, and watching it takes away its power.

When you do not meet a painful feeling with full attention, a leftover stays in your mind and body. Leftovers from many hurts join together, hurts from childhood too. He calls the whole pile the pain-body. He says you come close to the truth if you think of it as an invisible being of its own.

It has two states: asleep and awake. When it is asleep, it stays quiet. Almost anything can wake it, such as a thought or a remark from someone close. Watch for signs in yourself: irritation, impatience, a dark mood, a wish to hurt, or a need for drama.

Once it is awake, it wants to feed. He says it feeds only on pain, such as anger, drama and hate. To do that, it needs you to believe it is you. So it fills your head with angry thoughts, and your thinking starts to agree with it. It cannot feed on joy.

It also fears your attention. Its life depends on you not looking at it. So the way out is not to fight it, which only makes more inner conflict. You watch it, with presence: awake, alert attention. Watching means you accept that it is there.

Tolle sums up the method like this:

  1. Feel it. Put your attention on the feeling inside you.
  2. Name it. Say to yourself that this is the pain-body, and accept that it is there.
  3. Do not think about it. Do not judge it or work it out.
  4. Do not own it. Do not make it part of who you are.
  5. Stay present. Watch the feeling, and also the one who is watching.
In your life

A friend says, “You look tired,” and you feel a flash of anger. The remark was small, but the feeling is big. In Tolle's terms, something old woke up.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

You are angry, and you keep thinking about what they did and what you will do back. What are your thoughts doing?

Try this. Catch the feeling the moment it wakes. Next time you feel irritation or a dark mood, feel it inside your body. Stay with it as the watcher, and see what happens.

Now is all there is

Everything you have lived, and everything you will live, happens now. Past and future are only thoughts you have now.

Have you ever done, felt or thought anything outside the Now? The book says no. What you call the past happened in a Now. What you call the future will happen in a Now.

In Tolle's view, a memory is a trace stored in your mind, and you call it up now. The future is a picture your mind makes, and you make it now. Tolle compares them to the moon, which has no light of its own and only reflects the sun. Past and future borrow their reality from the Now.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

You lie awake dreading tomorrow's meeting. When is the dread happening?

Tolle does not say to drop time. He splits it in two. Clock time is the useful kind: appointments, learning from mistakes and goals. Psychological time is when your sense of who you are rests on the past, and your hopes sit in the future.

Clock timeYou learn from a mistake and use the lesson now. You work toward a goal and give your full attention to the step you are taking.
Psychological timeYou keep thinking about the mistake and feel guilt. You focus so hard on the goal that this moment is only a step toward something else.

Fear, he says, is about something that might happen, never about what is happening now. You can cope with now. You cannot cope with the future, because it exists only in your mind.

So Tolle asks you to tell your life from your life situation. Your life situation is your story: what went wrong, what you hope for. It lives in time. Your life is happening now. He asks: do you have a problem right now? Not tomorrow, not in ten minutes. Now.

Try this. Use your senses. Look around, and just look, without naming what you see. Listen to the sounds without judging them, then to the silence under them. Touch something. Feel your breathing.
