Fair rules, chosen blind

The fair rules for a society are the ones we would pick if we did not know where we would end up in it.

Part 7 ended on Kant's idea that a law is just if the whole public could have agreed to it. Here John Rawls, an American philosopher, builds a full answer on that idea. He also answers the ideas of Parts 2 and 4: make people as happy as possible, and let people keep what they earn.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

When a deal is not fair

A deal is not fair just because both sides agreed to it.

Rosa is a freelance designer, and she needs work. A studio offers her a contract. It pays late, and it lets the studio cancel at any time. Rosa has bills due and no other offer. She signs. Nobody forced her. Is the contract fair?

Many people say yes, because she agreed. But look at who sat at the table. The studio knew the market and could wait. Rosa could not wait. Her yes came from her need. The signature does not show that the terms are fair. To judge the terms, we need a standard that stands outside the deal.

A deal gets its moral weight from two things. First, it is chosen freely, with nobody pressing you. Second, both sides gain, so each gives back as much as it gets. This second idea is called reciprocity. A real deal often has less of both than it seems.

So a signature is weak evidence of fairness. It shows a yes. It does not show a fair yes.

Consent matters, but it is not the whole of justice. For the rules of a whole society, we need a deal where nobody holds more power or knows more. Rawls asks us to imagine one.

In your life

The next time someone says “but you agreed,” ask what you could have done instead of agreeing. The answer tells you how free the yes was.

The veil of ignorance

Rules chosen by people who do not know their own place are fair, because no one can push a deal their way.

A block of neighbors shares an empty lot. They will split it into garden plots and write rules. Who waters? Who gets the sunny corner? Who pays for seeds? If the plots were already handed out, the neighbor with the sunny corner would want rules that keep it. Everyone would argue from their own spot.

Now change one thing. The plots will be given out by lottery after the rules are written. Nobody knows who will get the sunny corner or the damp strip by the bins. What rules would you write? Probably rules you could live with on any plot.

That is Rawls's thought experiment, the veil of ignorance. You choose the rules for a whole society while a veil hides who you will be: your class, race, gender, talents, health, beliefs and family. You choose as a person who looks after their own interests. You also set aside your moral and religious views for this one test. Rawls does not claim that people are selfish in real life.

Why does this give fair rules? The veil removes the unequal power and knowledge that spoiled Rosa's deal. Nobody can use a strong position, even by accident. So whatever everyone agrees to behind the veil is fair.

But how can a deal that never happened bind anyone? Nobody signed it, and Rawls does not say anyone did. Its force comes from being a deal with no unfair edge. That makes it a test for real deals, which may have one.

Two rules we would choose

Behind the veil, people would choose equal basic freedoms first, then allow only the gaps that help the poorest.

What would you pick from behind the veil? Rawls says two ideas from this course would not survive it.

The first is the rule from Part 2: do whatever gives the most happiness in total. But you might land in a small group that the majority is happy to push around. Nobody wants to be the person whose loss pays for everyone else's pleasure. The second is the idea from Part 4: each person keeps whatever the market gives. You might land among the richest. You might land among the poorest, with no help. Most people would not risk that. So they would protect the worst place they might land.

So Rawls says two rules would come out. The first gives equal basic freedoms to everyone, such as free speech and freedom of religion. These come first. The wish to make most people happier may not override them. The second rule is about money and power. It does not demand equal incomes. It allows a gap only if the gap helps the least well off. Rawls calls this the difference principle.

Plan A is perfectly equal. In Plan B some people earn more, and the extra work and taxes raise the pay of the poorest above Plan A. In Plan C the gap is huge, the top keeps almost everything, and the poorest fall below Plan A. The difference principle prefers Plan B, where the poorest do best, and rules out Plan C. It never says that a gap is fair in itself. It asks who gains from it. And it judges the whole system of taxes and schools, not one person's pay.

Critics ask whether people behind the veil would be so careful. Some might gamble on landing on top. Rawls doubts it. With a whole life at stake, a bet on landing on top only makes sense for a person who loves risk, and nobody behind the veil can know who that is.
