Outliers

A summary of the book by Malcolm Gladwell

8 lessons · 20 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Success has a backstory

Successful people did not rise on talent and hard work alone. Hidden chances, the year they were born and the culture they came from helped make them.

Malcolm Gladwell, a writer for The New Yorker, studies outliers: people far outside the ordinary. He opens with Roseto, a small town in Pennsylvania. In the late 1950s a local doctor said he rarely saw anyone from there under sixty-five with heart disease. A visiting doctor ruled out diet, exercise, genes and place. The cause was the town itself: its close, caring community. Gladwell asks the same of success: where are successful people from?

A small head start grows

A tiny early edge can grow into a huge gap, because the ones who look best get the most chances.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Which birth months do the best junior hockey players in Canada come from most?

The reason is a cutoff date. Canadian hockey groups children by birth year, and the year starts on January 1. A boy born in January can play beside one born in December. At nine or ten, that gap of almost twelve months is a big gap in size and skill.

Coaches pick the bigger boys for the all-star team. They get better coaching and teammates, play many more games, fifty or seventy-five a season, not twenty, and practice more. By thirteen or fourteen they really are better. One top team in 2007 had seventeen of its twenty-five players born in the first four months.

Schools do the same. Teachers put older children in the fast groups, mistaking age for ability.

A sociologist named this the Matthew Effect, after a Bible verse that says those who have will be given more. So the top players did not simply rise on talent. A small edge brought chances, and the chances made the edge bigger.

The fix is simple. Set up two or three leagues divided by birth month, so children play against others of the same maturity.

Ten thousand hours of practice

Gladwell argues that world-class skill takes about ten thousand hours of practice, and few people get the chance to put in that many.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

At a top music school in Berlin, violinists were asked how many hours they had practiced by age twenty. Next to the future music teachers, the future soloists had practiced…

Psychologist K. Anders Ericsson led this study in the early 1990s. All the violinists began at about five and practiced two or three hours a week. The researchers found no naturals who practiced little and still reached the top. Around eight, the future stars pulled ahead. At fourteen they practiced sixteen hours a week, and at twenty, over thirty.

Studies of experts in many fields, Gladwell says, point to the same number: ten thousand hours. The neurologist he quotes admits the number cannot say why some people gain more from practice.

But ten thousand hours is huge, and almost no one gets there alone. In 1968 Bill Gates's private school in Seattle bought a computer terminal that let students program in real time. When the school's money ran out, companies gave the students free computer time in exchange for work. In one seven-month stretch, Gates and his friends used 1,575 hours, about eight a day, every day. By Gladwell's count, Gates was far past ten thousand hours before he left Harvard. Gates calls it a lucky series of events.

The Beatles had a version too. In 1960 the band got an invitation to Hamburg, in Germany, and played for hours every night. Lennon said that in Liverpool they played one-hour sets, but in Hamburg, eight hours. By 1964 they had played live about twelve hundred times.

Born at the right time

The year you are born decides which doors are open when you are ready to walk through them.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Historians listed the seventy-five richest people in all of history, from pharaohs to billionaires. How many were Americans born within the same nine years?

Why that group? Two decades, the 1860s and 1870s, remade the American economy with railroads, Wall Street and big factories. Those born in the late 1840s were too young to use that moment. Those born in the 1820s were too old, their minds set by the old economy. The fourteen include John D. Rockefeller and Andrew Carnegie.

Computers had a narrow window too. In January 1975 a magazine showed the first computer kit that ordinary people could build at home, for $397. Older people already had safe jobs at big firms that made room-sized computers, such as IBM. Younger ones were still in school. The best age was about twenty.

Timing also helped Joe Flom, a famous New York lawyer. In the 1940s the old Wall Street law firms turned away Jewish graduates, even the best. Flom's small firm took the work they looked down on, such as hostile takeovers, where one company tries to buy another that does not want to be sold. In the 1970s that work boomed, after about twenty years of Flom getting good at it.

Demographic luck is about the size of a generation. Families had fewer children in the Depression, so 18.7 babies were born per 1,000 people in 1935, against 29.5 in 1915. Those children met roomy new schools, then a market with jobs to spare.

The book sums up Flom's rise in three parts. This lesson teaches the first two, and the third has its own lesson.

  1. “The Importance of Being Jewish” Shut out of old firms, they took work nobody wanted.
  2. “Demographic Luck” Born when few babies were born, they met roomy schools and many jobs.
  3. “The Garment Industry and Meaningful Work” Their parents' work taught them to shape their world.
