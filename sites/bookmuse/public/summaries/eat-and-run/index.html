Eat & Run

A summary of the book by Scott Jurek and Steve Friedman

6 lessons · 16 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

A boy who learned to keep going

Strength to keep going is built by doing hard things one piece at a time, with good food, a calm mind and friends, and the journey matters more than the prize.

Scott Jurek grew up poor in Minnesota, cooking and stacking wood while his mother grew weaker from multiple sclerosis. He became an ultramarathon runner, someone who races farther than a marathon, which is 26.2 miles. He won the 100-mile Western States race seven years in a row on a plant-based diet. He wrote this book with Steve Friedman to show how he did it and how you can too.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Sometimes you just do things

Strength to keep going is built by doing hard things, starting small, without waiting to feel ready.

When Scott complained about chores, his father said, "Sometimes you just do things!" Scott's mother had multiple sclerosis, an illness that slowly weakens the body, so he did more and more at home. By ten he could cook a pot roast alone. His father also turned chores into games. How much wood could he haul in ten minutes? How many rocks could he pick from the garden? A race against the clock made dull work exciting, and finishing a hard job made Scott feel happy.

His first race showed it. In fourth grade he had never run a mile. By halfway he was twentieth out of twenty-five, boxed in and cramping. He kept going and broke away from the pack. With a quarter mile left, four runners were ahead, and then one. He did not win. But he saw that while other kids slowed down, he made up ground. He later wrote that his parents had been training him to be an endurance athlete, and that he knew how to suffer before he ever ran.

Pain was part of it. In his second 50-mile race his friend Dusty told him to let the pain go out his ears. Scott did the opposite. He listened to the pain and used it to keep going. So pain became something to work with, not a sign to stop. Starting a run needs no technique at all, the book says, so you can begin at any distance.

Priya wants to run but has not started. She says, "I will begin when I know my form." What fits the book?

Try this. Keep the pace slow enough to talk. The book says 50 to 70 percent of your top effort, and that if you cannot talk, you are going too fast. Start with 50 yards if that is all you can handle, and go farther tomorrow.

Food is who we are

Change what you eat in steps. His biggest advice: replace the calories you give up.

As a physical therapy student, Jurek did an internship at a hospital. There he watched a sick old man look at his lunch tray: Salisbury steak, instant potatoes and canned peas. The man said nothing. Jurek thought, "Food is who we are." He had noticed that eating better made him faster. Could eating badly make people sick?

He did not change overnight. At a ski camp as a teenager he ate salads and whole-wheat bread and felt stronger. In college he swapped summer sausage for cheese and spinach. Later he ate mostly plants. Before his first Western States race he stopped eggs, butter, milk and fish too. Giving up some foods led him to new ones. He is not strict: he ate cheese in Europe and beans cooked with lard when little else was offered, and says he will not starve for his principles.

He says he then felt lighter and faster. The soreness after long runs went away, and he needed less rest between hard workouts. His father said a runner needs steak, and his grandfather said no one could live on fruits and nuts. Jurek says he cannot be sure which part of the diet helped. The book also says he is not a doctor, so these are his results, not a promise.

Tap an answer

Many people worry about getting enough protein from plants. How much protein does the average American aged 19 to 30 eat, compared with the amount advised each day?

On protein, he learned that the body pools its amino acids, the building blocks of protein, over the whole day. So he did not have to match foods at every meal. He says a varied whole-foods diet with enough calories gives enough protein. His own day has a smoothie with nuts and plant protein powder, a big raw salad with tofu, tempeh or hummus, and beans with whole grains at dinner.

His biggest challenge is calories, not protein. Plants such as salad leaves are light, so he adds dense foods: nuts, nut butters, seeds, avocados, root vegetables such as potatoes, coconut milk and oils.

Run by feel, then check

Run by feel and joy, then check the results with a few simple measures.

In 2006 Jurek raced the Tarahumara, also called the Raramuri, a native people of Mexico's Copper Canyon who are known for running. They wore sandals patched with old tire rubber and carried no water bottles. They did no speed workouts. They ran and ate to live, and their steps were short, light and never wasted. Jurek, then at the top of his career, lost the 50-mile race to their champion Arnulfo by six minutes. The next year he won by eighteen.

The Raramuri were not against tools. They rode in a pickup truck, and they fixed their sandals with tire rubber. This matched what Jurek had learned in Seattle, where a lab measured his breathing and he logged every meal. He ran with wild freedom, he says, but checked the results, and the more he checked, the more he trusted his instincts.

One number the book gives is stride rate. Count how many times your right foot lands in 20 seconds, then multiply by three. Elite distance runners, he writes, take 85 to 90 strides or more a minute, and a stride is two steps. The most common mistake is overstriding: big, slow steps that land on the heel. That keeps the foot on the ground longer and hits the joints harder. Short, light, quick steps are safer and waste less energy.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Sam's training plan says to run hard today. His legs feel heavy and sore. What fits Jurek's way?

Even with a training plan, he takes a day off when he feels he needs one. The plan serves him, not the other way round.

Try this. Run three or more times a week, 30 to 45 minutes each time, for six to eight weeks. Then add hard running at 85 to 90 percent of your capacity for 5 minutes, followed by 1 minute of easy jogging. Keep that 5 to 1 ratio as you build.
