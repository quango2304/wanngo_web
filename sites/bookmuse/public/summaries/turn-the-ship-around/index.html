Turn the Ship Around!

A summary of the book by L. David Marquet

8 lessons · 20 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Leaders at every level

In Marquet's account, people treated as followers act like followers; give them control, plus the competence and the clarity to use it, and they act like leaders.

In 1999 L. David Marquet took command of the nuclear submarine USS Santa Fe, and he led it until 2001. Its crew was at the bottom of the fleet. Within a year the ship went from worst to first on most measures. He fired no one. He changed who made the decisions.

He calls the old way leader-follower: a few decide and the rest obey. His way is leader-leader, where everyone leads.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Followers act like followers

People who are treated as followers act like followers. Marquet's answer is to change how decisions are made, not to add a program.

Before taking command of the submarine USS Santa Fe, Marquet walked the ship and asked people what they did. A mid-ranking sailor on watch in the engine room answered, “Whatever they tell me to do.”

The answer stung because it was true. Of 135 men aboard, only 5 were using their full minds. Everything, from the end-of-day check-out to the way messages were routed, told the crew that the top people led and everyone else followed. The ship was at the bottom of the fleet, and only three sailors had chosen to stay in 1998.

He had tried this on an earlier submarine, the Will Rogers, by giving his engineers more say. But the captain above him still ran everything on “do what you are told.” It failed.

The old way lasts because it works for a while. A captain is graded on his ship until the day he leaves, so leaders are rewarded for being needed. The cost shows later: a good ship could turn bad overnight when a new captain came aboard.

Give control, keep responsibility

Move each decision to the person who knows the most about it. The leader gives up control but keeps responsibility.

On his first afternoon in command, he met the chiefs, the senior enlisted men who act as middle managers. They admitted they did not really run the ship, and that they wanted to. He asked what would make it true. No speeches, only changes to real practices.

They wanted control of their men's leave. A request for time off needed seven signatures, more than the form had lines for. The chiefs proposed that the chief of the boat (COB), the top enlisted man, sign last. Marquet crossed out one word, XO (the second in command), in the ship's rules and wrote COB. The rules were not his to change, so within a day he had gone beyond his authority. He called it Chiefs in Charge.

One word moved a chain of work. To own leave, the chiefs had to own the watch schedule, and so how sailors qualified for their posts. Marquet gave something away too: officer leave went to the second in command. He kept accountability for the ship.

The same idea ended a binder. A “tickler,” a thick binder of every message the ship owed, was reviewed by the department heads each week. It showed what was late but did not get it done. Asked who was responsible for his department, one head said, “I am, sir.” The binder went, and each head tracked his own work.

Tap an answer

A hotel manager signs off on every guest refund. The front desk sees what each guest needs. What fits Marquet's view?

Try this. Pick one decision your team waits on you for and finish: “When I think about delegating this decision, I worry that …” Marquet found the worries come down to competence or clarity, which come next.

Say what you intend to do

Do not ask “may I?” State what you intend to do, with your reasons. The leader then only has to say “Very well.”

In an early drill the reactor was shut down on purpose, and the ship ran slowly on a small electric motor, the EPM. Bored, Marquet suggested that the officer in charge of the watch go “ahead two thirds.” The officer ordered it, though the EPM has no such setting, and the helmsman said so. Marquet asked the officer why he had ordered it. “Because you told me to.” The officer had thought the captain knew something secret.

The danger of a top-down crew: when the leader errs, the whole crew errs with him. Marquet vowed never to give an order again. Instead, officers told him their plans with “I intend to …” and he answered “Very well.”

Soon he asked for the reasons the plan was safe, so a plain “Very well” was all he had to say. To give them, officers had to think like the captain. He also asked everyone to think out loud, sharing what they saw and doubted.

The leader has a job too. When the leader always supplies the answer, nobody else needs to think ahead. Marquet once told the night crew where to be at 0600 and woke to find the ship miles out of position: they had let passing ships push them away from his spot. When you feel the urge to give the answer, use three cases:

  1. It cannot wait. Decide, then ask the team to test it.
  2. It can wait a little. Ask for views, even briefly, then decide.
  3. It can wait longer. Make the team give views, and do not force everyone to agree.
