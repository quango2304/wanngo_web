The Simple Path to Wealth

A summary of the book by J.L. Collins

7 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Spend less, invest the rest, stay calm

Spend less than you earn, avoid debt, and put the rest in one low-cost fund that owns the whole stock market. Then stay calm when it falls.

This book grew out of letters J.L. Collins wrote to his daughter, after he learned about money the hard way. He started working at 13 and made many investing mistakes. His goal is not retirement. It is freedom: enough money to say no. These are his views, and he says himself that nothing here is guaranteed.

Money buys freedom

The best thing money buys is freedom: enough saved that you can say no.

The book opens with a short story. Two boyhood friends grow up. One becomes a monk, the other a minister to the king. The minister feels sorry for the thin monk and says that if he learned to please the king, he could stop eating rice and beans. The monk says that if the minister learned to live on rice and beans, he would not have to please the king. Collins sides with the monk. Needing less is a kind of freedom.

He calls enough money to be free of other people's demands “F-You Money.” He first had some at 25. After two years at $10,000 a year, he had saved $5,000. He wanted four months of unpaid leave to travel in Europe. His boss said no, so he resigned. Then the boss said not to do anything hasty, and talked to the owner. They agreed on six weeks of leave. Collins also won a month of vacation each year. His savings paid for the trip and gave him room to bargain.

The book's whole plan is one line: “Spend less than you earn, invest the surplus, avoid debt.” He suggests trying to save and invest half of your income, which he says is doable with no debt. He also warns about lifestyle inflation, when your spending grows with every raise. He suggests that raises go into investments. A high savings rate works twice. You have more to invest, and you learn to live on less.

Your boss offers you a worse schedule. With a few months of pay saved, you can weigh it. With nothing saved, you say yes.

Debt comes first

Debt is the biggest obstacle to wealth. If you owe money, paying it off comes before everything else.

Two years after college, Collins got his first credit card. The first month he spent about $300. The bill said only $10 was due. He thought it was a great deal, but his older sister pointed to the fine print. The card charged 18% interest on the $290 left unpaid. A small payment hid a big cost. Lenders, he says, assume we will not notice.

Debt hurts in several ways, he says. Part of your pay is already spent on interest. You depend on your paycheck, so you cannot choose freely. And the stress builds. Most people see debt as normal. He calls it a destroyer of wealth with no place in your financial life.

If you owe money, here is how he would clear it:

  1. List every debt. Write down each one and its interest rate.
  2. Cut all non-essential spending. Every coffee and dinner out adds up.
  3. Pay the minimum on every debt. Put all the rest on the highest rate first.
  4. Move down the list. When one is gone, start on the next.

He would not pay a service to help. He would not pay the smallest debts first just for the mood boost, though he accepts it if it keeps you going. The plan is simple but not easy. The good news is that once debt is gone, you can send the same money to investments.

Even a mortgage is often called “good debt.” He is wary. A bigger house also costs more in taxes, insurance, repairs and furniture. So look for the least house that meets your needs, not the most you can technically afford.

Money that works for you

Stop asking what your money can buy. Ask what it can earn.

Mike Tyson was one of the toughest boxers ever. After earning some $300 million, he went bankrupt. His lifestyle was said to cost $400,000 a month. Collins's view is that Tyson knew money only as a way to buy things. He never learned to think about what money can do on its own.

His way is to see that spent money is gone, and so is what it could have earned. He calls this “opportunity cost”: what you give up when you put your money in one thing instead of another. To measure it, he uses 8%, the low end of the stock market's usual yearly return.

You may know “compounding”: the interest you earn earns interest too, like a snowball that grows as it rolls. Opportunity cost is its evil twin. Borrowing or leasing the car makes it worse. In his words, you are saying you want to pay much more than $20,000.

None of this means never spend. It means knowing the true price when you do.
