The Art of War

A summary of the book by Sun Tzu

8 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Win before the fight begins

The best win costs the least: you prepare, you learn what the enemy will do, and you win before the fight starts, or without a fight at all.

Sun Tzu was a general in ancient China, for the king of the state of Wu. His book is short: thirteen chapters on how to plan and win a war. Its biggest idea is to spend as little as you can, and to win by knowing more than the enemy does.

The book speaks only about war and rulers.

What you'll learn

The thirteen chapters

Count the odds first

A fight is mostly decided before it starts, by how well you have counted the odds.

Sun Tzu opens with a warning. War decides whether a state lives or dies. It is the road to safety or to ruin, so it can never be ignored. His answer is to count. The general who wins a battle has made many calculations in his temple before the fight. The general who loses has made few. Someone who made none is worse off still.

He counts with five factors that every general should know:

  • The Moral Law: the people follow their leader without fear, whatever the danger.
  • Heaven: night and day, heat and cold, the seasons.
  • Earth: distances, safe and dangerous ground, open fields and narrow passes.
  • The Commander: wisdom, honesty, kindness, courage and strictness.
  • Method and discipline: how the army is organized, how supplies reach it, and how money is spent.

Then he compares the two sides with seven questions. Try them on a contest of your own.

These seven questions, he claims, let him forecast victory or defeat. He gives no scoring rule. Use the count to see where you are ahead, and where you have not looked yet.

In your life

You and one other person want the same job. Who is better trained? Who knows the company better? Who gets there at the better time? The answers may show a gap you can still close.

Try this. Before a big move, answer the seven questions for both sides, as Sun Tzu does.

Win without a fight

A long fight costs more than it wins, so the best win breaks the enemy's will without a battle.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Sun Tzu lists four ways to attack. Which one does he call the worst?

First, the cost. In Sun Tzu's day, an army of 100,000 men cost a thousand ounces of silver every day. If victory is slow, weapons go dull, men lose their fire, and the money runs out. Then other chiefs spring up and use your weak moment. No country has ever gained from a long war. The aim is victory, not a long campaign.

So what is the top skill? He says it is not winning every battle. It is "breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting". Taking a country whole is better than wrecking it.

Why are cities the worst? The figure shows the cost.

A general who cannot control his irritation sends his men up the walls like ants.

In your life

Two shops on one street start a price war. Both lose money every day, and a third shop quietly takes the customers.

Try this. Before you start a fight, ask what it will cost each day, and whether you can win a better way, by spoiling the other side's plan first.

Make yourself safe first

You cannot control the enemy's mistakes, but you can control your own, so make yourself safe and then wait.

The skilled fighters of long ago began by making defeat impossible. Only then did they wait for the moment to beat the enemy. Being safe is in your own hands. The chance to win is given by the enemy. So a good fighter can make himself safe, but cannot make sure of winning.

A win like this looks easy. It earns no fame for wisdom or courage, because the fighter wins by making no mistakes. And a victory that the whole country cheers is not the best kind.

What can make you unsafe from the inside? A general has five faults to watch for. When an army falls and its leader is killed, the cause will be among them. He also advises you to anger an enemy who has a quick temper.

  • Recklessness leads to destruction.
  • Cowardice leads to capture.
  • A hasty temper can be provoked by insults.
  • A touchy sense of honor is hurt by shame.
  • Too much worry for his men brings him trouble.

Try this. Rely on being ready, not on the other side staying away.
