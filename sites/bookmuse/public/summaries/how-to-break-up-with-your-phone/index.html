How to Break Up with Your Phone

A summary of the book by Catherine Price

7 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

A phone that serves you

Phones are built to hook your attention, so willpower alone is not enough. A 30-day plan can let you keep what you love about your phone and decide for yourself what gets your attention.

Catherine Price is a science journalist. After she got her first smartphone in 2010, her attention span seemed shorter and her memory weaker, and she came to suspect her phone. She read the research and built a plan. Nearly 150 volunteers, aged 21 to 73, tried it and gave feedback. The first half of the book shows how phones hook us. The second half is the plan.

Built to hook you

Your phone is built to hook you. If you can't put it down, the design is working, and it is not a lack of willpower.

Tap an answer

What keeps you checking your phone more?

A reporter asked Steve Jobs if his children liked the iPad. “They haven't used it,” he said, and the family limits technology at home. Many experts, Price writes, think these leaders know the risk of addiction. Companies want your time, because your attention is how they make money.

Dopamine is a brain chemical that links an action to a reward. Your brain learns the link, and soon it releases dopamine just from being reminded of the action. That is a craving. Sometimes you hope for something good. Just as often you want to escape boredom or worry. Either way, your brain learns that checking pays off. Rewards you can't predict hook you most, as a slot machine's do.

More tricks to know:

  1. Constant novelty. Something new waits in every app, so the dopamine keeps coming.
  2. Fear of missing out. Putting your phone down releases cortisol, a stress hormone. Checking calms it for a moment, then it returns.
  3. No stopping cues. Hunger fades when you feel full. Apps give no sign that you have had enough, and some start the next video five seconds after one ends.

Ramsay Brown sells code that helps app makers keep people in their apps. He told 60 Minutes that Instagram's code holds back some likes, then shows many at once, at the moment most likely to stop you leaving. He called users guinea pigs in a test run on millions of people. So a pull you can't resist may be design working as planned.

Try this. If your video app has autoplay, turn it off and see if you watch fewer videos.

Your attention is the product

Free apps are not free: advertisers are the customers, and your attention is what they buy.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Social media apps are free to use. How much do advertisers pay to reach the people using them?

Why are social media apps free? You are not the customer. The customers are advertisers, and the product is your attention. Ramsay Brown, who runs Dopamine Labs, puts it this way: “You don't pay for Facebook. Advertisers pay for Facebook.”

Companies call this engagement: the clicks, likes, shares and comments an app gets. In 2016, social media ad spending was $31 billion, almost double two years before. A New York Times analysis found that in 2014 Facebook users spent a combined 39,757 years of attention on the site each day. We experience and remember only what we pay attention to, so it is your life that is being sold, and you can't get it back.

The design pulls you in. Likes turn every post into a score you keep checking. Feeds are endless on purpose, because a stopping point could cut engagement. A 2017 study followed the same people over time. Liking others' content and clicking links predicted a later drop in their health and life satisfaction. Using an app by choice is fine. Using it because a trick pulls you is the problem.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Maya keeps checking the like count on her posts. What could she try?

Try this. If you chase likes, hide the like counts with a browser add-on for a while. Then ask why the app doesn't offer that itself.

Your brain becomes what you practice

What you do for hours changes your brain, and hours of scattered phone use train it to be scattered.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

London cab drivers must memorize roughly 25,000 streets. What did scans of their brains show?

To earn a licence, London cab drivers must pass a test called The Knowledge. They memorize roughly 25,000 streets and 320 common routes. In a 2000 study, Eleanor Maguire's team scanned their brains. The area for spatial memory was larger than in other people, and more so in drivers who had practiced longer. So what we practice reshapes the brain.

Now think about four hours a day. As of 2017, Americans spent more than that on their phones. Most of it is not deep thought. We pick the phone up for seconds or minutes, then scroll and swipe. Price calls it an intensely focused state of distraction, and she says this kind of use is especially good at changing the brain.

Price's summary of the research points to three costs:

  1. Switching. You can't do two hard tasks at once, so you switch between them. In a 2009 Stanford study, heavy multitaskers were worse at ignoring junk, keeping memories organized and switching tasks. They thought they were great at it.
  2. Skimming. Every link makes you decide in a split second whether to click. The more you read this way, the better you get at skimming and the worse at deep reading. Print has no links to decide on.
  3. Forgetting. Working memory is what you hold in mind right now. A phone floods it, so less moves into long-term memory.

None of this is permanent. Brains change with practice, so they can change back.
