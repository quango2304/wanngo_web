The 80/20 CEO

A summary of the book by Bill Canady

7 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Find the few that matter

A business gets stronger when its leaders find the small share of customers and products that bring most of the profit, put their effort there, and run a simple hundred-day system to get started.

Bill Canady grew up poor in North Carolina, joined the Navy, and later led several companies, often when they were in trouble. He built a set of steps for turning a business around and then growing it, and he calls it the profitable growth operating system. You get its main ideas, with his stories.

Face the facts, then act

Before you plan anything, learn the real state of the business, tell people the truth, and fix what is on fire.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Admiral Jim Stockdale led his fellow prisoners in a Vietnam War prison camp. Years later he was asked who did not make it out alive. Which group did he name?

Stockdale never doubted that he would get out and win in the end. The optimists kept naming release dates, and each missed date broke them. Stockdale also faced the hardest facts of his situation. The book calls this the Stockdale Paradox: keep your confidence, and keep “the discipline to confront the most brutal facts of your current reality.” So hope helps only when it sits next to hard facts.

Canady took this into business. In August 2021 he became CEO of Phoenix, a company that sold mechanical parts. Its sales, profit and morale were all falling. He went on what he calls a “Three L Tour”: listening, learning and using what he heard. He talked to everybody and asked plain questions. What is working? What is not? What do you like about your job?

Early in that turnaround he held a town hall for about 3,600 people in about forty places. He told them the truth: sales targets were being missed and costs were rising. Panic comes from not knowing what to do, so he gave people something to do. When a house is burning, you put the fire out first. Early wins lift morale and make people believe they can solve the rest.

Tap an answer

Dana takes over a bike shop whose cash is running out. Her team says sales will bounce back on their own after the holidays. What should she do first?

Try this. Before you meet a key person, send a short list of questions. Then meet three times: first to get acquainted, next to talk about the business, last for a deeper review. Study your notes between meetings.

The critical few

A small part of what you do brings most of your results, and the numbers can show you which part.

Vilfredo Pareto was an engineer, an economist and a keen gardener. In his garden he saw that about 20 percent of his pea plants gave about 80 percent of his healthy pods. He looked elsewhere. About 80 percent of Italy’s land belonged to about 20 percent of its people. About 80 percent of industrial output came from about 20 percent of its companies. The same lopsided split kept showing up. Today it is called the Pareto principle, or the 80/20 rule.

Business shows it too. About 20 percent of a company’s customers bring about 80 percent of its revenue, which is the money it takes in. About 20 percent of its products do the same. The book calls them “A” customers and “A” products. You find them by ranking: the A ones together make up the first 80 percent of sales. The rest are “B.”

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Cut a company’s products, ranked by sales, into four equal groups. How much of all sales does the best-selling group bring?

Pareto’s idea is not a judgment of you. It describes how effort and results spread, from pea plants to workers. The book even says about 80 percent of what you get done at work comes from about 20 percent of your time. A fact like this helps only if you use it. A report that only measures changes nothing. Look at the numbers, then put your resources where the results come from.

Try this. Take one year of sales numbers. Rank your products from the highest sales to the lowest and cut the list into four equal groups. Add up each group’s share of sales. Then do the same for your customers.

Four boxes, four treatments

Sort your customers and products into four boxes, then give each box a different level of effort.

Ranking products alone can fool you. Canady once helped cut the bottom half of a company’s products, and top customers sent letters about it. Some were “necessary evils”: items a company must offer because its best customers want them. So you also need to see who buys what.

The fix is to rank customers too. Mark each customer and each product “A” if it is in the first 80 percent of sales, and “B” if it is not. Then combine the two lists. You get four boxes. The book calls the best one the “Fort.”

Some managers feel this is unfair. The book says unequal is not unfair, because treating a big customer and a small one alike shortchanges your best. You will lose some B customers, and you want to: serving them takes effort from your best.

McDonald’s did something like this with its menu. In 2014 its sales were falling, and its menu had more than a hundred items. It cut five Extra Value Meals and eight other items. Too many choices slowed customers and workers, and fast-food customers come for speed. So McDonald’s went back to the meals most customers want most of the time.

For the lower boxes, the book lists twelve tools called the “Dirty Dozen.” Three of them:

  1. Set a minimum order (“All the Small Things”).
  2. Stop giving B customers discounts (“Can’t Buy Me Love”).
  3. Raise the price (“Ain’t No Mountain High Enough”). Customers can buy it, but at a high price.
QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

A small furniture maker finds that one slow-selling lamp loses money, but her best customers buy it with their sofas. What should she do?

Try this. Ask your B customers to order only on your website, pay by card up front, meet a minimum order, or pay for shipping.
