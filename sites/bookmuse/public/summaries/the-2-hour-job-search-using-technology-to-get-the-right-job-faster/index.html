The 2-Hour Job Search

A summary of the book by Steve Dalton

7 lessons · 17 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Find a person, not a posting

Online applications pile up unread, so jobs go to people who have someone inside speaking for them.

Steve Dalton is a career coach at the business school of Duke University. He saw very smart students apply to posting after posting with no result. His fix is a short routine in three steps: Prioritize, Contact and Recruit. The first two steps take about two hours, and the third runs on from there.

What you'll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Online applications go nowhere

Applying is now so easy that everyone does it, so employers hire from people they already know.

Becca was a top student at Dalton's school, and graduation was near. She had no job. In one last push she spent eight hours on a single night sending resumes to dozens of employers online. She never heard back from one of them.

Before the late 1990s you mailed a resume and waited. A recruiter got about a dozen applications over several weeks, mostly from nearby people. Now one click sends a resume. A recruiter gets hundreds or thousands within hours, from across the country. Nobody can read that many. Each “application submitted” message felt like progress to Becca, but it was not.

Hiring managers do not need the perfect person. A good one found fast is better for them. So they begin with referrals: people an employee vouches for. Dalton's answer has three steps:

  1. PrioritizeBuild a list of forty employers and rank it. About 70 minutes.
  2. ContactEmail one person at each top employer, then track replies. About 50 minutes.
  3. RecruitTalk with the people who answer, and keep in touch.

Dalton says he hates networking himself, but it is the only way to get a referral. Liking it is optional. Doing it with a plan is not.

Start with forty employers

Write down forty possible employers before you contact anyone, even ones you do not know.

On the TV show The Bachelor, twenty-five women compete for one man. Each has one chance in twenty-five. Waiting on one online posting feels the same. Dalton says to be the Bachelor instead: collect many choices, learn a little about each, and focus on the best.

Make the list long. People who name five soda brands tend to pick the best known, like Coke and Pepsi. A short job list holds only famous names too, and they draw the most applicants. In 2008, 99.9 percent of U.S. employers had fewer than five hundred workers. Most of your list will be dropped later, so a long list costs little.

Build it in four rounds. This is the L in LAMP, the book's method for ranking employers. Each lasts ten employers or ten minutes, whichever ends first:

  1. Dream employersName the ones you dream of, then their competitors.
  2. Alumni employersSee where graduates of your school work.
  3. Job postingsSearch a job site (Indeed.com, in the book's 2012 edition) for employers hiring now.
  4. TrendsSearch your field plus “trends,” and note the employers named.

Only copy the names. Do not stop to read about any of them. Adam, a former IT consultant, wanted to work in the mobile phone industry. The big firms had nothing for him. One contact sent him to the group that sets standards for all phone makers. It asked what he knew about the industry, and offered him a job the next day. A lesser-known employer meant less competition.

Score and sort your list

Give each employer three quick scores, then sort. Your best targets rise to the top.

The list is the L in LAMP: List, Alumni, Motivation, Posting. Scoring is quick on purpose. Each of the next three columns is a stand-in for something harder to learn.

  1. Alumni, 10 minutes. In your latest school's alumni (graduate) database, write Y if a graduate works at the employer, N if not. Copy no names. It stands in for finding a friendly contact.
  2. Motivation, 5 minutes. Score your favorite 5. Rate the rest against it: 4, 3 or 2. Use 1 for one you do not know. Look nothing up. It stands in for how hard you will work.
  3. Posting, 15 minutes. Search the employer plus a word for your job on a job site. A fitting job title scores 3, any job 2, none 1. Read only the results. It stands in for urgency.

Motivation comes first because it drives your effort. Alumni comes last because you can find other friendly contacts when no alum works there. Do not click into a posting while you score it. You can read it later, once the list shows which employers matter. This is the 80-20 rule at work, the book's idea that a small share of the actions gives most of the results. Here it gets about 80 percent of the benefit of research in 20 percent of the time.
