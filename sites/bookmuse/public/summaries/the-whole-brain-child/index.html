The Whole-Brain Child

A summary of the book by Daniel J. Siegel & Tina Payne Bryson

6 lessons · 18 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Surviving, and thriving too

A hard moment with your child is a chance to help the parts of their brain work together.

Daniel J. Siegel is a psychiatrist. Tina Payne Bryson is a child therapist. Together they turn brain science into twelve things a parent can do in the middle of a meltdown. You will learn a simple way to read your child’s behavior, and what to try for each kind of trouble.

What you’ll learn

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

Hard moments build the brain

A hard moment is a chance to help the parts of your child’s brain work together.

Siegel and Bryson call this teamwork integration. Think of your body. The lungs, heart and stomach each have a job, and you are healthy when they work as one. The brain is the same. One part thinks in logic, one feels, one reacts and one keeps memories. When they stop working together, you get tantrums and meltdowns.

The authors picture well-being as a river. In the middle the water is calm, and you can bend when things change. One bank is chaos, where you feel out of control. The other is rigidity, where you control everything and will not bend. A three-year-old who will not share his toy boat is near rigidity. If he then cries and throws sand when a friend takes it, he is near chaos.

Their pictures are simple on purpose. The authors say they use them for simplicity’s sake, and that the science can get complicated. These are a left side and a right side, and a house with an upstairs and a downstairs.

A child’s brain is not fully grown until the mid-twenties, and genes mostly set how fast it matures. You cannot hurry it. What you can shape is how well its parts connect. The brain changes with experience: cells that are active together build links.

That is why the authors say every survive moment is also a thrive moment. Two kids are fighting for the third time in three minutes? Splitting them up is a fine way to survive. It is also a chance to teach listening, compromise and forgiveness.

Tap an answer

Anya, nine, lost the class election. That night she sobs and cannot be calmed. The next day she insists nobody may mention the vote and refuses every suggestion. What does this show?

Try this. At the next meltdown, ask whether your child is near the chaos bank or the rigidity bank. Both mean the parts of the brain are not working together.

Both sides of the brain

When a child is upset, connect with the feeling first. Then bring in logic.

The left side of the brain likes order, logic and words. The right side deals in feelings, body language and the big picture. Young children run mostly on the right. An upset child has big right-brain feelings and little left-brain balance.

Bryson’s seven-year-old once came out of bed, angry that nobody ever leaves him a note at night. Then came more: his birthday was ten months away, and he hated homework. Arguing would not reach him. Instead she held him close, rubbed his back and said some days are hard. He said his little brother got more of her attention. He softened. Then she answered his points, briefly. In under five minutes he was back in bed.

This is Connect and Redirect. It is not permissiveness. Rules stay, and hitting or throwing stay off limits. Talk about the behavior once your child has calmed down.

  1. Step 1: connect with the right brain. Use touch, a kind face, a soft voice and listening.
  2. Step 2: redirect with the left brain. Now explain, plan and solve the problem.

Ana’s nine-year-old storms in, furious that the teacher picked someone else for the class job. He says it is not fair. What should Ana do?

The second strategy is Name It to Tame It. After a scary or painful event, help your child tell the story. To tell it in order, the left brain must work with the right brain’s feelings. The book says research finds that naming a feeling calms the emotional circuits.

Katie, four, loved preschool until the day she got sick there. After that she clung to her father and cried, “I’ll die if you leave me!” He retold the day in order, from breakfast to the moment he came to get her. They then made a little book of the story, with her favorite places at school. Before long she loved school again. The retelling gave the scary day an order and a name.

If a child won’t talk, don’t push. Talk while you do something else, or ask for a drawing.

Try this. When your child is upset, touch them, listen and say how they feel. Only then talk about the problem.

Upstairs and downstairs

A child in a full meltdown cannot think, so calm them first and teach later.

Picture the brain as a house. The downstairs holds instincts and strong feelings, like fear and anger. The upstairs, behind the forehead, plans, chooses, controls feelings and cares about others. A staircase lets the two work as a team.

Kids get trapped downstairs for two reasons. The upstairs is not finished until the mid-twenties. And the amygdala, a small almond-shaped part downstairs that watches for danger, can latch a baby gate across the stairs. The authors call this flipping your lid.

So there are two kinds of tantrum. In an upstairs tantrum, the child chooses to throw a fit to get her way. She could stop at once if you gave in. Hold a firm limit and follow through. In a downstairs tantrum, the lid is flipped. He truly cannot listen. Comfort him first, with touch and a soft voice. Talk about behavior later.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Jo’s daughter screams in a shop for princess slippers. Jo says no, and says she will lose her playdate. The girl screams louder, peeks at Jo’s face to see if it is working, and carries on. What now?

Three strategies work with this house.

  1. Engage, Don’t Enrage. Ask a question or ask for a solution, instead of a command.
  2. Use It or Lose It. The upstairs is like a muscle that grows with use. Let kids make choices and live with them, and do not rescue them.
  3. Move It or Lose It. Move your body to calm the downstairs. A boy overwhelmed by homework ran down the street, and came back calm.

Here is Engage, Don’t Enrage at work. Bryson’s four-year-old once left the table in a restaurant and stuck out his tongue, angry. She could have commanded and threatened. Instead she asked if he was angry. Dad had told him to eat half his quesadilla before dessert. She said to decide a fair amount and bargain with Dad. He came back and said ten bites. That was more than half. He ate them and got his dessert. Later they still talked about manners.

Try this. Parents flip their lids too. When yours flips, first make sure you hurt no one. Step away and calm yourself. Then repair with your child quickly.
