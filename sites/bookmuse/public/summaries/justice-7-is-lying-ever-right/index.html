Kant and the hardest case

Kant says a lie is wrong even to a murderer at the door, a defender finds a line between a lie and a misleading truth, and Kant tests a just law by an agreement that never happened.

This is Part 7 of 10 in Justice, our own course on the big ideas of moral and political philosophy. Part 6 gave Immanuel Kant's test of a moral rule. This part puts that test under the heaviest strain, lying, and then follows Kant from personal morality to the law. You can start here, because each idea is explained as it comes.

The murderer at the door

For Kant, you may not lie, even to someone who has come to kill your friend.

Part 6 gave Kant's test of a moral rule. It must hold for everyone, with no “if” and no exception. Now test it on the hardest case. A friend is hiding in your house. A man who wants to kill her knocks and asks, “Is she here?” Many people feel you should lie. Kant says no. In his book the Groundwork, lying is one of his main examples of a wrong act. Whatever follows, Kant says, the truth is owed.

A French thinker, Benjamin Constant, who lived at the same time, disagreed. He said the truth is owed only to people who have earned it, and a killer has not. Kant answered that this misses the point. A lie is not wrong because it harms the listener. It is wrong because it breaks a duty of truthfulness that you owe to everyone, whatever it costs.

A second point sits behind this: for Kant, morality is about the rule you act on, not about what follows, and you cannot control what follows. The friend may already have left by another way. The plain objection is that nobody can owe the truth to a person who is about to do harm. Many people find this view strange, and it is fair to ask whether the case shows a limit of Kant's test. The next lessons look at how far it holds.

Why a lie fails the test

A rule of lying when it suits you cannot be a law for everyone, because it would destroy itself.

Why does Kant hold so firm a line? Part 6's test gives one way to see why: you may act only on a rule you could want everyone to follow. Take Noor, who oversleeps and tells her manager, “My train was cancelled.” Her rule is: I will say what is false when it gets me out of trouble.

Her lie works only because other people usually tell the truth. Her rule needs the others to do the opposite of what it says. So she makes herself the exception. This is not a claim that lying would make the world worse. It is that the rule undoes itself once everyone follows it, so it cannot be a law for all.

This is why Kant refuses even a white lie, a small false word said to be kind. Sparing someone's feelings is a good aim. But if a good aim is enough to make an exception, the rule has an exception for every aim people think important. A moral law like that is no longer a law.

The same goes for the killer at the door. Saving your friend is a very good aim. For Kant, the aim does not let you lie.

A lie and a true dodge

A defender of Kant draws a line: saying something false is wrong, but saying something true that misleads is allowed.

Back at the door. You do not want to help the killer find your friend. You have two ways to answer. You could lie: “No, she is not here.” Or you could say something true that points the wrong way. Say your friend came by bus, and the visitor knows she always drives. You answer, “Her car is not in the driveway.” That is true, but it leaves him thinking she is not there.

One modern defender of Kant says his view allows the second answer but not the first. This is the defender's reading, in the spirit of Kant, and not Kant's own argument. Is the line real? Both answers hope for the same thing: the killer must believe she is not there. It can look like a very fine difference that changes nothing.

Kant has a reason to care about the line. Think of a white lie. A colleague plays you a song they wrote, and you do not like it. “It is wonderful!” is a white lie. “Thanks for playing it for me” is true. Both leave your colleague feeling good. Only the first says something you believe is false. It makes an exception to the rule against false words, for a kind aim, and Kant rejects such exceptions. The second does not.

On this reading, Kant would let you protect your friend, as long as every word you say is true.
