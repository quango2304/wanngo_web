Talking and listening without blame

Judging and demanding make people defend themselves or give in out of fear. Saying what you see, feel, need and ask for, and hearing the same four things in others, lets people give from the heart.

Marshall Rosenberg was a psychologist. As a boy in Detroit in 1943 he lived through a race riot, and two boys beat him up after school because of his name. For most of his life he asked what makes people violent and what lets others stay kind. He decided that words play a big part. His answer is a way of speaking and listening that he calls Nonviolent Communication, or NVC. You will take from it a simple way to say what bothers you, hear people who attack you, and stop attacking yourself.

The four parts

Say what you see, how you feel, what you need and what you ask for, and listen for the same four things in others.

Think of a man crawling under a street lamp, looking for his car keys. He dropped them in the alley, but the light is better under the lamp. In an argument we also look where it feels natural, at who is wrong. The answer is not there. Rosenberg built NVC to train your attention on four other things.

First, say what you observe: what someone said or did, with no judgment in it. Second, say how you feel about it. Third, say the need that feeling comes from. Fourth, make a request: one clear thing you would like them to do.

That is the first half: saying it honestly. The second half is hearing the same four things in the other person, even when they say them badly. They do not need to know NVC. The book says that if you stay with it, people tend to join in, though not always fast. The same four parts also make a good thank-you: what they did, how you feel, which need it met.

It is not a script, and it is not a trick to get your way. It is for people who want the other person to answer freely. The book says what counts is where your attention goes, not the exact words. You can even do all four without saying a word.

Priya learns the four parts and recites them to her sister, word for word, hoping her sister will finally do the dishes. Her sister rolls her eyes. What should Priya change?

Try this. Write the four parts on a small card and look at it in a tense talk. Rosenberg's friend Sam did this at work and at home, and after about a month he put the card away.

Describe, don't judge

When you mix what you saw with what you think of it, people hear criticism and defend themselves, so say what happened and keep your opinion apart.

A school's teachers and principal were stuck in conflict. Rosenberg asked the teachers what the principal did that bothered them. One said he had a big mouth. Another said he talked too much. A third said he thought only he had anything worth saying, and a fourth said he wanted all the attention. None of these said what the principal did. Each was a judgment or a guess about his mind. Then they named what he actually did.

At the joint meeting the teachers rolled their eyes and yawned instead of speaking, until one said, “Ed, you have a big mouth.” Only when they described the stories did the principal listen well. He asked why nobody had told him before. So labels got them nowhere, and a plain description did.

A judgment also says more about you than about them. If a partner wants more closeness than you give, she is “needy.” If you want more than she gives, she is “aloof.” The behavior is the same and the label flips with your own need. A label is a need said as an attack, and the other person hears the attack.

NVC does not ban opinions. It asks you to keep what you saw apart from what you think of it, and to tie it to one time and place. “He has scored no goals in twenty games” says more than “he is a poor player.” Words like “always” and “never” are fine when you are counting, as in the last three times I asked. As exaggeration they make people defend themselves.

A manager wants to raise a problem with Lena's emails. Which opening sticks to what happened?

Try this. Next time you want to say “too much” or “often,” count instead. The book swaps “Janice works too much” for a count: she was at the office more than sixty hours this week.

Name what you feel

Say a real feeling in one plain word, because “I feel that…” and words like “ignored” are opinions about the other person.

A college student was kept awake by his roommate's loud stereo. Asked what he felt, he said that blasting music at night is wrong. That is an opinion. He tried again and gave another opinion, then burst out with “I have no feelings about it whatsoever!” He had strong feelings and no words for them.

The book gives three signs that a sentence is not a feeling. Check the words after “feel”:

  1. That, like or as if. “I feel like a failure” is a thought about yourself.
  2. I, you, he, she, they or it. “I feel it is useless” is an opinion about the thing.
  3. A person’s name. “I feel Amy has been responsible” is a view of Amy.

Words such as ignored, misunderstood, betrayed and unappreciated sound like feelings, but they describe what you think others are doing to you. Even “good” is vague. It can mean happy, excited or relieved.

Naming a feeling takes nerve, and it can pay off. Hospital administrators had lost a vote 17 to 1 on a project, and they dreaded facing the doctors again. They said that admitting fear would let the doctors pick them to pieces. One administrator tried it anyway. He told the doctors how he felt and why he wanted their support. The doctors reversed themselves and voted 17 to 1 for the project.

Try this. When you say “I feel…,” check the next word. If it is “that,” “like” or a person, swap in a word such as sad, scared or relieved.
