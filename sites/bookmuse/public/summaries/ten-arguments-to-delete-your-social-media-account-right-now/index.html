Ten Arguments to Delete Your Social Media Account Right Now

A summary of the book by Jaron Lanier

7 lessons · 19 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

Paid to change you

Social media earns its money by changing what you do for customers you cannot see, so Lanier argues you should delete your accounts and find out who you are without them.

Jaron Lanier is a computer scientist from Silicon Valley who has no social media accounts. His book is a short, sharp argument. It is his opinion. He says himself that he is not a psychologist, and that hard research is thin in places. You will learn how the business works, why he thinks it is bad for you, and what he suggests you try.

What you'll learn

The book's ten arguments

  • You are losing your free will: constant tracking and feedback steer what you do.
  • Quitting social media is the most finely targeted way to resist the insanity of our times: the harm comes from one business plan.
  • Social media is making you into a jerk: the race for attention rewards your worst side.
  • Social media is undermining truth: fake people and private feeds blur what is real.
  • Social media is making what you say meaningless: others set the scene your words are seen in.
  • Social media is destroying your capacity for empathy: you cannot see what other people's feeds show them.
  • Social media is making you unhappy: being ranked all day by machines leaves you feeling hollow.
  • Social media doesn't want you to have economic dignity: free services hide the people whose data runs them.
  • Social media is making politics impossible: the loudest, angriest voices win the attention.
  • Social media hates your soul: it gives its own answers to the biggest questions about life.

Then a short quiz, and the key takeaways on one page.

A cage that goes with you

Lanier argues that social media tests small rewards and shocks on you, all day, to change what you do.

Nearly everyone now carries a smartphone. It is built for changing behavior, because it tracks you and sends you feedback all the time. Algorithms, which are step-by-step programs, compare what you do with what millions of other people did. They do not understand you. With enough people, the numbers are enough.

Old advertising was one pitch that many people saw. Now each person gets a feed that is adjusted all the time. This is called behavior modification: methods that change the habits of animals and people. It can treat an addiction, and it can create one.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

A child gets a candy every time she says please. Then, once in a while, no candy comes. What can happen?

The treats are likes. The shocks are being ignored, belittled or insulted, which most users have felt. Real rewards are not needed. A bell was enough for Pavlov's dog, and pictures of candy are enough in Candy Crush. Lanier says rewards that come at random times can hook people more than steady ones.

Lanier says feelings about other people are the easiest rewards to use. Fear of not seeming cool hurts, and feeds use that fear. Fear and anger also rise fast and last long, so Lanier says they are the cheap choice for a business. He says no evil genius planned this.

Sean Parker, the first president of Facebook, said the makers knew they were using a weakness in human psychology. They did it anyway. So the makers knew what the design did. Lanier also says that after using these services he feels judged and hollow, because programs rank people.

A news app tests two headlines on every reader: a calm one and an angry one. After thousands of tests, which will its program use more, by Lanier's idea?

In your life

You post a photo. Nothing happens for an hour. Then three likes come at once, and then a rude comment. You check your phone again before you finish dinner.

Try this. Use plain words. Where the industry says "engagement," meaning keeping you using the app, say addiction and behavior modification, as Lanier asks. Awareness is the first step to freedom.

A business plan, not a phone

The harm comes from one business plan, not from phones or the internet, so you can aim at it.

The problem is not the smartphone, and it is not the internet. The problem is a business plan. Customers pay to change what other people do, and each person's feed is tuned until their behavior changes. He names it BUMMER, short for Behaviors of Users Modified, and Made into an Empire for Rent.

BUMMER has six parts. Together they make one machine:

  • Attention: ordinary people can win only attention, and the nastiest get the most.
  • Butting in: constant spying through the devices you carry.
  • Cramming: programs choose what each person sees and push it in.
  • Directing: feeds are tuned, sneakily, to change what you do.
  • Earning: customers pay to change other people, whoever they are.
  • Fakes: bots and fake people make fake crowds.

Its effect works on odds, like climate change. No one can prove that one storm came from it, but over many people the odds shift. Lanier admits he is a computer scientist, not a psychologist, and that hard research is thin on some of this.

He compares BUMMER to paint that contains lead. When lead was shown to be harmful, no one said houses should never be painted again. Pressure and new laws made lead-free paint the standard. Smart people waited to buy paint until a safe kind was on sale. In the same way, he says, delete your accounts until safer versions exist.

Rule changes alone do not fix it, he adds. When the money stays the same, people find ways around the rules.

In your life

A friend says the answer is to throw out the phone. You can say that the phone is fine. The part to leave is the paid push on what you do.

Try this. Keep the phone, the websites and the cloud services. Delete your accounts on the platforms that make their money this way.

The Pack switch

Lanier argues the contest for attention online pushes you from thinking for yourself to fighting for rank.

Lanier met this early. In the late 1970s, in online discussions, he would suddenly fight with people about silly things, like piano brands. Years later he blogged for the Huffington Post. The comments gave him a low boiling rage, and likes gave him a silly glow. He caught himself writing things he did not believe, only to get a reaction. He quit again, because he did not like who he was becoming. He blamed the design of the technology.

He explains it with a guess he calls the Solitary/Pack switch. He says it is a working idea, not a proven fact. Like a wolf, you can run in two modes. Alone, you think for yourself and make things. In a pack, rank matters most. You flatter those above you and push down those below. Friends and enemies change quickly, and the only steady bond is a shared enemy.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

A business school professor asks each student to guess how many jelly beans are in a big jar, then averages the guesses. What usually happens?

Now imagine the students see the jar only through a feed. In Lanier's made-up version, camps form and mock each other, and Russian spies add fake photos of jars with other counts. Nobody can guess the number any more. He says democracy and markets both need people who think alone, and both fail in Pack mode.

What flips the switch? His answer: being cut off from real life, with only status games left. He says LinkedIn has the fewest nasty people among big networks, because people there have a real stake, their careers. He admits he has a professional tie to it.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

A stranger insults Dana's cooking in a comment. She types a reply built to hurt him, and enjoys it. What does Lanier suggest?

In your life

In a big group chat, you catch yourself checking who agrees with you before you say what you think.

Try this. When you use a platform, look inside yourself. If you feel an insecurity or a wish to lash out, leave that platform.
