More than we chose

On the view this part ends with, we may owe each other more than we ever agreed to, and a fair society cannot avoid asking what a good life together is.

This last part builds on Part 6 (Kant: we are free when we follow a law we give ourselves) and Part 8 (Rawls: we choose rules without knowing our place). Both treat us as people who choose. Here we ask if that is the whole story, and we return to Aristotle's idea from Part 9: justice depends on what a thing is for.

What you'll learn

Debts we never signed

If we owe only what we choose, nobody can owe anything for a wrong done before they were born.

From the 1910s to the early 1970s, the Australian government forcibly took many Aboriginal children of mixed race from their mothers and sent them to white foster homes or settlement camps. The aim was to make them part of white society and to speed the end of Aboriginal culture.

In 1931, three girls escaped from one camp and set out on a journey of 1,200 miles back to their mothers. A film, Rabbit-Proof Fence, tells their story.

In 1997, a human rights commission recorded the harm done to the “stolen generation” and asked for a yearly day of national apology. Prime minister John Howard refused: Australians today, he said, should not formally apologize for what an earlier generation did. In 2008, a new prime minister, Kevin Rudd, gave the apology. He offered no money to individuals, but promised steps to reduce the disadvantages Indigenous Australians still face.

Look at what sits under the no. To apologize is to take some responsibility, and how can you take responsibility for what happened before you were born? The other side says an apology honors those who suffered and admits that the harm lasts in their families.

The no rests on an idea called moral individualism. It says to be free is to be bound only by what I take on myself: a promise, an agreement, a choice. Beyond basic respect for every person, whatever I owe, I owe because I said yes. It is a claim about what freedom means.

Locke argued that a government is just only if the people it rules consent to it. Kant's autonomy (Part 6) and Rawls's imagined deal (Part 8) both picture a person who steps back from family and past, and then chooses. For such a person, there is no special debt without a promise.

A third kind of duty

Besides duties to everyone and duties we agree to, there may be duties we owe to those we share a history with.

Rawls sorted duties into two kinds. The first is owed to everyone, just for being a person: do not be cruel, do not steal. No promise is needed, because these duties rest on the law we give ourselves (Kant) or the rules we would choose behind the veil (Rawls). These are natural duties. The second is owed to one person because I agreed: if I promise to paint your fence, I must paint it. These are voluntary obligations.

Now Mr. Hale, the oldest person on Alder Street, breaks his hip, and the neighbors set up a meal sign-up. You join in without being asked. Strangers in other towns break hips too, and you would send some money to help them. Still, you feel a stronger pull toward Mr. Hale, though you never promised him anything. Where does the pull come from?

Some thinkers say the pull is a third kind of duty: obligations of solidarity, or membership. Like a promise, it is owed to particular people. Unlike a promise, it needs no yes. It grows from a shared history: a street, a family, a team, a country.

A real case shows it. In 1951, the German chancellor said most Germans had hated the crimes against the Jews and taken no part. Yet he said the crimes were done in the name of the German people, and that called for repair. Germany has since paid out sums worth billions of dollars. On the third view, this is a duty of belonging: people who are not guilty can still owe something. A defender of the choosing self would reply that nobody alive agreed to pay.

A critic says this is only favoritism toward our own group. The reply is that membership can also bind us to outsiders we wronged, and owning that can be part of belonging. Defenders of the choosing self reply that we may quietly accept what neighbors give us, or that favoring our own is fine if no one's rights are broken.

The story you are part of

We are storytelling beings: what I should do depends on which story I find myself part of.

The philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre answers the choosing self with another picture. In his 1981 book After Virtue, he argues that people do not first pick goals and then act. We live our lives as stories. Before deciding what to do, we must first work out which stories we belong to.

Ben is offered a job in the city. He would love it. But his grandmother started the clinic in his small town, his mother runs it, and it needs a manager. A pure chooser would list pros and cons. The story view starts elsewhere. Where does Ben fit in his family's story?

On this view, deciding is more like reading than willing. You interpret your life so far, then choose the path that makes the most sense of it.

There is a real risk. Ties we did not choose can be a cage: a job or a place fixed at birth. MacIntyre's reply is that a story is not a script. You do not know what happens next, and you still must interpret and decide. You can be placed and free at once.

Those who hold the story view add that it explains pride and shame. A visitor can admire a village's old stone bridge. Only the villagers feel proud of it, and only they feel ashamed if they learn its builder cheated the workers. On this view, real pride in a shared past comes with some duty for its burdens. Critics answer that a feeling of belonging is not a promise.
