Born a Crime

A summary of the book by Trevor Noah

8 lessons · 20 min · quiz and takeaways

Read it lesson by lessonGet the app

The child the law forbade

Apartheid made Trevor Noah’s birth a crime and tried to keep people apart, and a mother who loved hard, taught him to think and found the cracks in unjust rules gave him ways to cross every line.

Trevor Noah grew up in South Africa and is now a comedian. He was born in 1984 to a black mother and a white father, which was a crime under apartheid, the system of laws that kept South Africans apart by race. His book tells true stories from his childhood in and around Johannesburg, as apartheid ended. Mostly it is the story of his mother.

When he was nine, she saved his life by throwing him out of a moving minibus and jumping out behind him with his baby brother, after the driver threatened them. The book is funny, and it also covers violence, poverty and abuse. You take away how one stubborn, loving woman raised a child to see past the lines the law drew.

Finding the loopholes

When a rule makes no sense, look for the gap in it. His mother lived that way, and she taught him to do the same.

Trevor Noah was born in 1984, under apartheid, the system of laws that kept South Africans apart by race. His mother is black and his father is white. Sex between the races was a crime, with five years in prison as the penalty. So, as he puts it, he was born a crime.

His mother did not wait for the law to change. She looked for the cracks in it. Office jobs were for whites by law, so she took a typing class anyway. Then the government began to hire a few black people as typists, to calm protests from other countries, and she got a job as a secretary.

The hardest crack was Trevor himself. Being mixed, with one black and one white parent, was illegal. Being colored, the separate official group for people of long mixed ancestry, was not. So his mother moved him around as a colored child. A colored neighbor named Queen walked beside him in the park as if she were his mother, while his real mother walked a few steps behind, like the maid.

He inherited the habit. At his Catholic school, only Catholics could take communion, which was grape juice and crackers. He asked a priest whether Jesus, who was Jewish, would be allowed to share it. No one had a good answer. One morning he drank all the grape juice and ate all the crackers behind the altar. Asked what kind of sick person does that, he said, “A hungry person.” His mother agreed the rule was silly, so she did not punish him for breaking it.

Keeping people apart

Apartheid ruled by keeping groups apart and teaching each group to blame another for what it could not have.

Noah calls apartheid, South Africa’s system of racial laws, “apart hate.” Split people into groups and make them hate each other, and you can run them all. Black South Africans outnumbered whites nearly five to one, yet they were divided into peoples with different languages, like the Zulu and the Xhosa, and given different rights to keep them at odds. The hatred was real. One night a Zulu minibus driver heard Noah’s Xhosa mother speak and threatened her.

People of long mixed ancestry were called colored, and they had a special place. They had fewer rights than whites, but some privileges black people lacked, to keep them hoping for more. White Afrikaners, descended from Dutch settlers, called them the almost-boss. Each year a few colored people were promoted to white. In one test, a pencil went into the hair. A pencil that stayed in meant colored, and one that fell out meant white.

So colored people were taught that black people were the reason they were kept out. Noah compares it to a bouncer at a club door. He tells one friend the other friend is the problem, then says the same to the other. Both blame each other, and neither was ever getting in.

None of it followed logic. Chinese people were classed as black, because there were too few of them to get a class of their own. Japanese people were treated as honorary whites, because the government wanted to import their cars and electronics. Groups followed what the rulers wanted.

Language beats color

People trust those who speak their language, so a language can cross lines that skin color draws.

As a child, Noah watched American shows dubbed into African languages. Black actors speaking those languages felt familiar. Then he heard the original English on the radio, where the same actors had black American accents, and they felt like foreigners. A shared language says we are the same, and a language barrier says we are different. Apartheid used this by teaching black children only in their home language, so groups kept thinking they were different.

His mother used languages like keys. She spoke English, Xhosa, Zulu, German and Afrikaans, the language of many white South Africans, which Noah says is worth knowing because it is the language of your oppressor. In a shop, the owner told his guard in Afrikaans to follow the black customers in case they stole. She answered in fluent Afrikaans and asked why he did not help them find what they wanted. He apologized, not for the racism, but for aiming it at them.

Trevor learned to do the same. As a young man, he walked down a street while Zulu men behind him said, in Zulu, that they would rob the white guy. He spun around and offered, in Zulu, to rob someone with them. They laughed, said sorry and explained they had meant to rob white people.

Noah decided that language, even more than color, shapes who you are to people. He called himself a chameleon: his color stayed the same, but he could change how others saw it. Nelson Mandela said that a language a man merely understands reaches his head, while his own language reaches his heart.
