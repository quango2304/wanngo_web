The first two seconds

Decisions made in the first two seconds can be as good as slow, careful ones, but they can be fooled, so learn when to trust a first impression, when to guard it and how to train it.

Malcolm Gladwell, a staff writer at The New Yorker, wrote Blink in 2005. It starts with a statue that a museum bought after fourteen months of tests, while art experts called it a fake at a glance. His stories from medicine, war, business and policing show when a snap judgment is right, when it fails and how to train it.

A mind that knows before you do

Your mind can solve a problem in a flash, before you know it has, so your first two seconds are worth listening to.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

In a card game, players turn over cards from four decks. Two red decks pay a lot but cost a lot. Two blue decks pay $50 and cost little. Scientists measured sweat in each player’s palms. When did the palms first react to the red decks?

Most players had a hunch after about fifty cards, and could say why after about eighty. But their palms began to sweat at card ten, and they soon picked fewer red cards. They worked out the game before they knew they had.

Gladwell calls the part of the brain that does this the adaptive unconscious: a giant computer that quietly sorts a lot of information so you can act fast.

In 1983 an art dealer offered the Getty Museum in California a marble statue of a standing young man, called a kouros, for just under $10 million. The museum spent fourteen months on tests and papers, and then agreed to buy it.

Then experts looked. Thomas Hoving, a former head of New York’s Metropolitan Museum, thought of one word the moment he saw it: “fresh.” Another expert stared at the fingernails and felt something was wrong. At that moment none could say why. The papers tracing the statue to a Swiss doctor later turned out to be fakes, so a glance had beaten fourteen months of study.

Gut feelings can be fooled too. The Getty wanted the statue to be real, and that wish blocked its own experts. The rest of the book is about when to trust a first reaction and when to guard it.

Dana, a nurse, feels something is off about a patient the moment he walks in. She cannot say why, and his first tests look fine. What fits this lesson?

Try this. When you meet something new, note the first word that comes to your mind, as Hoving does.

A thin slice tells a lot

A thin slice, a few minutes of the right clues, can tell you more than long study.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

The psychologist John Gottman films a married couple for fifteen minutes as they talk about something they disagree on. How often can he tell whether they will still be married fifteen years later?

Since the 1980s, Gottman has filmed more than three thousand married couples. His team scored every second of each film for emotions such as anger, defensiveness and sadness. Couples often do not know how they sound, so he watches the film and does not ask them. In one film a young couple argued about their dog and both smiled. Yet the husband was defensive for 40 of the first 66 seconds, and the wife rolled her eyes.

How? He does not count everything. Gladwell tried ten three-minute clips, could not follow everything and got five right, the same as a coin flip. Gottman watches four signs: defensiveness, stonewalling (turning away), criticism and contempt. Contempt matters most: speaking as if you are better than the other person, like an insult or an eye roll.

The same trick works with doctors. Patients rarely sue over mistakes alone. They sue when they feel rushed or ignored. Judges who heard only the blurred tone of a surgeon’s voice, with the words gone, could tell who had been sued: a dominant voice marked them.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

Raj watches two friends argue and tries to count every sigh, joke, frown and shrug. He loses track and cannot tell how they are doing. What fits this lesson?

Try this. The next time you see a doctor, notice whether he listens and treats you with respect. If you sense he does not, listen to that feeling.

The locked door

Quick judgments are made behind a locked door, so you can know something without knowing why, and the reasons people give may be made up.

GUESS FIRST

Tap an answer

Students did a five-minute word test. Some read words about being rude, others words about being polite. Then each student walked down a hall to ask the experimenter for the next task. The experimenter was busy talking with another person in the doorway. What did the polite group do?

The psychologist John Bargh ran this test. In another, words about old age hidden in a language test made people walk down the hall more slowly. They did not know why. Think of the quick mind as working behind a locked door: you see what comes out, not what happens inside.

Hidden nudges can change how well you think. Two Dutch researchers gave students forty-two hard trivia questions. Before the quiz, half spent five minutes thinking about being a professor. The others thought about soccer hooligans.

Experts cannot see through the door either. The tennis coach Vic Braden called double faults, two missed serves in a row, before the racket hit the ball. He was right sixteen times out of seventeen, but he never found out how he knew.

Hidden hints work the same way. A psychologist asked people to tie together two ropes hanging too far apart to hold both. Few found the trick: swing one rope like a pendulum. Then he casually brushed a rope, and most people got it. Only one gave the true reason. The rest said things like “It just dawned on me.” They were not lying. They made up the most likely reason.

QUICK CHECK

Tap an answer

A chef says his secret is adding the salt at the end. A video shows he adds it in the first minute. What fits this lesson?

Try this. When someone asks why you made a quick choice and you do not know, say “I don’t know.” The psychologist Joshua Aronson says we should say it more often.
