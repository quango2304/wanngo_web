How rulers win and keep power

A ruler keeps power by judging people as they really are, relying on his own strength, never making his people hate him, and preparing in calm times for the day luck turns.

Niccolò Machiavelli wrote this short book as a gift for Lorenzo de’ Medici, a powerful man. He says it holds what he learned from long experience of the affairs of his day and from steady study of the past. The advice is harsh, and the book admits that cruelty and betrayal may bring power but never glory. You get its main ideas, with its stories.

See people as they are

Plan for how people really act, not for how they should act.

Machiavelli gives advice to a ruler, and he starts with a warning. Many writers have described perfect states that never existed. He prefers “the real truth of things.” The way people live and the way they should live are far apart. A ruler who is perfectly good among people who are not good will be ruined.

So a ruler must learn when not to be good. Some things that look like virtues lead to ruin. Some things that look like faults keep you safe. His example is generosity.

Why? The ruler who wants a name for generosity must keep giving big gifts. He soon spends everything. Then he taxes his people, and they hate him. The wise ruler lives with the name of miser. Because he saves, he needs no new taxes and can defend his land. Most people see that he takes nothing from them.

Fear is safer than love

If you cannot have both, fear holds people better than love, because love depends on them and fear depends on you.

Machiavelli asks an old question: is it better for a ruler to be loved or feared? He would like both. But love and fear seldom go together. So if he must choose, “it is far safer to be feared than loved.”

His reason is blunt. People, he says, are thankless, change their minds easily, and run from danger. They back you while you do them good, and they turn on you when you need them. Love is a tie of thanks, and people break it when their own interest calls. Fear is a tie of punishment, and it does not let go.

Scipio was one of the greatest generals of Rome. He was easy on his own soldiers. What happened in Spain?

Compare Hannibal, a famous general of old. He led a huge army of men from many nations, far from home. In good times and bad, no one rose against him. The book credits his cruelty, which made him feared. Scipio shows the other side. A ruler should rely on his own strength, not on the feelings of others.

One limit applies. Fear must not turn into hate. A ruler can be feared and still not hated, if he leaves his people’s property alone.

Never be hated or despised

A ruler’s best protection is not a wall but the good will of his people: do not make them hate you, and do not let them see you as weak.

A ruler is safe from most plots, Machiavelli says, if he avoids two things. The first is being hated. A ruler is hated most quickly when he takes people’s property or harms their honor. Leave those alone, and most people live content. Then he has only a few ambitious men to deal with.

The second is being despised. People despise a ruler who seems changeable, silly, timid or unable to decide. So his decisions should hold. No one should think he can fool him or talk him out of a choice.

A plot is hard to carry out. A plotter needs helpers, and the only ones he can find are unhappy men. But an unhappy man who hears the plan can win a reward by telling the ruler instead. And a plotter who kills a loved ruler still has the people against him afterward.

In Bologna, a rival family murdered its lord, Annibale Bentivoglio, and left only an infant son. The people rose at once and killed every one of the murderers. Then they fetched a young man of the family from Florence to rule until the boy grew up. The people stood by a family they loved.

So walls matter less than people. Fortresses may help for a time, but “the best fortress you can have, is in not being hated by your subjects.”
